Treatise on the Great Way, Upper Essay

Upper Essay of the Treatise on the Great Way — human beings arise from the Boundless and Supreme Ultimate; following inner nature to restore life is the Way, shared by all three teachings, with the Golden Elixir as its active fulfillment

About This Chapter

The Treatise on the Great Way (Dadao lun) opens Scroll 3 of the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng. Divided into Upper, Middle, and Lower essays, it serves as the grand outline of Zhang Sanfeng's internal alchemy. The Upper Essay is the longest and unfolds in four main movements:

  1. Where human life comes from: Before one's parents conceive one lies the "Boundless" (wuji); when a single spark of luminous breath enters the womb, that is the "Supreme Ultimate" (taiji). After birth, the Original Spirit is called True Nature, and Original Breath is called True Life—the undivided wholeness of a newborn child is our "Heavenly Nature and Heavenly Life." Simply following this Heavenly Nature to restore Heavenly Life is the Way.
  2. Why people age, fall ill, and die: As intellectual clarity opens and sensory awareness deepens, the seven emotions and six desires churn day and night: "when the mind moves too long, Spirit gradually tires; when essence is heavily spent, Breath grows ever more exhausted." Waiting until sickness strikes to call a doctor is already too late.
  3. Why the Way of the immortals is no heresy: Zhang Liang, Zhuge Liang, and Li Bi all achieved stillness first and only then acted effectively in the world; Ge Hong, Zhao Yu, and Xu Xun attained the Way and still served in office to bring peace to the people. Daoist adepts are neither the "obscure eccentrics" despised by Confucius nor the egoists and Mohists rejected by Mencius. What Han Yu and Zhu Xi attacked were counterfeit Buddhists and Daoists, not genuine practitioners. From this flows the essay's most famous verdict: Confucianism practices the Way to aid the times; Buddhism awakens to the Way to enlighten the world; Daoist immortals treasure the Way to liberate beings—the three teachings share one and the same Way.
  4. The Golden Elixir is the straight path; side-paths lead nowhere: Cultivation takes hidden virtue as its substance and the Golden Elixir as its function. Sexual exploitation of women and ingesting mineral poisons are useless side-paths, while massage, calisthenics, and breath-holding can only relieve illness without completing the Way. Finally, Zhang warns that once the Elixir is formed, one should live in quiet seclusion rather than parading alchemical gold-making at court.

At the end stands an original commentary praising this essay as "a thunderous bell that awakens the deaf, a grand composition weaving heaven and earth."

Reading Note: Phrases here and in the Middle Essay such as seeking the Golden Elixir "only within one's own kind" or "seeking the other through oneself" have been interpreted differently across history. In this very essay, Zhang Sanfeng explicitly classes sexual practices ("mounting women to harvest yin") as a worthless side-path. Consistent with the rest of this site, our translation and notes read these expressions according to the pure-stillness method—yin and yang existing within one's own body. See the Translator's Notes for details.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

夫道者，统生天生地、生人生物而名，含阴阳动静之机，具造化玄微之理，统无极，生太极，无极为无名，无名者天地之始，太极为有名，有名者万物之母，因无名而有名，则天生、地生、人生、物生矣。今专以人生言之：父母未生以前，一片太虚托诸於穆，此无极时也。无极为阴静，阴静阳亦静也。父母施生之始，一片灵气投入胎中，此太极时也。太极为阳动，阳动阴亦动也。自是而阴阳相推，刚柔相摩，八卦相荡，则乾道成男，坤道成女矣。故男女交媾之初，男精女血混成一物，此即是人身之本也。

What we call the "Way" is the name given to the single source that gives birth to heaven and earth, to humanity and the myriad things. It holds the pivot of yin and yang, stillness and movement, and embodies the subtle principles of cosmic creation. It encompasses the Boundless (wuji) and brings forth the Supreme Ultimate (taiji). The Boundless is the "Nameless," and the Nameless is the beginning of heaven and earth; the Supreme Ultimate is the "Named," and the Named is the mother of the myriad things (as Chapter 1 of the Daodejing says). Because the Named arises from the Nameless, heaven is born, earth is born, human beings are born, and all things are born.

Let us speak here specifically of human birth. Before our parents conceived us, there was only an expanse of Great Emptiness resting in the deep, silent mystery of Heaven—this is the stage of the Boundless. The Boundless belongs to yin stillness, and when yin is still, yang too is still. At the moment our parents bestow life, a spark of luminous breath enters the womb—this is the stage of the Supreme Ultimate. The Supreme Ultimate belongs to yang movement, and when yang moves, yin moves with it. From then on, yin and yang propel one another, the firm and the yielding rub against each other, and the eight trigrams stir one another (as the Appended Remarks of the Book of Changes put it), so that the Way of Qian forms the male and the Way of Kun forms the female. Thus, at the very beginning of union, the father's essence and the mother's blood blend into a single substance—this is the root of the human body.

§2
Original (Chinese)

嗣后而父精藏于肾，母血藏于心，心肾脉连随母呼吸，十月形全，脱离母腹，斯时也，性浑于无识，又以无极伏其神，命资于有生，复以太极育其气，气脉舒而内蕴元神则曰真性，神思静而中长元气则曰真命，浑浑沦沦孩子之体，正所谓天性天命也。人能率此天性以复其天命，此即可谓之道，又何修道之不可成道哉！

Thereafter, the father's essence is stored in the kidneys and the mother's blood in the heart. Connected by the vessels between heart and kidneys, the fetus breathes along with its mother until, after ten lunar months, its form is complete and it leaves the womb. At that moment, inner nature is undivided and free of discursive knowledge, with the Boundless keeping its spirit peacefully hidden; life is sustained by embodied existence, with the Supreme Ultimate nourishing its breath. When the breath-channels are relaxed and open while harboring the Original Spirit within, this is called "True Nature." When spirit and thought are tranquil while nurturing Original Breath within, this is called "True Life." That undivided, unspoiled body of a newborn infant is precisely what is called "Heavenly Nature and Heavenly Life." If a person can follow this Heavenly Nature to restore this Heavenly Life, that itself may be called the Way—how could anyone who cultivates the Way fail to attain it?

§3
Original (Chinese)

奈何灵明日著，知觉日深，血气滋养岁渐长成，则七情六欲万绪千端，昼夜无休息矣。心久动而神渐疲，精多耗而气益惫，生老迫而病死之患成，并且无所滋补，则瘵病频生，而欲长有其身，难矣。观此生死之道，人以为常，诚为可惜，然其疾病临身，亦有求医调治，望起沉疴图延岁月者，此时即有求生之心，又何益乎！

Alas, as intellectual clarity grows more pronounced and sensory perception deepens, and as blood and breath nourish the growing frame year by year, the seven emotions and six desires tangle into ten thousand threads, churning day and night without rest. When the mind moves unceasingly, Spirit gradually grows weary; when essence is repeatedly drained, Breath becomes ever more depleted. Birth and aging press close, and the afflictions of sickness and death take shape. With nothing to replenish the loss, wasting illnesses strike again and again, and keeping one's body intact for long becomes difficult indeed.

Looking at this cycle of birth and death, most people treat it as ordinary—a truly regrettable thing. Only when illness falls upon them do they seek physicians and remedies, hoping to recover from grave disease and stretch out their years. Yet waiting until that hour to yearn for life—what good can it do?

§4
Original (Chinese)

予观恶死之常情，即觅长生之妙术，辛苦数年得闻仙道，仙道者，长生之道也。而世人多以异端目之。夫黄老所传，亦正心修身治国平天下之理也。而何诧为异端哉？况人能修正身心，则真精真神聚其中，大才大德出其中，圣经云「安而后能虑」富哉言乎！吾尝论之矣。有如子房公之安居下邳——而后能用汉报韩，诸葛君之安卧南阳——而后能辅蜀伐魏，李邺侯之安养衡山——而后能兴唐灭虏，他若葛稚川之令勾漏，赵清源之刺嘉州，许真君之治旌阳，是皆道成住世，出仕安民者，彼其心不皆有君父仁义之心哉？

Observing the universal human dread of death, I set out in search of the wondrous art of long life. After several arduous years, I came to hear of the Way of the immortals—which is simply the Way of longevity. Yet the world mostly dismisses it as heresy. In truth, what the Yellow Emperor and Laozi transmitted is the very same principle of rectifying the mind, cultivating the person, governing the state, and bringing peace to all under heaven. Why express astonishment and brand it heresy? Moreover, when a person rectifies and cultivates body and mind, true essence and true spirit gather within, and great talent and great virtue issue forth. As the classic Great Learning says, "Only after being settled can one deliberate well"—how rich are those words!

I have discussed this before: take Zhang Liang (Zifang), who first dwelt quietly at Xiapi and only then could guide the Han dynasty and avenge Han; or Zhuge Liang, who first rested peacefully in Nanyang and only then could assist Shu and march against Wei; or Li Bi (Marquis of Ye), who first nourished himself in seclusion on Mount Heng and only then could restore the Tang and quell the rebels. Others too—Ge Hong (Zhichuan) seeking the magistracy of Goulou, Zhao Yu (Qingyuan) governing Jiazhou, and Perfected Xu Xun administering Jingyang—all attained the Way, remained in the world, and took office to bring peace to the people. Did not their hearts beat with loyalty, filial devotion, humaneness, and righteousness?

§5
Original (Chinese)

孔子鄙隐怪，孟子拒杨墨，隐也者，乃谶纬说、封禅书之类，怪也者，乃微生高、陈仲子之类，仙家不然也。长春朝对——皆仁民爱物之言，希夷归山——怀耿介清高之致，何隐怪之有哉？杨子为我，墨子薄亲，仙家不尔也。三千功行——济人利世为先资，二十四孝——吴猛丁兰皆仙客，又何杨墨之可同哉！

Confucius disdained "the obscure and the eccentric," and Mencius rejected Yang Zhu and Mozi. By "obscure" is meant prophetic apocrypha and treatises on imperial Feng and Shan sacrifices; by "eccentric," affected rigorists like Weisheng Gao and Chen Zhongzi. The school of immortals is nothing like that. When Qiu Changchun (Qiu Chuji) answered the Mongol emperor at court, every word urged compassion for the people and care for all creatures; when Chen Xiyi (Chen Tuan) returned to the mountains, he carried a spirit of upright integrity and lofty purity. Where is there anything obscure or eccentric in this? Yang Zhu taught pure self-interest, and Mozi thinned out family affection; the immortals do neither. They require "three thousand meritorious deeds," taking aid to humanity and service to the world as their first qualification, and among the Twenty-Four Exemplars of Filial Piety, both Wu Meng and Ding Lan are figures of the immortal tradition. How could they ever be equated with Yang Zhu or Mozi?

§6
Original (Chinese)

孔曰求志，孟曰尚志，问为何志？曰仁义而已矣。仁属木，木中藏火，大抵是化育光明之用乃曰仁，义属金，金中生水，大抵是裁制流通之用乃曰义，仙家汞铅即仁义之种子也。金木交并，水火交养，故尝隐居求志，高尚其志，而后汞铅生，丹道凝，志包仁义汞铅，而兼金木水火之四象，求之尚之者，诚意为之，意土合而五行全，大道之事备矣。

Confucius spoke of "seeking one's aspiration," and Mencius spoke of "exalting one's aspiration." Ask what that aspiration is: simply humaneness (ren) and righteousness (yi). Humaneness belongs to Wood, and within Wood hides Fire; broadly speaking, it is the nourishing, illuminating function that we call humaneness. Righteousness belongs to Metal, and from Metal arises Water; broadly speaking, it is the discerning, flowing function that we call righteousness. What the immortals call Mercury and Lead (see Lead and Mercury) are precisely the seeds of humaneness and righteousness. Metal and Wood unite; Water and Fire nourish each other. Therefore one dwells in quiet seclusion to seek this aspiration and exalt it, and only then do Mercury and Lead arise and the Way of the Elixir congeal. This aspiration embraces humaneness and righteousness, Mercury and Lead, and the four emblems of Metal, Wood, Water, and Fire; while seeking and exalting it require sincerity of intention. Intention belongs to Earth (see True Intention). Once the Earth of Intention joins in, the Five Phases are complete, and the work of the Great Way is whole.

§7
Original (Chinese)

故孔孟当日只辟隐怪杨墨，而未闻摈斥佛老，唐宋以来，乃有韩朱二贤力辟二氏，诸大儒和之，群小儒拾其唾余以求附尾，究其究竟辟著何处，反令世尊含笑，太上长叹，小儒辈不过徒吹滥竽，未必有韩朱之识见也。何以言之？韩朱之辟二氏者，辟其非佛非老之流，非辟真学佛老者也。不然昌黎诗集往来赠答，又何以极多二氏之人，如送张道士有诗、送大颠有诗、送惠师灵师皆有诗，或以为借人发议，故于惠师云「吾疾游惰者，怜子愚且淳。」于灵师亦云「方将敛之道，且欲冠其颠。」味之似讥之矣。然何以于张道士尽无贬词，于大颠师全无诮语。盖此二师者，乃真仙真佛之徒，张仙以尚书不用而归真，颠佛以聪明般若而通禅，虽昌黎亦不能下手排之，肆口毁之也。且其家又生韩湘仙伯，雪拥蓝关。盖已知远来者之非凡人也。朱子少年亦尝出入二氏。盖因不得其门而入，为二氏之匪徒所迷，故疑其虚无荒诞、空寂渺茫，回头抵牾耳。迨其晚年学博，则又爱读参同契，并云「参同之书，本不为明易。盖借此以寓其进退行持之候耳。」更与人书云「近者道间不挟他书，始得熟玩参同。」是更津津然以仙道为有味也。然则韩朱二贤，特辟其非佛非老之流，非辟真学佛老者也。否则前后一身自相矛盾，则二贤亦可笑也。

Therefore, in their own day, Confucius and Mencius refuted only the obscure, the eccentric, Yang Zhu, and Mozi—never once were they heard rejecting Buddhism or Laozi. Only from the Tang and Song onward did two worthies, Han Yu and Zhu Xi, vigorously criticize the two teachings. Many great Confucians echoed them, and crowds of lesser scholars picked up their cast-off phrases to tag along behind. Yet when you examine what they actually refuted, it only makes the World-Honored One smile and the Supreme Lord Lao sigh; those lesser scholars are merely blowing pipes to fake a place in the orchestra, possessing none of Han Yu's or Zhu Xi's real discernment.

Why say this? Because what Han Yu and Zhu Xi refuted were the pretenders who were neither genuine Buddhists nor genuine Daoists, not true students of the Buddha and Laozi. Otherwise, why does Han Yu's collected poetry contain so many exchanges with Buddhists and Daoists—poems seeing off Daoist Zhang, Master Dadian, Master Hui, and Master Ling? Some say he used these poems to voice critique, noting that to Master Hui he wrote, "I detest the idle drifters, yet pity you as simple and sincere," and to Master Ling, "I am about to draw you back to the Way and place a Confucian cap upon your head"—lines that sound teasing on the surface. Yet why is there not a single disparaging word for Daoist Zhang, nor a single mocking line for Master Dadian? Because those two were genuine followers of the immortals and the Buddha: Daoist Zhang turned to self-cultivation after his memorials went unheeded, and Master Dadian penetrated Chan through keen wisdom. Even Han Yu could find no foothold to reject or recklessly slander them. What is more, his own clan produced the immortal Han Xiang, whose arrival in the poem "snow blocks the Blue Pass" showed that Han Yu already recognized his kinsman from afar as no ordinary mortal.

As for Master Zhu Xi, in his youth he too explored Buddhism and Daoism, but failing to find the true doorway and misled by unscrupulous charlatans, he came to suspect them of empty absurdity and hazy nihilism, and turned against them. Yet in his later years, when his learning had grown vast, he loved reading The Seal of the Unity of the Three (Cantong qi), remarking: "The Cantong qi was never meant to expound the Book of Changes; it borrows the Changes to lodge its timing of advance, retreat, and cultivation." In a letter to a friend he further wrote: "Lately on the road I carried no other book, and at last had time to savor the Cantong qi thoroughly." Here he clearly relished the Way of the immortals. Thus Han Yu and Zhu Xi specifically attacked counterfeit Buddhists and Daoists, not sincere practitioners—otherwise both worthies would contradict themselves in one and the same lifetime and appear quite laughable.

§8
Original (Chinese)

予也不才，窃尝学览百家，理综三教，并知三教之同此一道也。儒离此道不成儒，佛离此道不成佛，仙离此道不成仙，而仙家特称为道门，是更以道自任也。复何言哉！平允论之曰：儒也者——行道济时者也。佛也者——悟道觉世者也。仙也者——藏道度人者也。各讲各的妙处，合讲合的好处，何必口舌是非哉！

Though lacking in talent, I have ventured to survey the hundred schools and synthesize the principles of the three teachings, and I know that all three share this single Way. Without this Way, Confucianism cannot be Confucianism, Buddhism cannot be Buddhism, and the immortal tradition cannot be the immortal tradition. That the immortals specifically call themselves the "Gate of the Way" shows all the more that they take the Way as their own responsibility—what more need be said?

To put it fairly: Confucianism practices the Way to aid the times; Buddhism awakens to the Way to enlighten the world; the immortals treasure the Way in hiddenness to liberate beings. Each speaks of its own wonders, and together they speak of their shared good—why waste breath wrangling over right and wrong?

§9
Original (Chinese)

夫道者，无非「穷理尽性以至于命」而已矣。孔子隐诸罕言，仙家畅言之、喻言之，字样多而道义微，故人不知耳。人由天地而育，亦由父母而生，顺而用之——则鼻祖耳孙嗣续而成，逆而用之——则真仙上圣亦接踵而出，同其理也。悟真篇云「修身之事不拘男女，此金丹大药，虽愚昧野人得之，立登仙位。」故不拘贵贱贤愚、老衰少壮，只要素行阴德，仁慈悲悯，忠孝信诚，全于人道，仙道自然不远也。又须洞晓阴阳，深参造化，察其真伪，得阴阳之正炁，觅铅汞之真宗，方能换骨长生，居不夜之天，玩长春之景，与天地同久，日月同明，此正大丈夫分内事也。

The Way is nothing other than "fathoming principle, fulfilling inner nature, and thereby arriving at destiny" (as the Discussion of the Trigrams in the Book of Changes states). Confucius veiled it and spoke of it rarely; the immortals spoke of it freely and through metaphor, so that as terms multiplied the core meaning grew subtle, and people failed to recognize it.

Human beings are nurtured by heaven and earth and born of father and mother. Used in the forward direction, this produces an unbroken line from remote ancestors to distant descendants; used in reversal, it brings forth perfected immortals and lofty sages one after another—the principle is one and the same. The Folio on Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian) says: "Self-cultivation makes no distinction between man and woman; if even an unlettered rustic obtains this Great Medicine of the Golden Elixir, he ascends at once to the ranks of the immortals." Therefore, regardless of noble or humble birth, wisdom or simplicity, old age or youth, as long as one steadily practices hidden virtue—compassionate and kind, loyal, filial, trustworthy, and sincere—and fulfills the human Way, the Way of the immortals is naturally never far away.

One must also thoroughly understand yin and yang, deeply investigate cosmic creation, discern the genuine from the false, obtain the upright breath of yin and yang, and seek the true lineage of Lead and Mercury. Only then can one transform one's bones and achieve longevity, dwell in a heaven without night, delight in scenes of perpetual spring, endure as long as heaven and earth, and shine as brightly as the sun and moon. Truly, this is the rightful calling of a person of great resolve.

§10
Original (Chinese)

至于旁门邪径，御女采阴、服炼三黄、烧饵八石，是旁门无功也。又有以按摩导引、吐纳呵嘘、修服药草，为养生之方者，虽能暂去其疾，难逃老衰命尽，而被达人耻笑也。伯端翁云「闭息一法，若能忘机绝念，亦可入定出神，奈何精气神属阴，宅舍难固，不免有迁徙之苦。」更有进气补血，名为抽添接命之术者，亦能避疾延年，保身健体，若欲服食养就胎仙，必不能也。其他旁门邪径，实乃为吾道之异端也。何足道哉！

As for heterodox side-paths—exploiting women to harvest yin, refining and swallowing the Three Yellows (realgar, orpiment, and sulfur), or firing and ingesting the Eight Minerals—these are barren byways without real merit. Others rely on massage and calisthenics, exhaling and inhaling with vocalized breaths, or preparing herbal elixirs as regimens for nourishing life. Though these can temporarily clear away illness, they cannot escape aging and the end of life, and draw the derision of those who truly understand.

Old Master Zhang Boduan (Ziyang) said: "With breath-retention, if one can forget scheming and cut off thoughts, one may indeed enter absorption and send out the spirit; alas, because essence, breath, and spirit remain yin, the bodily dwelling cannot be made firm, and one still cannot escape the suffering of moving from house to house (rebirth)." Still others practice drawing in breath to replenish blood, calling it the art of "extracting, adding, and extending life"; this too can ward off illness, lengthen one's years, and keep the body strong, yet if one hopes thereby to ingest the Elixir and nurture the Immortal Embryo, it will surely fail. All other side-paths and crooked trails are truly heresies within our Way—scarcely worth mentioning!

§11
Original (Chinese)

或者谓人之生死皆有定数，岂有违天数而逃死者？独不思福自我求，命自我造，阴骘可以延年，学长生者，只要以阴功为体，金丹为用，则天数亦可逃也。

Some object: "Human life and death are fixed by allotted numbers—how could anyone defy Heaven's allotment and escape death?" Have they never reflected that blessings are sought by oneself, destiny is forged by oneself, and quiet hidden virtue can extend one's years? For those who study long life, as long as hidden merit is made the substance and the Golden Elixir the function, even Heaven's allotted count can be transcended.

§12
Original (Chinese)

伏维我　太上道祖，列圣高真，施好生之心，广度人之愿，宏开玄教，秘授仙方名曰金丹，因此尽露天机，大泄元奥，其中原始要终，行持妙用，三候三关，九琴九剑，药材法器，火候符章，悉已敷衍全备，各宜详究诸经，以还其性命之本，予论虽俗，义理最美，所谓真实不虚也。倘得者无所猜疑，庶可以行持下手，虽不遇明师好友，得遇此书，即如师友在前，自能顿悟无上也。较诸行世丹经，悉合一理，罔不洞彻，实属苦海之慈航，指迷之智烛，虽曰行之惟艰，然勿畏难而苟安也。

Humbly I reflect upon our Supreme Patriarch of the Way and the lineage of sages and lofty Perfected Ones: acting from a heart that cherishes life and a vast vow to liberate humankind, they threw open the Mysterious Teaching and secretly transmitted the immortal formula called the Golden Elixir, laying bare the heavenly mechanism and revealing primordial mysteries. Everything from first beginnings to final completion—the subtle workings of practice, the three phases and three passes, the nine lutes and nine swords, the medicinal ingredients and ritual vessels, the fire phasing and talismanic emblems—has been set out in full. Each student should examine the classics closely in order to return to the root of nature and life.

Though my essay uses plain language, its meaning and principles are of the finest order—truly real and free of falsehood. If those who obtain it harbor no suspicion, they may safely begin their practice; even without meeting an enlightened teacher or noble companion, encountering this book is like having teacher and friend right before one, enabling sudden awakening to the highest truth. Compared with all alchemical scriptures circulating in the world, it accords with the same single principle and leaves nothing obscure—truly a raft of mercy upon the sea of suffering, a lamp of wisdom lighting the lost way. Even though practice is never easy, do not shrink from difficulty and settle into complacency.

§13
Original (Chinese)

再有进箴者，身抱金丹之后，即宜高隐洞天深藏福地，勿以黄白卖弄朝廷，为方士之先导，隐显度世以待天符，白日飞升不露圭角，此方为无上上品真人，历万劫而不坏者也。后来同志，玩之鉴之。

One further admonition: once you carry the Golden Elixir within your body, withdraw at once into lofty grotto-heavens and hide deep in blessed lands. Never parade the art of yellow and white (transmuting base metals into gold and silver) before the imperial court, acting as a trailblazer for court magicians. Appearing or remaining hidden as the moment calls, aid the world while awaiting Heaven's tally; ascend in broad daylight without flashing sharp edges. Only such a one is a Perfected One of the supreme, highest rank, indestructible across ten thousand kalpas. Fellow seekers of later ages—savor this and take it to heart.

Original Commentary
Original (Chinese)

长篇巨观，首探大道之源，而讲生人之理与人生老病之故，引彼回头向道修正治平，如古来英雄神仙，身名两树，忠孝两全，非同杨墨、隐怪，使人知此道亦儒道也。养汞培铅无异乎居仁由义，韩朱辟二氏只辟二氏之假徒，更为通论，外扫旁门邪径，一归于穷理尽性以至于命之道，振聋发聩之洪响，经天纬地之大文。

Original commentary: A sweeping, monumental composition. Opening with the source of the Great Way, it explains the principle of human birth and the causes of aging and sickness, turning the reader back toward the Way of self-cultivation, moral rectification, governance, and peace—like the heroic immortals of antiquity who established both personal virtue and noble renown, fulfilling loyalty and filial piety alike. Far from Yang Zhu, Mozi, or obscure eccentrics, it lets the reader see that this Way is also the Confucian Way: nurturing Mercury and cultivating Lead differ not at all from "dwelling in humaneness and walking in righteousness." Its argument that Han Yu and Zhu Xi attacked only counterfeit followers of Buddhism and Daoism is a remarkably broad-minded verdict. Sweeping aside every heterodox side-path, it brings all back to the Way of "fathoming principle, fulfilling inner nature, and arriving at destiny"—a thunderous bell to awaken the deaf, a grand essay weaving heaven and earth.

Key Terms

  • Boundless (wuji) and Supreme Ultimate (taiji): here the expanse of Great Emptiness before conception is the Boundless (yin stillness), while the single spark of luminous breath entering the womb is the Supreme Ultimate (yang movement).
  • True Nature and True Life: here "relaxed breath-channels harboring the Original Spirit within" is True Nature, and "tranquil spirit and thought nurturing Original Breath within" is True Life; see Original Spirit (yuanshen) and inner nature (xing) / existence, life (ming).
  • Heavenly Nature and Heavenly Life: the undivided state of a newborn child; "following this Heavenly Nature to restore Heavenly Life" is itself the Way.
  • Mercury and Lead as the seeds of humaneness and righteousness: humaneness corresponds to Wood containing Fire, and righteousness to Metal generating Water, aligning Confucian virtues with alchemical Lead and Mercury and the four emblems, completed into the Five Phases by the Earth of True Intention (zhenyi).
  • reversal: "Used forward, ancestors beget descendants; used in reversal, perfected immortals and sages emerge."
  • Side-paths (pangmen): the essay lists exploiting women to harvest yin, ingesting the Three Yellows, and firing the Eight Minerals (barren side-paths); massage, calisthenics, vocalized breathing, and herbs (offering only temporary relief); breath-retention (unable to escape rebirth); and drawing in breath to replenish blood (unable to nurture the Immortal Embryo).
  • Hidden merit as substance, Golden Elixir as function: Zhang Sanfeng's foundational formula for cultivating the Way.
  • Yellow and White (huangbai): external alchemy aimed at transmuting base metals into gold and silver.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Cultivating the Way begins with being a decent human being. Zhang Sanfeng places "steadily practicing hidden virtue, compassion, loyalty, filial piety, trustworthiness, and sincerity, fulfilling the human Way" ahead of every technique—a core theme repeated throughout the entire book.
  2. "Only after being settled can one deliberate well." The examples of Zhang Liang, Zhuge Liang, and Li Bi make one simple point: calm the mind first, and only then can you accomplish great work. Cultivation is not fleeing life; a settled person handles life far more capably.
  3. Do not wait for illness to care for yourself. "When the mind moves too long, Spirit gradually tires; when essence is heavily spent, Breath grows ever more exhausted." Conserving energy and cultivating quietness day by day is the plainest and truest hygiene.
  4. Recognize side-paths clearly. Zhang Sanfeng explicitly dismisses sexual exploitation and mineral potions as worthless; massage, calisthenics, and breathing drills can help relieve ailments, but should not be mistaken for the whole path.
  5. The three teachings need not quarrel. "Each speaks of its own wonders, and together they speak of their shared good." Studying internal alchemy requires neither rejecting Confucianism and Buddhism nor pitting them against Daoism.

Translator's Notes

  1. The original text comes from Scroll 3 of the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng, attributed beneath the title to "Originally authored by the Perfected Zhang Sanfeng, Elder Leftover of the Great Yuan; re-edited by Li Xiyue, Mountain Recluse of Changyi from Hanjia." Li Xiyue is Li Hanxu (1806–1856), who re-edited the collection during the Daoguang reign.
  2. "An expanse of Great Emptiness resting in the deep, silent mystery of Heaven (wumu)": wumu comes from the Classic of Poetry ("Odes of Zhou"), referring to the profound, silent movement of Heaven's Way; the character 於 is pronounced wū here rather than yú.
  3. "The eight trigrams stir one another": in standard editions of the Appended Remarks of the Book of Changes, the verb is written dang (荡), while the original text here uses the variant character 盪 with the same meaning.
  4. "Wasting illnesses" (zhai bing): zhai denotes consumption or chronic debilitating illness caused by depletion.
  5. "Li Bi (Marquis of Ye) nourishing himself on Mount Heng and restoring the Tang": Li Bi lived in seclusion on Mount Heng before assisting Emperors Suzong and Daizong in suppressing the An Lushan Rebellion.
  6. "Ge Hong seeking the magistracy of Goulou": in his later years, Ge Hong heard that cinnabar came from Jiaozhi and requested appointment as magistrate of Goulou so he could compound elixirs. "Zhao Yu governing Jiazhou": Zhao Yu served as prefect of Jiazhou at the end of the Sui dynasty and was later venerated as the Perfected Lord of Clear Source. "Perfected Xu Xun administering Jingyang": Xu Xun served as magistrate of Jingyang during the Jin dynasty.
  7. "Weisheng Gao and Chen Zhongzi": Weisheng Gao appears in the Analects (begging vinegar from a neighbor to give to someone who asked him for some), and Chen Zhongzi in the Mencius (refusing his brother's stipend as unrighteous)—classic examples of strained, affected scrupulousness.
  8. "Qiu Changchun answering at court": Qiu Chuji journeyed west to meet Genghis Khan, urging him to revere Heaven, love the people, and curb slaughter. "Chen Xiyi returning to the mountains": Chen Tuan repeatedly declined imperial summonses and lived in seclusion on Mount Hua.
  9. "Twenty-Four Exemplars of Filial Piety—Wu Meng and Ding Lan": Wu Meng let mosquitoes feast on himself to spare his parents, and Ding Lan carved a wooden statue to serve his deceased mother; both appear in the Twenty-Four Exemplars, and Wu Meng was also Xu Xun's teacher, later revered as an immortal.
  10. "The immortal Han Xiang, snow blocking the Blue Pass": when Han Yu was exiled to Chaozhou and crossed Blue Pass, his grandnephew Han Xiang came to see him off, inspiring Han Yu's famous couplet, "Clouds stretch across the Qinling peaks—where is my home? Snow blocks the Blue Pass; my horse will not go on." Later folklore identified Han Xiang with Han Xiangzi of the Eight Immortals.
  11. Zhu Xi did indeed study the Cantong qi in his later years, publishing his Textual Examination of the Cantong qi under the pseudonym "Daoist Zou Xin of Kongtong." The two quotations here match the sense of Zhu Xi's statements, paraphrased in Zhang Sanfeng's words.
  12. "Fathoming principle, fulfilling inner nature, and thereby arriving at destiny": from the Discussion of the Trigrams (Shuogua) in the Book of Changes.
  13. "Self-cultivation makes no distinction between man and woman... ascends at once to the ranks of the immortals": cited by the original text as from the Folio on Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian); retained as printed.
  14. "Old Master Zhang Boduan said: 'With breath-retention...'": the gist appears in Zhang Boduan's own preface to the Wuzhen pian, paraphrased here.
  15. "Humbly I reflect upon our Supreme Patriarch of the Way": the original text leaves a blank honorific space before "Supreme Patriarch" (Taishang), a traditional sign of respect preserved verbatim in the Chinese quote block.
  16. "Of the same kind" (tonglei) and "seeking the other through oneself" (yi ji qiu bi): phrases here and in the Middle Essay such as "seek only within one's own kind" and "the other as host, oneself as guest" have sometimes been read as references to sexual dual cultivation. Yet Zhang Sanfeng explicitly rejects sexual harvesting ("mounting women to harvest yin") as a side-path here and states in the Middle Essay that his method is "no crooked byway." Consistent with the earlier volumes on this site, we read and translate these terms via the pure-stillness approach—yin and yang residing within one's own body, with "self" and "other" signifying Spirit and Breath.
