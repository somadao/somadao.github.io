Song of Completing the Way (Drifter in the World) · Palindrome Poem

Song of Completing the Way (Drifter in the World) and Palindrome Poem — the carefree, unencumbered wandering Daoist

About This Chapter

Two lighthearted pieces revealing the "Scruffy Daoist" side of Zhang Sanfeng:

  • Song of Completing the Way (sharing the same title as the earlier Song of Completing the Way, distinguished here by its opening words "Drifter in the World"): Calling himself a "drifter in the world" (hunshichong), he does not study Chan, does not bow in worship, does not sit in meditation, and is too lazy to take precepts; coming upon wine he drinks a few cups, coming upon meat he eats a few bites; he owes no state grain tax and carries no debts of sons and daughters; a rough stone serves as his pillow and the blue sky as his quilt—"free, free, truly free." Here "not sitting in meditation" is not an attack on quiet sitting, but the voice of one whose practice has matured beyond outward form.
  • Palindrome Poem: A seven-character regulated verse that reads as a complete poem both forward and backward, portraying a secluded mountain life of playing the zither and refining the elixir.

Original Text and Translation

§1Song of Completing the Way (Drifter in the World)
Original (Chinese)

混世虫，混世界；终日混，无宁奈；真孔窍，人不解；寻得着，真自在；莫人喜，莫人爱；无人嫌，无人怪；不参禅，不礼拜；不打坐，懒受戒；走天涯，看世界；遇酒吃几杯，遇肉啖几块；化碗饭，塞皮袋；寻块布，遮四大；房屋破，自家盖。

A drifter in the world, drifting through the world; drifting all day long, nothing to fuss over. The true aperture people do not understand; once you find it, you are truly free. Nobody dotes on me, nobody loves me; nobody dislikes me, nobody blames me. I don't practice Chan, I don't bow in ritual; I don't sit in formal meditation, I'm too lazy to take precepts. I walk to the ends of the earth and watch the world go by; finding wine, I drink a few cups; finding meat, I chew a few bites. Beg a bowl of rice to stuff this leather sack; find a scrap of cloth to cover the Four Great Elements; if the hut is leaky, I patch the roof myself.

§2
Original (Chinese)

主人公，要安泰；不登名利场，不管成和败；不欠国家粮，不少儿女债；他来寻我，我无罣碍；朝游五湖边，暮宿青山内；顽石当枕头，青天作被盖；虎豹不能侵，妖魔不敢害；不觉睡到日头红，无恐无惊无怖骇；从今打破是非门，翻身跳出红尘外；拍手打掌笑呵呵，自在自在真自在。

The Master of the House must dwell in peace and ease: never step into the arena of fame and profit, pay no mind to success or failure; owe no grain tax to the state, carry no karmic debts of children. Should anyone come looking for me, I have no hindrance or entanglement. At dawn I roam the shores of the Five Lakes; at dusk I sleep deep in the green hills. A rough stone is my pillow, the blue sky my quilt; tigers and leopards cannot harm me, demons and goblins dare not touch me. Unawares I sleep until the sun climbs red—without fear, without alarm, without dread or terror. From now on I smash through the gate of right and wrong, somersaulting right out of the red dust; clapping my hands and laughing "he-he"—free, free, truly free!

§3Palindrome Poem
Original (Chinese)

桥边院对柳塘湾，夜月明时半户关；遥驾鹤来归洞晚，静弹琴坐伴云闲；烧丹觅火无空灶，采药寻仙有好山；瓢挂树高人隐久，嚣尘绝水响潺潺。

The courtyard beside the bridge faces the willow-lined pond bend; when the night moon shines bright, the door stands half closed. Driving a crane from afar, I return late to the grotto; sitting quietly strumming the zither, I keep idle clouds company. Firing the elixir and tending the flame, the stove is never empty; gathering herbs and seeking immortals, there are fine mountains here. A gourd hangs high in the tree where the recluse has long dwelt in hiding; worldly clamor cut off, only stream water murmurs softly.

(Read in reverse: Murmuring stream water cuts off worldly clamor; long in hiding, the lofty recluse hangs his gourd in the tree. Fine mountains hold immortals where one seeks herbs to gather; never an empty stove lacking fire when seeking the elixir to refine. Idle clouds keep company with the sitter whose zither plays in stillness; late to the grotto returns the crane driven from afar. The door stands half closed on a bright moonlit night; the willow-lined pond bend faces the courtyard beside the bridge.)

Key Terms

  • Drifter in the world (hunshichong): Literally "world-drifting bug"—Zhang Sanfeng's self-deprecating nickname for one who drifts unpretentiously through the world.
  • Leather sack (pidai): A common Buddhist and Daoist expression for the physical body.
  • Four Great Elements (sida): Earth, water, fire, and wind—referring to the physical body.
  • Palindrome poem (huiwenshi): A poetic form designed to be read meaningfully both forward and backward.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Freedom comes from having no entanglements. "Never step into the arena of fame and profit, pay no mind to success or failure; owe no grain tax to the state, carry no debts of children"—owing nothing, grasping nothing, contesting nothing, the heart grows light.
  2. Form is not the goal. "Don't practice Chan, don't bow in ritual, don't sit in meditation" is spoken from mature mastery, not as advice for beginners to skip practice—yet it reminds us that cultivation must ultimately land in everyday ease and freedom.

Translator's Notes

  1. This scroll contains two poems titled Song of Completing the Way: the earlier one ("When Spirit does not stray outward and Essence does not leak") appears in chapter 4; this one opens with "Drifter in the world," added in parentheses in this site's title to distinguish them.
  2. "Drifting all day long, nothing to fuss over" (zhongri hun, wu ningnai): Ningnai is a variant of naihe ("what can be done / no way around it").
  3. The reverse reading of the Palindrome Poem is provided by this site by reversing the original text character by character for the reader's enjoyment; it does not appear in the original book.
