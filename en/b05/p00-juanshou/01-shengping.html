Introduction to the Life of Zhang Sanfeng

A biographical guide to Zhang Sanfeng — separating the figure in the historical records from the figure in legend, and tracing how this Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng came to be

A Note Before Beginning

Zhang Sanfeng is probably the Daoist master best known to Chinese readers: Mount Wudang, Taijiquan, and the towering Master Zhang of Jin Yong's martial arts novels. Yet what kind of person was the historical Zhang Sanfeng? What do the writings attributed to him actually teach? This introduction aims to keep three things clearly distinct: how the official histories record him, how later legends portray him, and how this Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng came into being. Reading this first will keep the many competing stories from tangling together when you move on to the Treatise on the Great Way and the Chapters on the Mysterious Essentials.

I. Zhang Sanfeng in the Historical Records

The most reliable historical account of Zhang Sanfeng is the "Biographies of Technicians and Diviners" (Fangji zhuan) in the Qing court's official History of the Ming (included as the "Biography" in Scroll 1 of the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng). Its main points are as follows:

  • Names and Epithets: A native of Yizhou in Liaodong (around modern Fuxin in Liaoning), his personal name was Quanyi and also Junbao (sometimes written Junshi), with "Sanfeng" as his sobriquet. Because he paid no heed to his appearance, people also called him "Sloppy Zhang" (Zhang Lata).
  • Appearance and Habits: Tall and imposing, with the build of a tortoise and the back of a crane, large ears, round eyes, and whiskers as stiff as halberds; winter and summer alike, he wore a single patched robe and a bamboo hat. At a meal he could eat an enormous amount, yet at times he went days or even months without eating. Whatever he read, he remembered after a single glance. His comings and goings were unpredictable—some claimed he could travel a thousand li in a day—and he loved to laugh and banter as though no one else were around.
  • Mount Wudang: He once wandered through Mount Wudang and remarked that the mountain was destined to flourish greatly in the future. At that time, the Five Dragons, South Cliff, and Purple Heaven temples on Wudang lay ruined by war. Together with his disciples, he cleared away brambles and rubble, built thatched huts to live in, and before long moved on again.
  • Imperial Searches by Emperors Taizu and Chengzu: In the twenty-fourth year of Hongwu (1391), Emperor Taizu sent envoys to search for him without success. During the Yongle reign, Emperor Chengzu dispatched Supervising Secretary Hu Ying and others bearing imperial letters, incense, and gifts to seek him everywhere. After years of fruitless searching, the emperor mobilized more than three hundred thousand artisans at a cost in the millions to rebuild the palaces and temples of Mount Wudang on a grand scale—fulfilling Zhang Sanfeng's earlier words.
  • Imperial Title: In the third year of Tianshun (1459), Emperor Yingzong granted him an imperial patent bestowing the title "Perfected One Who Penetrates the Subtle and Manifests Transformation" (Tongwei Xianhua Zhenren). The History of the Ming concludes: "In the end, no one could fathom whether he was still alive or not."

The History of the Ming also notes two stranger tales: one says that at Jintai Abbey in Baoji he announced his own death, left a verse, and passed away, only to revive inside his coffin with audible sounds during the burial; another claims he lived under the Jin dynasty and studied under the same master as Liu Bingzhong at the dawn of the Yuan. The History of the Ming itself admits that both "cannot be verified."

Thus, only a few facts are historically certain: in the late Yuan and early Ming, an extraordinary Daoist named Zhang Sanfeng was active around Mount Wudang; two early Ming emperors made earnest efforts to summon him and failed to find him; and Emperor Yingzong formally conferred a title upon him. The histories preserve no reliable dates for his birth or death.

II. Zhang Sanfeng in Legend

Over the centuries, legends around Zhang Sanfeng multiplied:

  • Longevity and Miracles: Some accounts say he lived over two hundred years, spanned the Song, Yuan, and Ming dynasties, or returned from death and appeared and vanished at will. In the Qing dynasty, Wang Xiling, Li Hanxu, and others even reported that Zhang Sanfeng continued to "manifest in person" to transmit the Way. These belong to religious faith; this site presents them as background without passing judgment.
  • Taijiquan: The claim that Zhang Sanfeng invented Taijiquan became widespread in Ming–Qing boxing manuals and modern martial arts circles, yet no Ming historical source records it, and scholars generally treat it as a later attribution. This volume collects his internal alchemy writings and has nothing to do with martial arts.
  • Fiction and Screen Adaptations: The Zhang Sanfeng of Jin Yong's Heaven Sword and Dragon Saber is a fictional character whose adventures are literary invention.
  • Lineage: In the Author's Preface to the Chapters on the Mysterious Essentials included in this book, Zhang Sanfeng states that during the Yanyou reign of the Yuan dynasty he met the Perfected Fire Dragon (traditionally a disciple of Chen Tuan) in the Zhongnan Mountains and received the transmission of internal alchemy. This lineage likewise comes from internal Daoist tradition.

When reading this book, there is no need to debate how many centuries Zhang Sanfeng lived. The worth of an alchemical text lies in whether its principles and methods are genuine and genuinely beneficial to body and mind—something each reader can judge directly.

III. How This Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng Came to Be

Zhang Sanfeng left no collected works compiled by his own hand. The writings we read today were gathered and edited across several generations:

  1. Ming Dynasty: A number of poems and alchemical verses attributed to Zhang Sanfeng already circulated within Daoist circles, and Ming authors such as Lu Xixing (Qianxuzi) cited him.
  2. Early Qing Edition of Wang Xiling: In the early Yongzheng reign, Wang Xiling (courtesy name Mengjiu) served as an official in Sichuan and claimed that Zhang Sanfeng manifested in his government compound. He compiled Zhang's writings and deeds into a book. Scroll 1 of the Complete Works includes Wang's Basic Biography of Master Sanfeng.
  3. Supplements by Liu Zhuo'an: Later, Liu Zhuo'an obtained Wang's edition, supplemented it, and had woodblocks carved for printing.
  4. Re-edition by Li Hanxu: During the Daoguang reign of the Qing, Li Hanxu of Leshan in Sichuan (1806–1856; personal name Xiyue, sobriquets Hanxusheng and Changyi Shanren) took Wang's edition as his base, reorganized it into the eight-scroll Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng current today, and added extensive commentaries and notes. Li Hanxu claimed to have received direct transmission from Lü Dongbin and Zhang Sanfeng on Mount Emei, founding the "Western School" of internal alchemy. Most of the "Original Commentary" and "Original Note" entries in this book are his.
  5. Today's Punctuated Edition: The base text used for this site is the traditional vertical-layout edition punctuated and collated by Cai Congzhe, published by Religious Culture Publishing House in 2013.

It should be noted that not every piece in the Complete Works can be proven to come from Zhang Sanfeng's own brush; some may be later attributions or adaptations, and Li Hanxu himself repeatedly weighs authenticity in his notes (for instance, on the Twenty-Four Poems on the Golden Elixir and the Daoist Ballad Lamenting Monastic Departure). This site follows the received edition without conducting text-by-text authorship verification.

IV. What This Edition Selects and How to Read It

The eight scrolls of the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng cover diverse material: biographies, miracle accounts, alchemical treatises, poetry, moral exhortations, scriptures, and miscellanea. This site selects only the sections devoted to internal alchemy:

The two essays On Fire Phasing in Refining the Elixir, Song of Seated Meditation, Plain Sayings on the Way, and Collected Sayings of Master Sanfeng are already included with Master Qiu Ling's annotations in Book 2, Essentials for Entering Alchemy, so this volume provides links to them instead.

A few reading suggestions:

  1. Begin with the Treatise on the Great Way. It sets out the overall framework of the book with the clearest reasoning.
  2. Be sure to read Dispelling Confusion on Ascending to Heaven. There Zhang Sanfeng states outright that taking a woman as a "vessel or tripod" has "not a shred of reason," and that terms like man and woman, furnace and tripod, other and self "all refer metaphorically to the inner and outer yin and yang within one's own single body, with no literal distinctions of male and female." This is the key to understanding the whole book and recognizing heterodox side-paths.
  3. Read the poems and Daoist ballads as poetry. Part II of the Chapters on the Mysterious Essentials consists mostly of popular songs meant to awaken everyday listeners, full of colloquialisms and metaphor. First take in their spirit—letting go of worldly attachments, quieting the heart, curbing desires, and cultivating within ordinary life—without forcing every image into a rigid technical scheme.
  4. Interpret through the pure-stillness approach. Terms in the text such as "yin and yang," "of the same kind," "the other house," and "gathering" have historically been read in different ways. Consistent with the earlier books on this site, our translation and commentary follow the pure-stillness method—understanding yin and yang as inherent within one's own single body. A small number of pieces centered on dual-cultivation theories have been omitted, as noted in the chapter introductions.
  5. Treat advanced descriptions as information only, not instructions to imitate. Descriptions of lofty phenomena (such as the yang spirit leaving the body, thunderous vibrations, or spirits wailing) as well as concrete breath-guiding exercises are presented strictly for understanding. If any discomfort arises during quiet sitting, stop at once and seek medical care if needed.

Translator's Notes

  1. The History of the Ming passage summarized here comes from Scroll 299, "Biographies of Technicians and Diviners" (Fangji zhuan), reprinted as "Biography" (Liezhuan) in Scroll 1 of the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng.
  2. Biographies record Zhang Sanfeng's personal names variously as Quanyi, Junbao, Junshi, Tong, and Xuanxuan, and write his sobriquet Sanfeng with slightly different characters. This site standardizes on "Zhang Sanfeng."
  3. "Yizhou": a prefecture under the Liaoyang Branch Secretariat during the Yuan dynasty, located around modern Fuxin in Liaoning Province.
  4. "Perfected One Who Penetrates the Subtle and Manifests Transformation" (Tongwei Xianhua Zhenren): this is the wording in the History of the Ming; another biography in Scroll 1 adds "Great" (Da Zhenren).
  5. Li Hanxu's dates (1806–1856) follow the standard scholarly consensus, though minor discrepancies appear in different sources.
