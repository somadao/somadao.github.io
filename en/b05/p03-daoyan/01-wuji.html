III · Sayings on the Way

On the Two Earths Wu and Ji

On the Two Earths Wu and Ji — one True Intention divided into the two earths of movement and stillness; Wu and Ji combining to form the Knife-Tip Measure (daogui)

About This Chapter

The “Sayings on the Way” section in Volume Three of the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng contains three pieces in all. The first two—Plain and Simple Words on the Dao and Five Passages Collected by Sanfeng—are already included in Part Seven of Book Two with Qiu Ling’s commentary, so they are not repeated here; please read them directly:

  • Plain and Simple Words on the Dao — concentrate the spirit and regulate the breath, regulate the breath and concentrate the spirit; a level mind and harmonious breath; finding stillness in the midst of noise
  • Five Passages Collected by Sanfeng — Bai Ziqing’s formula of the Mysterious Pass and formula of the furnace and tripod; Lu Qianxu’s three passages on regulating the breath

This third piece, On the Two Earths Wu and Ji, was actually written by Li Hanxu (the re-editor of this collection). At the opening, Zhang Sanfeng remarks that when he discussed the Seven Reversions and Nine Returns and the Golden Liquid Great Elixir with Hanxu, Hanxu replied, “All of that is simply the wondrous function of the Knife-Tip Measure”; later, seeing that Hanxu had written this essay and finding it “deeply in accord with the original intent,” Zhang recorded and transmitted it for students.

The essay clarifies the ubiquitous alchemical concept of the “two earths Wu and Ji”:

  1. The two earths are really a single “True Intention.” Kan holds Wu-Earth; Li holds Ji-Earth. Spoken of as “intention” (yi), there is only one; spoken of as “earth” (tu), there are two—the intention centered on movement that watches for and draws the yang inside Kan is called Wu-Earth; the intention centered on stillness that warmly nurtures within Li is called Ji-Earth.
  2. The two earths also merge back into one intention. Stillness can enter movement, and movement can merge into stillness; at that point there are neither two earths to cling to nor any rigid boundary of inner and outer.
  3. Knife-Tip Measure (daogui): The character gui (圭) consists of two “earth” graphs (土) stacked together; the character dao (刀, “knife”) is explained as the top stroke of ji (己, namely 𠃌) joined with the diagonal stroke of wu (戊, namely 丿). Combining Wu and Ji into “Knife-Tip Measure” points the practitioner to uniting Li’s Ji-Earth and Kan’s Wu-Earth in one single place—the “center of the central palace.”

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

先生曰：吾与涵虚子谈七返九还金液大丹之道，涵虚云「据先生言，是皆刀圭妙用耳。」他日见涵虚作戊己二土篇深合元意，爰录而传之，学人欲了长生，舍此不能通神也。

Master Zhang Sanfeng said: When I discussed the Way of Seven Reversions and Nine Returns and the Golden Liquid Great Elixir with Master Hanxu, Hanxu said, “Going by what you say, Master, all of this is simply the wondrous function of the Knife-Tip Measure.” Another day I saw that Hanxu had composed On the Two Earths Wu and Ji, which accorded deeply with my original intent, so I recorded it to hand down. If a student wishes to settle the matter of long life, apart from this they cannot penetrate the divine.

§2
Original (Chinese)

涵虚曰：悟真篇云「离坎若还无戊己，虽含四象不成丹；只缘彼此怀真土，遂使金丹有返还。」此四句诗，极尽和合丹头之妙，盖以火中生木汞，水里产金铅，木火金水，含四象于坎离之间，此时离中有己，坎中有戊，二土为媒，四象可合也。

Hanxu said: The Folios on Awakening to Reality says: “If Li and Kan were without Wu and Ji, though containing the Four Emblems they could not form the elixir; solely because each side harbors True Earth does the Golden Elixir achieve reversion and return.” These four lines exhaust the marvel of harmonizing the elixir seed. Because fire gives birth to Wood-Mercury and water produces Metal-Lead, wood, fire, metal, and water—the Four Emblems—are all contained between Kan and Li. At this juncture Li holds Ji-Earth and Kan holds Wu-Earth; with the two earths acting as matchmaker, the Four Emblems can unite.

§3
Original (Chinese)

或谓：真意属土，土有戊己，得非有二意乎？曰：似也，然非也。名为意则一，号为土则二，以一意分作两意，乃有二土之可名，始有内外之可别，然必知动知静焉：原夫戊土者外药也，阴里真阳也，以动为主，故取坎之期惟侦其动，知动者意也，即以知动之时为一土。己土者内药也，阳里真阴也，以静为主，故填离之后致养于静，知静者意也，即以知静之时为一土。此一意分为两意，而可名为二土者也。

Someone asks: True Intention belongs to earth, and earth has both Wu and Ji—does that not mean there are two intentions? Answer: It seems so, yet it is not so. Named “intention,” it is one; styled “earth,” it is two. Dividing one intention into two gives rise to the names of the two earths and establishes the distinction between outer and inner—yet one must know movement and know stillness.

Wu-Earth is the outer medicine, True Yang within yin, taking movement as its keynote; hence at the time of drawing from Kan one watches solely for its stirring—what knows the movement is intention, so the moment of knowing movement is reckoned as one earth. Ji-Earth is the inner medicine, True Yin within yang, taking stillness as its keynote; hence after replenishing Li one devotes oneself to nurturing in stillness—what knows stillness is intention, so the moment of knowing stillness is reckoned as the other earth. This is how one intention divides into two and may be called the two earths.

§4
Original (Chinese)

又以两意合为一意，斯无二土之可拘，并无内外之可执，然必能动能静焉：原夫己土者内丹也，亦可变为戊土，以离日而映坎月，种铅得铅，只见戊而不见己，静能入乎动也。戊土者外丹也，亦可化为己土，以坎阳而补离阴，种汞得汞，只见己而不见戊，动能并乎静也。此两意合为一意，而不拘为二土者也。然则彼此怀真土，亦各怀一土乎？亦共怀其一土乎？聪明活泼人自了然也。

Again, when the two intentions reunite into one, there are no two earths to be bound by and no inner and outer to cling to—yet one must be capable both of movement and of stillness.

Ji-Earth is the inner elixir, yet it can also turn into Wu-Earth: illuminating the moon of Kan with the sun of Li, planting lead to obtain lead, one sees only Wu and not Ji—this is stillness able to enter movement. Wu-Earth is the outer elixir, yet it can also transform into Ji-Earth: replenishing the yin of Li with the yang of Kan, planting mercury to obtain mercury, one sees only Ji and not Wu—this is movement able to merge into stillness. This is how the two intentions reunite into one without being bound to two earths.

Then when the poem says “each side harbors True Earth,” do they each harbor a separate earth, or do they share one and the same earth? A bright and nimble reader will see it clearly at once.

§5
Original (Chinese)

金丹返还之道，非此真意不行，非惟和合四象，并能驱使四象。何也？盖以木中藏火，投之水乡而火发，木载金升，汞去复来，火得金而为返也。水里生金，烹以火功而水沸，金随木上，铅去复回，金得火而为还也。金火返还之术，即是七返九还金液大丹，人欲大丹成就，舍此真意奚由哉？

The Way of reversion and return of the Golden Elixir cannot proceed without this True Intention; not only does it harmonize the Four Emblems, it can also direct them. Why? Because wood conceals fire: cast into the watery realm, fire blazes up, wood carries metal upward, and mercury goes out only to come back—that is fire obtaining metal, which is “reversion.” Water engenders metal: simmered with fire work, the water boils, metal ascends with wood, and lead departs only to return—that is metal obtaining fire, which is “return.” This art of metal and fire reverting and returning is precisely the Seven Reversions and Nine Returns, the Golden Liquid Great Elixir. If one wishes to bring the Great Elixir to completion, apart from this True Intention, where else could one turn?

§6
Original (Chinese)

紫阳云「赤龙黑虎各西东，四象交加戊己中。」陶仙谓「龙从火出，青龙变为赤龙，虎向水生，白虎乃名黑虎，只这龙虎赤黑四字，已具四象。」交加者，攒簇也。愚按：作丹之士，欲使四象攒簇，必令五行相辏，其所谓戊己中者，不是离中坎中，乃是中宫中央也。东西间隔，刀圭合之，二物变为四象，四象围入一村，一村聚会五行，五行聚而丹乃结，故下二句云「复姤自兹能应用，金丹谁道不成功。」复姤应用，十月火符也。进火于复，退符于姤，运用抽添，自然中度，金丹乃可成矣。

Ziyang (Zhang Boduan) said: “Red dragon and black tiger stand apart east and west; the Four Emblems intertwine within Wu and Ji.” Immortal Tao explained: “The dragon emerges from fire, so the azure dragon turns into the red dragon; the tiger is born from water, so the white tiger is called the black tiger. These four words alone—dragon, tiger, red, and black—already contain the Four Emblems.” “Intertwine” means to gather and cluster.

I (Hanxu) note: When an alchemist wishes the Four Emblems to cluster together, they must bring the Five Phases to converge. What the poem calls “within Wu and Ji” is neither the center of Li nor the center of Kan, but the very center of the central palace. East and west stand separated, and the Knife-Tip Measure joins them: two things become Four Emblems, the Four Emblems gather into one village, that one village assembles the Five Phases, and when the Five Phases assemble, the elixir congeals. Hence the next two lines say: “From here on Return (Fu) and Encounter (Gou) can be applied at will; who says the Golden Elixir will not succeed?” Applying Return and Encounter is the ten-month fire talisman: advance the fire at Return, withdraw the talisman at Encounter, operating extraction and addition so they naturally hit the mark—then the Golden Elixir can be achieved.

§7
Original (Chinese)

丹家以戊己为刀，二土为圭者，结字肖形，正示人以打合之意。但二土为圭，人所共知；戊己为刀，人所鲜知。潜虚云「有一士人，会意而解，𠃌（音捏）己字，丿（音撇）戊字，前无所本，似为得之。」涵虚曰：非无本也。琴谱以数字攒一字，一字取一画，合左右投弹之法，备见于一字之中，此即以琴谱为本者也。𠃌者，己之头，丿者，戊之旁，戊己二土，以刀圭两字合之，盖望人将离己、坎戊之二土，合为一处也。仙家隐语，往往如是。

When alchemists take Wu and Ji as “knife” (dao, 刀) and two earths as “jade tablet” (gui, 圭), they are using the graphic structure of the characters to point directly to the idea of uniting them. Yet while everyone knows that two “earths” (土) make gui (圭), few know how Wu and Ji make dao (刀). Lu Qianxu remarked: “A certain scholar interpreted it by compound ideography: 𠃌 (pronounced nie) represents ji (己) and 丿 (pronounced pie) represents wu (戊); though without earlier precedent, this seems to hit the mark.”

Hanxu says: It is not without precedent! Guqin tablature compresses several characters into a single glyph, taking one stroke from each character so that the fingering of both left and right hands is displayed inside a single sign—this analysis takes guqin tablature as its model. 𠃌 is the head of ji (己), and 丿 is the side stroke of wu (戊). Combining the two earths Wu and Ji through the two characters dao and gui expresses the wish that practitioners unite Li’s Ji-Earth and Kan’s Wu-Earth into one single place. Veiled expressions of the immortals are often like this.

Key Terms

  • Wu-Earth and Ji-Earth: In the Five Phases, earth divides into yin and yang—Wu is yang earth, Ji is yin earth. Alchemists call the yang within Kan Wu-Earth and the yin within Li Ji-Earth; see Wu and Ji.
  • True Intention (zhenyi): Alchemists assign intention to earth, calling it “True Earth” or the “Yellow Dame”; this essay explains that intention is really one, divided into two earths according to movement and stillness.
  • Outer medicine / inner medicine, outer elixir / inner elixir: Here Wu-Earth (drawing yang from Kan) is called outer medicine / outer elixir, and Ji-Earth (nurturing within Li) is called inner medicine / inner elixir—both located within one’s own body.
  • Drawing from Kan to replenish Li (qu Kan tian Li): Taking the yang inside Kan to fill the yin inside Li; see conjunction of Kan and Li.
  • Four Emblems (sixiang): Wood, fire, metal, and water (azure dragon, red dragon, white tiger, black tiger).
  • Knife-Tip Measure (daogui): Explained here as “Wu and Ji form dao, two earths form gui,” symbolizing the union of the two earths.
  • Return (Fu) and Encounter (Gou): The Return hexagram marks one yang arising; the Encounter hexagram marks one yin arising. Advancing fire at Return and withdrawing the talisman at Encounter form the outline of the ten-month fire phasing.
  • Central palace (zhonggong): In the Five Phases, earth occupies the center—what alchemists call the Yellow Court or central palace.

Putting It into Practice

  1. There is only one intention. The “two earths,” “Yellow Dame,” and “True Intention” in alchemical books all refer to one single awareness. Do not imagine you must juggle several separate “intentions” at once.
  2. Know movement in movement; know stillness in stillness. When movement arises, know clearly that it is moving; when settling into stillness, know clearly that it is still—that clear knowing itself is True Intention.
  3. Movement and stillness are not two. Once practice matures, stillness can respond to movement and movement never leaves stillness; there is no need to fuss over which is Wu and which is Ji.
  4. Learn to recognize veiled imagery when reading alchemy texts. Terms like “Knife-Tip Measure,” “Wu and Ji,” and “dragon and tiger” are metaphors drawn from character shapes and the Five Phases; once you see what they point to, the terminology will no longer tangle you up.

Translator's Notes

  1. The original text appears in Volume Three, “Sayings on the Way,” of the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng, following Plain and Simple Words on the Dao and Five Passages Collected by Sanfeng. The opening “Master said” paragraph is attributed to Zhang Sanfeng; the main body is written by Li Hanxu (styled Hanxuzi and Xiyue).
  2. “If Li and Kan were without Wu and Ji… does the Golden Elixir achieve reversion and return”: A seven-character quatrain from Zhang Boduan’s (Perfected Ziyang’s) Folios on Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian).
  3. “Red dragon and black tiger stand apart east and west… who says the Golden Elixir will not succeed?”: Also a seven-character quatrain from the Folios on Awakening to Reality.
  4. “Immortal Tao”: Exact identity uncertain; possibly Tao Susi, a Ming-dynasty commentator on the Folios on Awakening to Reality.
  5. “Qianxu”: Lu Xixing (styled Qianxuzi), founder of the Eastern Lineage of Ming inner alchemy and author of the Brief Preface to the Folios on Awakening to Reality and other commentaries.
  6. “𠃌 (pronounced nie) represents ji, 丿 (pronounced pie) represents wu”: The character 刀 (“knife”) consists of two strokes—the horizontal-hook (𠃌) and the left-falling diagonal (丿)—explained as taking the opening stroke of 己 and the diagonal stroke of 戊. The original interlinear phonetic glosses are retained.
  7. “Guqin tablature compresses several characters into a single glyph”: Refers to the abbreviated-character notation (jianzipu) of the seven-string zither (guqin), which combines fragments of several characters into one symbol specifying left- and right-hand fingerings.
