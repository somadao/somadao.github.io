1 · Settling the Mind and Regulating the Breath — what to do before you sit down

Listen to this chapterENEnglish

About This Chapter

This section is only one short passage, yet it is the foundation of the whole practice. Before you sit down, first settle the mind — when something comes, deal with it; when it has gone, let it go; do not ask about success or failure, simply do your best. Then regulate the breath — when the breath should rise, let it rise; when it should descend, let it descend; do not deliberately interfere. The time of sitting is not tied to the zi and wu hours: sit whenever you have time. Nor need you cross your legs, but the hands should hold the “firm grip.” Qiu Ling’s note explains exactly how to make the firm grip, and adds that when you are startled you can tighten it a little and the fright will settle by itself.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

未坐先治心，空如寂如，事来则理，理去则忘，置事成败于勿问，惟自尽心而已。次则理气，充如浩如，当升听升，当降听降，置作用于勿事，惟俟机醇而已。盖以气由心生，心治气自治焉。如是行持，总以无间为妙。无间，则身心自泰。故坐不拘子午，得闲则坐，坐亦不须结趺，而手须握固，所以镇惊也。

Before sitting, first settle the mind: let it be empty, let it be still. When something comes, deal with it; when it has been dealt with, forget it. Put the success or failure of things aside and do not ask about it; simply do your own best, that is all. Next, regulate the breath: let it be full, let it be vast. When it should rise, let it rise; when it should descend, let it descend. Put deliberate action aside and do not engage in it; simply wait for the moment to ripen of itself, that is all. For breath is born of the mind; when the mind is settled, the breath settles of itself. When you practice like this, being without interruption is what matters most; without interruption, body and mind are naturally at peace. So sitting is not tied to the zi and wu hours: whenever you have free time, sit. Nor need you sit with the legs fully crossed, but the hands must hold the firm grip, which serves to steady you against fright.

Qiu Ling's noteon §1
Original note (Chinese)

练功须全身放松，求其自然，先治心，次理气，然后打坐。无间，即练功不要间断。打坐不必讲究子时午时，子时为晚间十一时至晨一时，午时为上午十一时至下午一时。打坐亦不必盘膝，惟须握固，即以大拇指甲置于无名指下节横纹下，以四指握之，须用中指中节压盖大指指甲之末，闻惊则加劲握，其惊自定。

In practice the whole body must be relaxed, seeking what is natural: first settle the mind, then regulate the breath, then sit. “Without interruption” means not interrupting practice. Sitting need not be confined to the zi or wu hour — the zi hour is from eleven at night to one in the morning, the wu hour from eleven in the morning to one in the afternoon. Nor need one sit cross-legged, but one must hold the firm grip: place the nail of the thumb just below the crease at the base of the ring finger and close the four fingers over it, pressing the end of the thumbnail with the middle joint of the middle finger. On hearing a startling sound, grip a little harder, and the fright will settle by itself.

Key Terms

  • Settling the mind: making the mind empty and still; dealing with things as they come and forgetting them when they have gone, not asking about success or failure, simply doing one’s best.
  • Regulating the breath: letting the breath be full and vast, letting it rise when it should rise and descend when it should descend, without deliberate interference.
  • Waiting for the moment to ripen (si ji chun 俟机醇): waiting until the time ripens naturally.
  • Without interruption (wujian 无间): not interrupting practice.
  • Zi and wu: the zi hour (11 pm–1 am) and the wu hour (11 am–1 pm), the traditional practice hours of the alchemists.
  • Sitting with the legs fully crossed (jiefu 结趺): the full-lotus posture.
  • Firm grip: the thumb pressed against the crease at the base of the ring finger, the four fingers closed around it; used to steady oneself against fright. From the Laozi: “its bones are weak and its sinews soft, yet its grip is firm.”

Putting It into Practice

  1. Three sentences before you sit: when something comes, deal with it; when it has been dealt with, forget it. Do not ask about success or failure; simply do your best. When the breath should rise, let it rise; when it should descend, let it descend. These can serve as an “opening” before each session of quiet sitting, recited silently once.
  2. How to make the firm grip: the tip of the thumb (the nail) rests on the crease at the base of the ring finger; the other four fingers close around it, with the middle joint of the middle finger pressing on the thumbnail. Hold both hands like this and rest them naturally on the legs.
  3. No fixed time, no fixed posture: sit whenever you have time; a loose cross-legged position, a half lotus or a chair are all fine — what matters is “without interruption,” sitting every day.
  4. When startled: if a sudden sound startles you during quiet sitting, tighten the firm grip slightly and relax deeply once; the fright will pass.

Translator's Notes

  1. “Put deliberate action aside and do not engage in it” (zhi zuoyong yu wu shi 置作用于勿事): setting aside deliberate action and not engaging in it; the translation renders it accordingly.
  2. “Firm grip” (wogu): from Laozi, chapter 55, “its bones are weak and its sinews soft, yet its grip is firm,” describing an infant’s clenched fist. Schools differ slightly in how they make the firm grip; this translation follows Qiu Ling’s description.
