Appendix: Sun Bu’er’s Poems on the Stages of Women’s Inner Alchemy, with Commentary · 第九首 炼神

Poem 9 · Refining the Spirit

Poem 9 · Refining the Spirit — the same for men and women

Listen to this chapterENEnglish

About This Chapter

生前舍利子，一旦入吾怀。慎似持盈器，柔如抚幼孩。地门须固闭，天阙要先开。洗濯黄芽净，山头震地雷。

The ninth poem is about “refining the spirit,” and is the same for men and women.

  • “Śarīra” is a metaphor for the Original Spirit: the Original Spirit does not change through the kalpas; what changes is the cognitive spirit; by refining the body in true emptiness, the cognitive spirit is gradually refined away and the Original Spirit gradually appears, like polishing a mirror — when the dust is gone, the brightness appears of itself;
  • In guarding it, be as careful as holding a vessel filled to the brim, as gentle as caring for an infant;
  • The lower openings must be gathered in tightly; when essence and breath have gathered to fullness, they naturally rush up to the brain;
  • “Washing” relies entirely on settled stillness: a small stillness must bring a small movement, a great stillness a great movement.

Two passages of Chen Yingning’s commentary on this poem are especially fine:

  1. “The power of settled stillness is something we can master ourselves… as for the effect of tremors, that follows nature and cannot be forced by human effort.” All one can master is settled stillness; the tremors are a natural response. Ordinary people have no such effects because they cannot attain settled stillness; cultivators do not obtain great effects because their power of settled stillness is still weak.
  2. Appended note: the difference between śarīra and the golden elixir. He quotes the view of the Old Man of Qinghua: Buddhists take seeing one’s nature as their principle and do not value essence and breath; after nirvāṇa, the essence and breath left behind form śarīra; Daoists cultivate nature and life together, merging essence, breath, and spirit into one, and after “feathered transformation” no śarīra remain. His final conclusion is very even-tempered: “Ultimately, at the supreme source, nature is breath and breath is nature; what is the same is the reality, what differs is only the name.”

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

生前舍利子，一旦入吾怀。

The śarīra from before birth, once it enters my bosom.

Chen Yingning's noteon §1
Original note (Chinese)

“舍利子”，乃佛家之名词，此处比喻元神。“生前”者，即未有此身之前。吾人元神历劫不变，变者识神也。用真空炼形之功，将识神渐渐炼去，则元神渐渐显出，譬如磨镜，尘垢既消，光明斯现，乃知一切神通，皆吾人本性中所固有者，非从外来。

此诗云一旦入吾怀，似指气之一方面而言。然此时气与神已不可分离，言神而气在其中，言气而神在其中。吕祖《敲爻歌》云：“铅池迸出金光现，汞火流珠入帝京。”曰铅池、曰金光，言气也；曰汞火、曰流珠，言神也。“帝京”即中丹田，又名绛宫、神室，乃心之部位。心为一身君主，故曰帝京。此诗所谓入吾怀者，亦同此意。

“Śarīra” (shelizi 舍利子) is a Buddhist term, here a metaphor for the Original Spirit. “Before birth” means before we had this body. Our Original Spirit does not change through the kalpas; what changes is the cognitive spirit. Using the work of refining the body in true emptiness, the cognitive spirit is gradually refined away and the Original Spirit gradually appears, like polishing a mirror — once the dust is gone, the brightness appears; only then does one know that all spiritual powers are inherent in our original nature and do not come from outside.

When this poem says “once it enters my bosom,” it seems to speak from the side of breath. But at this point breath and spirit can no longer be separated: speak of spirit, and breath is within it; speak of breath, and spirit is within it. Patriarch Lü’s Song of Striking the Lines says: “From the lead pool golden light bursts forth and appears; the flowing pearl of the mercury fire enters the imperial capital.” The lead pool and golden light speak of breath; the mercury fire and the flowing pearl speak of spirit. “The imperial capital” is the middle Cinnabar Field, also called the Crimson Palace or the chamber of the spirit, the position of the heart. The heart is the sovereign of the whole body, hence the imperial capital. What this poem calls “entering my bosom” has the same meaning.

§2
Original (Chinese)

慎似持盈器，柔如抚幼孩。

Careful as holding a vessel filled to the brim; gentle as caring for a small child.

Chen Yingning's noteon §2
Original note (Chinese)

老子云：“持而盈之，不如其已。”又云：“保此道者不欲盈。”又云：“大盈若冲，其用不穷。”即此可知此联上句之义。

老子云：“专气致柔，能如婴儿乎？”又云：“我独泊兮其未兆，如婴儿之未孩。”又云：“人之生也柔弱，其死也坚强。”即此可知此联下句之义。

Laozi said: “To hold it and fill it to the brim is not as good as stopping in time.” He also said: “Those who preserve this Way do not wish to be full.” And: “Great fullness seems empty; its use is inexhaustible.” From these one can know the meaning of the first line of this couplet.

Laozi said: “Concentrating the breath and attaining softness, can you be like an infant?” He also said: “I alone am placid, with no sign yet, like an infant that has not yet learned to smile.” And: “At birth people are soft and weak; at death they are hard and stiff.” From these one can know the meaning of the second line of this couplet.

§3
Original (Chinese)

地门须固闭，天阙要先开。

The gate of Earth must be firmly shut; the portal of Heaven must first be opened.

Chen Yingning's noteon §3
Original note (Chinese)

凡言“地”者，皆在人身之下部；凡言“天”者，皆在人身之上部。修炼家最忌精气下泄，故凡下窍皆要收敛紧密。一身精气，渐聚渐满，既不能下泄，必上冲于脑部，斯时耳闻风声，目睹光掣，脑后震动，脐下潮涌，异景甚多。

龙门派第十七代，广西洪教燧君，传有《金丹歌》一首，尚未行世，曾记其中有句云：“万马奔腾攻两耳，流星闪电灼双眉，若还到此休惊惧，牢把心神莫动移。”即言闭地门、开天阙时之现象。

Whatever is called “Earth” is in the lower part of the body; whatever is called “Heaven” is in the upper part of the body. What cultivators most avoid is the downward leakage of essence and breath, so all the lower openings must be gathered in tightly. When the essence and breath of the whole body gradually gather and gradually fill, and cannot leak downward, they must rush up to the brain; at this time the ears hear the sound of wind, the eyes see flashes of light, there are tremors behind the head and surges below the navel — there are many strange scenes.

Mr. Hong Jiaosui of Guangxi, of the seventeenth generation of the Longmen school, transmitted a Song of the Golden Elixir that has not yet circulated in the world; I remember a passage in it that says: “Ten thousand horses galloping assail the two ears; shooting stars and lightning blaze at the two eyebrows; if you come to this point, do not be alarmed or afraid; hold firm to mind and spirit and do not be moved.” This describes the phenomena when the gate of Earth is shut and the portal of Heaven opened.

§4
Original (Chinese)

洗濯黄芽净，山头震地雷。

Wash the yellow sprouts clean; on the mountain top the earth-thunder shakes.

Chen Yingning's noteon §4
Original note (Chinese)

吕祖度张仙姑，有《步蟾宫词》云：“地雷震动山头雨，要洗濯黄芽出土。”“黄芽”者，大还丹之别名也。此处言“山头”，大约是指头上泥丸宫。前诗第三首亦云：“山头并海底，雨过一声雷。”据字面观之，似无差别；以实际论，则效验大异。

“洗濯”之作用，不外乎静定。凡丹道小静之后，必有小动；大静之后，必有大动。其静定之力愈深，则震动之效愈大，充其震动之量，直可冲开顶门而出，然非大静之后不克至此。

今按静定之力，吾人能自作主，可以由暂而久，由浅而深。若夫震动之效，乃是顺其自然，非人力可以勉强造作，似乎不能由人做主，但小静必小动，大静必大动，其反应百不爽一。常人所以无此效验者，因其未能静定故。修炼家所以不能得大效验者，因其虽知静定，而静定之力犹嫌薄弱故。释门学禅者，亦能静定数日，而终久无此效验者，因其徒知打坐不知炼气故。

When Patriarch Lü converted the Immortal Lady Zhang, he wrote the lyric “Stepping into the Toad Palace,” which says: “The earth-thunder shakes the rain on the mountain top; the yellow sprouts must be washed to come out of the soil.” “Yellow sprouts” is another name for the Great Returned Elixir. The “mountain top” here probably refers to the Muddy Pellet Palace in the head. The third poem above also says: “Between the mountain top and the sea bottom, after the rain, a clap of thunder.” Looking at the wording, there seems to be no difference; in actual practice, however, the effects differ greatly.

The working of “washing” is nothing but settled stillness. In the alchemical Way, a small stillness is always followed by a small movement, and a great stillness by a great movement. The deeper the power of settled stillness, the greater the effect of the tremors; at the full measure of the tremors, it can burst straight out through the crown gate — but this cannot be reached except after great stillness.

Note now: the power of settled stillness is something we can master ourselves; it can go from brief to long, from shallow to deep. As for the effect of tremors, that follows nature and cannot be forced by human effort; it seems not to be something one can master; but a small stillness always brings a small movement and a great stillness a great movement, and the response does not fail once in a hundred times. Ordinary people have no such effects because they cannot attain settled stillness. Cultivators cannot obtain great effects because, though they know settled stillness, their power of settled stillness is still too weak. Buddhists who study Chan can also remain in settled stillness for several days, yet never have such effects, because they only know how to sit in meditation and do not know how to refine the breath.

Chen Yingning's noteappended note: *śarīra* and the golden elixir
Original note (Chinese)

附注：“舍利子”在此处为内丹之代名词，然非佛家所谓舍利之本意。究竟舍利子与金丹，是同是异？修佛与修仙，其结果有何分别？皆吾人所急欲知者，而各家经书咸未论及。虽《楞严经》有十种仙之说，是乃佛家一面之辞；除佛经外，凡中国古今一切书籍记载，皆未见有十种仙之名目，似未可据为定论。吾国人性习，素尚调和，非但儒道同源本无冲突，即对于外来之佛教，亦复不存歧视，彼此融通，较他种教义之惟我独尊者，其容量之广狭，实大不同。

而青华老人之论舍利，尤为公允，意谓：佛家以见性为宗，精气非其所贵。万物有生有灭，而性无生灭，涅槃之后，本性圆明，超出三界，永免轮迴。遗骸火化之后，所余精气，结为舍利，譬如珠之出蚌，与灵性别矣；而能光华照耀者，由其精气聚于是也。

人身精气神，原不可分，佛家独要明心见性，洗发智慧，将神光单提出来，遗下精气，交结成形，弃而不管；然因其诸漏已尽、禅定功深，故其身中之精气，亦非凡物，所以舍利子能变化隐显，光色各别。由此推之，佛家所谓不生不灭者，神也，即性也；其舍利子者，精气也，即命也。彼灭度后，神已超于象外，而精气尚留滞于寰中也。

若道家则性命双修，将精气神混合为一，周天火候，炼成身外之身，神在是，精在是，气在是，分之无可分也。故其羽化而后，皆无舍利之留存。倘偶有坐化而遗下舍利者，其平日工夫，必是偏重于佛教方面，详于性而略于命也。性命双修之士，将此身精气神团结得晶莹活泼，骨肉俱化，毛窍都融，血似银膏，体如流火，畅贯于四肢百节之间，照耀于清静虚无之域。故能升沉莫测，隐显无端，释道之不同如此。

佛家重炼性，一灵独耀迴脱根尘，此之谓“性长生”；仙家重炼炁，遍体纯阳，金光透露，此之谓“炁长生”。究竟到了无上根源，性就是炁，炁就是性，同者其实，异者其名耳。

Appended note: “śarīra” here is a code name for the inner elixir, but it is not the original meaning of śarīra in Buddhism. Are śarīra and the golden elixir, after all, the same or different? What difference is there between the results of cultivating Buddhahood and cultivating immortality? These are things we are eager to know, yet the scriptures of all the schools have not discussed them. Although the Śūraṅgama Sūtra speaks of ten kinds of immortals, that is the Buddhists’ one-sided account; apart from the Buddhist scriptures, in all the books and records of China, ancient and modern, the names of ten kinds of immortals are nowhere to be seen, and it seems this cannot be taken as settled. The disposition of our people has always valued harmony: not only do Confucianism and Daoism share a common source and have no conflict, but even toward the foreign Buddhism they harbor no discrimination, blending and communicating with it; compared with other doctrines that hold themselves alone to be supreme, the breadth of their capacity is truly very different.

And the Old Man of Qinghua’s discussion of śarīra is especially fair. His meaning is: Buddhists take seeing one’s nature as their principle, and do not value essence and breath. The myriad things arise and cease, but nature neither arises nor ceases; after nirvāṇa, the original nature is perfectly bright, transcends the three realms, and is forever freed from transmigration. After the remains are cremated, the remaining essence and breath form śarīra, like a pearl coming out of an oyster, already separated from the numinous nature; and that they can shine with radiance is because essence and breath are gathered there.

The essence, breath, and spirit of the human body are originally inseparable. Buddhists alone want to illuminate the mind and see their nature, to cleanse and develop wisdom, lifting out the light of the spirit by itself and leaving essence and breath behind to combine and take form, discarding them and paying them no heed; yet because all their outflows are exhausted and their meditative absorption is deep, the essence and breath in their bodies are no ordinary things either, and so śarīra can transform, appear and disappear, and differ in light and color. Reasoning from this, what the Buddhists call neither arising nor ceasing is spirit, that is, nature; their śarīra are essence and breath, that is, life. After their passing into extinction, the spirit has already transcended beyond images, while essence and breath still linger in the world.

The Daoists, however, cultivate nature and life together, merging essence, breath, and spirit into one; with the fire phasing of the Celestial Circuit they refine a body beyond the body, in which spirit is present, essence is present, breath is present, and they cannot be divided even if one wished. Therefore after their feathered transformation, none leaves śarīra behind. If occasionally someone who passed away in meditation leaves śarīra, his everyday work must have leaned toward the Buddhist side, detailed on nature and sketchy on life. Those who cultivate nature and life together bind the essence, breath, and spirit of this body into something crystalline and lively; bones and flesh are all transformed, the pores all melted, the blood like silver cream, the body like flowing fire, passing freely through the four limbs and hundred joints, shining in the realm of purity, stillness, and emptiness. Therefore they can rise and sink unfathomably, appear and disappear without trace. Such is the difference between Buddhism and Daoism.

Buddhists emphasize refining nature; one numinous light shining alone, far removed from the roots and dusts — this is called “long life of nature.” Immortals emphasize refining breath; the whole body pure yang, golden light shining through — this is called “long life of breath.” Ultimately, at the supreme source, nature is breath and breath is nature; what is the same is the reality, what differs is only the name.

Key Terms

  • Śarīra: in this poem a metaphor for the Original Spirit, and in the appended note a code name for the inner elixir; see Holy Embryo.
  • Original Spirit, cognitive spirit: the Original Spirit does not change through the kalpas; what changes is the cognitive spirit; when the cognitive spirit is refined away, the Original Spirit appears of itself.
  • Imperial capital: the middle Cinnabar Field, also called the Crimson Palace or the chamber of the spirit, the position of the heart; see Crimson Palace.
  • Lead pool, golden light: speaking of breath; mercury fire, flowing pearl: speaking of spirit.
  • Holding the full, caring for the young: Laozi, “to hold it and fill it to the brim is not as good as stopping in time,” “concentrating the breath and attaining softness, can you be like an infant?”
  • Gate of Earth, portal of Heaven: the openings of the lower and upper parts of the body; see Gate of Heaven / Door of Earth.
  • Yellow sprouts: another name for the Great Returned Elixir; see Golden Flower.
  • Washing: nothing but settled stillness.
  • Small stillness, small movement; great stillness, great movement: the power of settled stillness one can master; the effect of tremors follows nature.
  • Long life of nature, long life of breath: Buddhists emphasize refining nature, immortals emphasize refining breath; ultimately nature is breath.

Putting It into Practice

  1. All one can master is “settled stillness.” This passage of Chen Yingning can serve as a motto: the power of settled stillness can go from brief to long, from shallow to deep; as for the effects, they follow nature and cannot be forced. Put your effort where you have mastery.
  2. “Careful as holding a vessel filled to the brim; gentle as caring for a small child” is the attitude of all cultivation. Seek not fullness, not hardness, but care and gentleness.
  3. If strong bodily reactions occur — roaring in the ears, flashes between the eyebrows — “if you come to this point, do not be alarmed or afraid; hold firm to mind and spirit and do not be moved.” But persistent ringing in the ears, headaches, or visual disturbances call for a doctor first.
  4. Chen Yingning’s attitude toward Buddhism and Daoism is worth learning from: he acknowledges the differences but belittles neither, and finally comes down to “what is the same is the reality, what differs is only the name.”

Translator's Notes

  1. In this poem’s commentary the heading quotes “careful as holding a soft vessel” (慎似持柔器), whereas the original poem reads “careful as holding a vessel filled to the brim” (慎似持盈器). Judging from the commentary’s quotations “to hold it and fill it to the brim,” “do not wish to be full,” and “great fullness seems empty,” 盈 (“full”) should be correct, and we use 盈 throughout.
  2. The Song of Striking the Lines: attributed to Lü Dongbin.
  3. Hong Jiaosui: of the seventeenth generation of the Longmen school; his life is unknown.
  4. The Old Man of Qinghua: an immortal under whose name texts were produced in the Qing; the relevant passages are found among the recorded sayings of the Secret Text of Qinghua tradition; Chen Yingning also quotes the recorded sayings of the Old Man of Qinghua in the commentary on poem 13.
  5. The “ten kinds of immortals” of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra: see the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, fascicle 8, which lists ten kinds such as earth-walking immortals and flying immortals.
  6. The “appended note” originally stands at the end of the commentary on this poem; we have set it out as a separate note block.
