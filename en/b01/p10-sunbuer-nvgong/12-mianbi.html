Appendix: Sun Bu’er’s Poems on the Stages of Women’s Inner Alchemy, with Commentary · 第十二首 面壁

Poem 12 · Facing the Wall

Poem 12 · Facing the Wall — the same for men and women

Safety note (translator): The commentary to this poem describes the ancients entering stone caves deep in the mountains and having the entrance sealed with a huge rock, or sitting long in a small shrine “without counting months or days,” as well as “no breathing through the nose, the pulse not beating,” and so on. These are all ancient records and must never be imitated. Chen Yingning himself says the method of sealing a cave “is not necessarily suitable today,” and even for sitting quietly in a small shrine he requires that “someone must keep watch day and night, guarding carefully against unforeseen dangers.” Prolonged motionless sitting carries health risks such as blood clots.

About This Chapter

万事皆云毕，凝然坐小龛。轻身乘紫气，静性濯清潭。炁混阴阳一，神同天地三。功完朝玉阙，长啸出烟岚。

The twelfth poem is about “facing the wall,” and is the same for men and women.

  • All affairs are said to be finished; one sits still and composed in a small shrine;
  • The body light, as if riding purple breath; the nature still, as if being washed in a clear pool;
  • The breath merges yin and yang into one; the spirit stands with Heaven and Earth as the third;
  • When the work is complete, one pays court at the Jade Palace, gives a long whistle, and goes forth beyond the mists.

Chen Yingning annotated only the first couplet, telling the origin of facing the wall and how the ancients practiced it. After that he wrote a very candid passage: “From the third line of this poem through to the last line of the fourteenth poem, all belongs to an inconceivable realm, so I have given no commentary… but from the time I began seeking the Dao until now, thirty years have passed, and I have truly never seen with my own eyes what the Yang Spirit looks like or how it comes out… having no actual experience, I dare not speak recklessly.” A scholar who had studied the alchemical Way for thirty years daring to say, “I have not seen it, and I dare not talk nonsense” — this honesty is itself worth learning from.

Chen Yingning's noteon §1
Original note (Chinese)

面壁之说，始于达摩。当梁武帝时，达摩止于嵩山少林寺，终日面壁而坐，九年如一日，故后世道家之修静功者，皆曰“面壁”。今之佛家反无此说，徒知念阿弥陀佛而已。

辟谷一关，既已经过，不但烟火食可以断绝，即芝芋之类亦可不食矣。古人修炼到此程度时，大半择深山石洞而居之，令人用巨石将洞口封没，以免野兽之侵害，及人事之烦扰，且不须守护者。但此法在今日未必相宜。

普通办法，即于山林清静之处，结茅屋数椽，以备同道栖止，然后用木做一小龛，其中仅容一人坐位，垫子宜软厚，前开一门，余三面须透空气而不进风，最好用竹丝编帘遮蔽，如轿上所用者。人坐其中，不计月日，直至阳神出壳，始庆功成，惟昼夜须有人守护，谨防意外之危险；中间若不愿久坐，暂时出来亦可。

此时身内已气满不思食，神全不思睡，其外状则鼻无呼吸，脉不跳动，遍体温暖，眼有神光，其身体内部之作用，自与凡夫不同，不可以常人之生理学强加判断。此等现象，今世尚不乏其人，余昔者固亲见之矣，然皆未知其有何等神通，是或丹经所谓“慧而不用”者乎？

今按自本首第三句以后，直至第十四首末句止，概属不可思议之境界，故未作注。当日某女士尚疑余故守秘密，致书相诘，奈余自访道至今，已三十年矣，实未曾目睹阳神是何形状，如何出法，即当日师传亦不及此，仅云“时至自知”，故对于出神以后种种作用，因无实验，不敢妄谈。且学者果能行面壁之功，何患不知出神之事？请稍安毋躁，以待他年亲证可乎？

The idea of facing the wall began with Bodhidharma. In the time of Emperor Wu of the Liang, Bodhidharma stayed at the Shaolin Monastery on Mount Song and sat facing the wall all day long, for nine years as if it were a single day; hence later Daoists who cultivate the work of stillness all call it “facing the wall.” Today’s Buddhists, on the contrary, no longer speak of it, knowing only how to recite the name of Amitābha.

Once the barrier of abstaining from grain has been passed, not only can cooked food be given up, but even zhi mushrooms, taro, and the like need not be eaten. When the ancients reached this degree of cultivation, most chose to live in stone caves deep in the mountains and had the entrance sealed with a huge rock, to avoid the harm of wild beasts and the disturbance of human affairs, and no one was needed to keep watch. But this method is not necessarily suitable today.

The ordinary way is this: in a quiet place among mountains and forests, build a few rooms of thatch to house fellow practitioners; then make a small shrine of wood, just large enough for one person to sit in, with a soft, thick cushion and a door opening in front; the other three sides must let air through but not wind — best screened with blinds woven of fine bamboo, like those used on sedan chairs. One sits within, without counting months or days, until the Yang Spirit leaves the shell, and only then celebrates the completion of the work; but someone must keep watch day and night, guarding carefully against unforeseen dangers; if in the meantime one does not wish to sit for long, one may come out for a while.

At this time the body is already full of breath and does not think of food, the spirit is complete and does not think of sleep; outwardly there is no breathing through the nose and the pulse does not beat, the whole body is warm, and the eyes have a divine light. The workings within such a person’s body naturally differ from those of ordinary people and cannot be forcibly judged by ordinary physiology. Such phenomena are still not lacking in the present age; I myself have indeed seen them with my own eyes in the past; but in no case did I know what spiritual powers they had — is this perhaps what the alchemical scriptures call “having wisdom and not using it”?

Note now: from the third line of this poem through to the last line of the fourteenth poem, all belongs to an inconceivable realm, so I have given no commentary. At the time, that lady even suspected me of deliberately keeping secrets and wrote to question me. But from the time I began seeking the Dao until now, thirty years have passed, and I have truly never seen with my own eyes what the Yang Spirit looks like or how it comes out; even my teacher’s transmission at the time did not reach this point, saying only, “When the time comes, you will know of yourself.” So as regards all the workings after the spirit goes out, having no actual experience, I dare not speak recklessly. Moreover, if students can truly carry out the work of facing the wall, why worry that they will not know about sending out the spirit? Please be patient and do not be hasty, and wait to verify it yourself in years to come — will that do?

Key Terms

  • Facing the wall: beginning with Bodhidharma’s nine years of facing the wall; Daoists call the work of stillness facing the wall, that is, “the nine years of facing the wall”; see the three transmutations.
  • Small shrine: a small wooden chamber just large enough for one person to sit.
  • Having wisdom and not using it: having wisdom without displaying or using it.
  • When the time comes, you will know of yourself: the words of Chen Yingning’s teacher’s transmission.
  • The spirit, with Heaven and Earth, makes three: the spirit standing alongside Heaven and Earth as the three powers.
  • Jade Palace: the palace in the heavens.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Chen Yingning’s honesty is the best lesson of this poem: what one has not personally verified, one says one has not verified. The alchemical scriptures should be read the same way — distinguish what you have experienced yourself from what is merely said in books.
  2. “Please be patient and do not be hasty, and wait to verify it yourself in years to come.” There is no need to be in a hurry to know about advanced realms; do the work in front of you well.
  3. Only when “all affairs are said to be finished” does one “sit still and composed in a small shrine.” The precondition for the ancients’ long sitting was that worldly affairs were settled. For us today, it is enough to arrange suitable times for quiet sitting within our lives; there is no need, nor should we seek, to go into long retreat.

Translator's Notes

  1. “Thirty years”: in his preface Chen Yingning says the commentary was drafted twenty years earlier, while here he says thirty years have passed since he began seeking the Dao; the two are counted from different starting points, and both are kept as in the original.
  2. Chen Yingning gives no commentary on the second to fourth couplets; we give only a literal translation. “Riding purple breath,” “paying court at the Jade Palace,” and the like are all imaginative Daoist descriptions of becoming an immortal.
