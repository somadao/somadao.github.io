Appendix: Sun Bu’er’s Poems on the Stages of Women’s Inner Alchemy, with Commentary · 篇首导读

Introduction to the Appendix

What this book is

This is the appendix of the whole book, and also the last part of the first book.

Sun Bu’er (1119–1182), styled the Lady of Purity and Stillness (Qingjing sanren), was the only woman among the “Seven Perfected of the North” of the Quanzhen school in the Jin dynasty, the wife of Ma Danyang (Ma Yu), and a native of Ninghai in Shandong (present-day Muping, Yantai). These fourteen regulated poems of five-character lines are traditionally attributed to her; they run from “Gathering in the Mind” to “Soaring Aloft,” describing the whole course of women’s inner alchemy step by step, hence the title Poems on the Stages of Women’s Inner Alchemy (Nügong neidan cidi shi 女功内丹次第诗).

The commentator, Chen Yingning (1880–1969), was a celebrated modern scholar of Daoism who promoted the “study of immortality” (xianxue 仙学) and served as president of the Chinese Daoist Association. In his early years, discussing the Dao with a certain lady, he explained these poems as occasion arose and mailed his explanations to her; twenty years later he put his old drafts in order, and they became this commentary.

Chen Yingning’s commentary has two features:

  1. One note per couplet. Each poem has eight lines, grouped in pairs into four couplets, and under each couplet he explains it word by word and line by line.
  2. He speaks very plainly, and very candidly. He uses Buddhist and Daoist texts to corroborate each other, and where he has not personally verified something he says outright, “having no actual experience, I dare not speak recklessly”; from the third line of the twelfth poem onward he gives essentially no commentary.

The notes in this part are Chen Yingning’s commentary, not Qiu Ling’s; Qiu Ling added only one editorial note each, at the beginning of the part and to the last poem.

Chen Yingning's original preface

按女丹诀传世者，现止数种，较之男丹经，未及百分之一，已憾其少，且大半是男子手笔，虽谈言微中，终非亲历之境，欲求女真自作者，除曹文逸之《灵源大道歌》而外，其惟此诗乎。原诗行世既久，无人作注，余往岁与某女士谈道之余，随时解释，邮寄赠之，距今已阅廿稔，旧稿零乱，杂于故纸堆中，难以卒读，爰为检出，重校一过，幸无大谬，遂录存之，固不敢自信尽得孙仙姑之玄义，但为后之读此诗者，辟一门径而已。注中容有未臻圆满处，因欲启诱初机，故卑之毋高论耳。

Note: of the formulas of women’s alchemy handed down, only a few kinds survive today, not even one hundredth of the scriptures of men’s alchemy; their scarcity is already regrettable, and moreover most of them were written by men, who, though sometimes hitting the mark, never had firsthand experience of the realms they describe. If one seeks something written by a female Perfected herself, apart from Cao Wenyi’s Song of the Great Way of the Numinous Source, perhaps there is only this set of poems. The original poems have long circulated, yet no one has written a commentary on them. Some years ago, in the course of discussing the Dao with a certain lady, I explained them as occasion arose and mailed my explanations to her as a gift; twenty years have now passed. The old drafts were in disorder, mixed among piles of old papers and hard to read through; so I picked them out and collated them once again, and finding, fortunately, no great errors, I copied them out to keep. I certainly do not presume to have fully grasped the mysterious meaning of the Immortal Lady Sun, but only to open a path for those who read these poems after me. If the notes are in places less than complete, it is because, wishing to guide beginners, I kept them simple and did not indulge in lofty discourse.

Qiu Ling's editorial note

原书有简介云，孙不二为北七真之首马阳真之妻，山东宁海人，金世宗二十二年，在洛阳苦修成道。元帝赐号“清静渊真顺德真人。”道派名清静派。

The original book has a brief introduction saying: Sun Bu’er was the wife of Ma Danyang, the first of the Seven Perfected of the North, and a native of Ninghai in Shandong; in the twenty-second year of the Dading reign of Emperor Shizong of the Jin (1182) she attained the Dao through hard cultivation in Luoyang. An emperor of the Yuan dynasty conferred on her the title “Perfected of Pure Stillness, Profound Truth, and Compliant Virtue.” The Daoist lineage she transmitted is called the Qingjing (Purity and Stillness) school.

What this part is about (in one sentence)

Fourteen poems, one stage per poem: the first three — gathering in the mind, nourishing the breath, practicing the work — are broadly the same for men and women; the fourth and fifth, “Beheading the Dragon” and “Nourishing the Elixir,” are beginning practices unique to women; from the sixth on — embryonic breathing, the fire tallies, receiving the medicine, refining the spirit, up to ingesting, abstaining from grain, facing the wall, sending out the spirit, and soaring aloft — is the ideal sequence from forming the elixir to becoming an immortal.

Reading tips

  1. The principles of the first three poems and of the sixth and seventh apply to both men and women, and are the closest to everyday practice. In particular, poem 1’s “by no means is it fixing on a certain part of the body and rigidly guarding it… if beginners rigidly guard one spot without knowing how to adapt, they will surely develop strange illnesses later,” and poem 3’s “if it were all written down on paper, it would turn a living method into a dead one… learning such a dead method only leads to illness,” are very clear-headed warnings from Chen Yingning.
  2. The fourth poem, “Beheading the Dragon,” is about using practice to stop menstruation. This is a central idea of ancient women’s alchemy, and we translate it according to its original meaning, but it must be said: in modern medicine, cessation of menstruation outside pregnancy and menopause is a symptom requiring medical examination. No practice should take the cessation of menstruation as its goal; if your menstruation changes noticeably during practice, please see a doctor first. Safety notes have been added to the relevant chapters.
  3. The eleventh poem, “Abstaining from Grain,” and the twelfth, “Facing the Wall,” describe fasting, sitting quietly in a sealed cave, and “no breathing through the nose, the pulse not beating” — all ancient records that must never be imitated.
  4. From the twelfth poem on, Chen Yingning himself says it is “an inconceivable realm,” and that he “has truly never seen with his own eyes what the Yang Spirit looks like”; of the last poem, “Soaring Aloft,” Qiu Ling says outright that it “should be regarded as feudal superstition.” Please read these as documents for understanding the whole picture of women’s alchemy.
  5. Chen Yingning’s commentary contains some statements bearing the stamp of their time, such as explaining the body’s emitting light in terms of “electricity” and “radium,” or asserting that cultivation can turn white hair black; we translate them as in the original and comment on them in the translator’s notes.
