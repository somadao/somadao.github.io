Poem 2 · Nourishing the Breath

Poem 2 · Nourishing the Breath — the same for men and women

Listen to this chapterENEnglish

About This Chapter

本是无为始，何期落后天。一声才出口，三寸已司权。况被尘劳耗，那堪疾病缠。子肥能益母，休道不迴旋。

The second poem is about “nourishing the breath,” and is the same for men and women. The first three couplets describe how a person is worn down step by step: originally the One Breath of Prior Heaven, without action, one falls unawares into Posterior Heaven; at birth, with one loud cry, the breathing of mouth and nose takes over the power of life; add to this the wear of worldly toil and the entanglement of illness. The last couplet points out the way out: when the breath of Posterior Heaven (the child) is fully nourished, the breath of Prior Heaven (the mother) arises of itself — do not say it cannot be turned back.

Chen Yingning’s description of breathing and life is very interesting: in childhood the inhalation is long and the exhalation short, and the body grows stronger day by day; in adulthood inhalation and exhalation are equal, and growth stops; after middle age the exhalation is long and the inhalation short, and the body weakens day by day; at the end of life there is only exhalation and no inhalation.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

本是无为始，何期落后天。

(Life) originally began in non-action — who would have thought it would fall into Posterior Heaven?

Chen Yingning's noteon §1
Original note (Chinese)

顺乎自然而无为者，先天之道；出于人力而有为者，后天之功。吾人当未生之初，本是浑元一气，无名无形，不觉而陷入于胎中，于是有身。既已有身，而大患随之矣。

What follows nature and is without action is the way of Prior Heaven; what comes from human effort and involves action is the work of Posterior Heaven. Before we were born, we were originally the primal One Breath, without name and without form; unawares we sank into the womb, and so came to have a body. Once we have a body, great affliction follows with it.

§2
Original (Chinese)

一声才出口，三寸已司权。

As soon as the first cry leaves the mouth, the three inches already hold power.

Chen Yingning's noteon §2
Original note (Chinese)

婴儿在胎，仅有胎息，鼻不呼吸。及至初出胎时，大哭一声，而外界之空气乘隙自鼻而入，于是后天呼吸遂操吾人生命之权。

其始也，吸入之气长，呼出之气短，而身体日壮；其继也，呼吸长短平均，身体之发育，及此而止。到中年以后，呼出之气渐长，吸入之气渐短，而身体日衰；临终时，仅有呼出之机，而无吸入之机，鼻息一停，命根遂断。

“三寸”者，指呼吸而言。

An infant in the womb has only embryonic breathing; the nose does not breathe. When it first leaves the womb, it gives one loud cry, and the air of the outside world seizes the gap and enters through the nose; from then on the breathing of Posterior Heaven holds the power over our life.

At the beginning, the inhaled breath is long and the exhaled breath short, and the body grows stronger day by day; next, inhalation and exhalation are equal in length, and the body’s growth stops at this point. After middle age, the exhaled breath gradually lengthens and the inhaled breath gradually shortens, and the body weakens day by day; at the end of life there is only the function of exhaling and no function of inhaling; once the breath through the nose stops, the root of life is cut off.

“The three inches” refers to breathing.

§3
Original (Chinese)

况被尘劳耗，那堪疾病缠。

What is more, worn away by worldly toil — how can one bear the entanglement of illness?

Chen Yingning's noteon §3
Original note (Chinese)

上言人身生死之常理，此言人之自贼其身也。色、声、香、味、触、法，是名“六尘”。劳心、劳力，皆谓之“劳”。

吾人自然之寿命，本为甚短，纵不加以戕贼，在今世亦少有能过百岁者。况尘劳与疾病，皆足以伤竭人之元气，使不得尽其天年，故多有寿命未终而中途夭折者。（或问：“六尘之说，乃释氏语，何故引以注丹经？”答曰：“非我之咎，原诗已喜用佛家名词，如生灭、如真如、如舍利子等，皆非道家所本有者，不引佛典，何能作注？”）

The previous couplet speaks of the ordinary principle of birth and death in the human body; this one speaks of how people harm their own bodies. Form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental objects are called the “six dusts.” Laboring the mind and laboring the body are both called “toil.”

Our natural life span is in fact very short; even if we did not harm it, in this age few live beyond a hundred years. How much more so when worldly toil and illness are both enough to injure and exhaust a person’s original breath, so that one cannot live out one’s natural years; hence many die early, midway, before their life span is over. (Someone asked: “The idea of the six dusts is Buddhist; why quote it to annotate an alchemical scripture?” I answered: “That is not my fault. The original poems themselves like to use Buddhist terms, such as arising and ceasing, suchness, śarīra, and so on, none of which Daoism originally had; without quoting Buddhist texts, how could I write the commentary?”)

§4
Original (Chinese)

子肥能益母，休道不迴旋。

When the child is plump, it can benefit the mother — do not say it cannot turn back.

Chen Yingning's noteon §4
Original note (Chinese)

“子”者，后天气；“母”者，先天气。后天气，丹道喻之为“水”；先天气，丹道喻之为“金”。按五行之说，金能生水，是先天变为后天也。丹道重在逆转造化，使水反生金，是由后天返还先天也。

昔人谓为九转还丹，九乃阳数之极，又为金之成数，故曰九还，非限定转九次也。先天难于捉摸，必从后天工夫下手，方可返到先天。后天气培养充足，则先天气自然发生，故曰子肥能益母。

“迴旋”者，即返还逆转之谓。

“The child” is the breath of Posterior Heaven; “the mother” is the breath of Prior Heaven. The alchemical Way likens the breath of Posterior Heaven to “water” and the breath of Prior Heaven to “metal.” According to the theory of the five phases, metal produces water; this is Prior Heaven changing into Posterior Heaven. The alchemical Way puts its emphasis on reversing creation, making water produce metal in turn; this is returning from Posterior Heaven to Prior Heaven.

The ancients called it the “ninefold-turned Returned Elixir.” Nine is the extreme of the yang numbers and also the completing number of metal, hence “ninefold return”; it does not stipulate turning nine times. Prior Heaven is hard to grasp; one must begin from the work of Posterior Heaven before one can return to Prior Heaven. When the breath of Posterior Heaven has been fully cultivated, the breath of Prior Heaven naturally arises; hence it is said, “when the child is plump, it can benefit the mother.”

“Turning back” (huixuan 迴旋) means returning and reversing.

Key Terms

  • Prior Heaven and Posterior Heaven: what follows nature and is without action is the way of Prior Heaven; what comes from human effort and involves action is the work of Posterior Heaven.
  • Primal One Breath: the One Breath of Prior Heaven before birth; see One Breath of Prior Heaven.
  • Three inches: refers to breathing; see ordinary breath.
  • Embryonic breathing: the infant’s way of breathing in the womb; see True Breath.
  • Six dusts: form, sound, smell, taste, touch, mental objects (a Buddhist term).
  • Child, mother: the breath of Posterior Heaven and the breath of Prior Heaven; water and metal.
  • Ninefold-turned Returned Elixir: nine is the extreme of the yang numbers and the completing number of metal; it does not stipulate turning nine times.
  • Turning back: returning and reversing; see the method of reversal.

Putting It into Practice

  1. “Prior Heaven is hard to grasp; one must begin from the work of Posterior Heaven.” This is the most practical line in the whole poem: do not set out at the start to look for some “One Breath of Prior Heaven”; begin honestly from Posterior Heaven — evening out the breath, looking after the body, wasting less spirit.
  2. Worldly toil and illness both consume the original breath. Nourishing the breath means first of all not consuming it: be moderate in mental and physical labor, and if you are ill, treat the illness first.
  3. Chen Yingning’s observations about the length of inhalation and exhalation can serve as a reference: natural, deep, steady breathing is one sign of good physical condition. But do not deliberately lengthen the inhalation or shorten the exhalation.

Translator's Notes

  1. “Nine is the extreme of the yang numbers and also the completing number of metal”: according to the numbers of the River Chart, Heaven’s one produces water and Earth’s six completes it; Earth’s four produces metal and Heaven’s nine completes it; nine is thus the completing number of metal.
  2. “Turning back” (迴旋): the same as 回旋.
