Appendix: Sun Bu’er’s Poems on the Stages of Women’s Inner Alchemy, with Commentary · 第六首 胎息

Poem 6 · Embryonic Breathing

Poem 6 · Embryonic Breathing — the same for men and women

About This Chapter

要得丹成速，先将幻境除。心心守灵药，息息返乾初。炁复通三岛，神忘合太虚。若来与若去，无处不真如。

The sixth poem is about “embryonic breathing,” and is the same for men and women.

  • If you want the elixir to form quickly, first remove illusory realms;
  • With every thought guard the numinous medicine (the True Breath), with every breath return to the beginning of Qian (true emptiness) — mind and breath relying on each other for a long time is embryonic breathing;
  • The breath returns and passes through the three Cinnabar Fields; the spirit forgets everything and unites with the Great Void;
  • Coming and going, there is nowhere that is not suchness.

Chen Yingning gives two successive explanations of “illusory realms,” and the second is especially important: illusory realms are all kinds of scenes that the yin demons in the body take the opportunity to stir up secretly — some make one infatuated, some frightened, some make one mistakenly believe one has gained spiritual powers, even to the point of the spirit and consciousness becoming confused, injuring one’s own limbs, and falsely claiming to have met a sage. All these must be swept away. He also says something very thought-provoking: those who have studied the Dao for decades without any demonic obstacles are those who have never truly practiced.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

要得丹成速，先将幻境除。

If you want the elixir to form quickly, first remove illusory realms.

Chen Yingning's noteon §1
Original note (Chinese)

“幻境”即世间一切困人之环境，窘迫万状，牵缠不休，至死未由自拔。故修道者，必须设法断绝尘缘，然后方收速效。世有学道数十年，毫无进步者，皆未脱俗累之故。

今按：前解虽是，然非幻境本义，因对初学说法，故浅言之耳。其实所谓“幻境”者，乃身中阴魔乘机窃发之种种景象，或动人爱恋，或使人恐怖，或起嗔恨，或感悲伤，或令人误认为神通，或引人错走入邪路，甚至神识昏迷，自残肢体，偶有见闻，妄称遇圣，凡此等类，皆是幻境，必宜扫除。不经法眼，终难辨别，所以学者要从师也。世有学道数十年，毫无魔障者，皆未曾实行之故。

“Illusory realms” are all the circumstances in the world that trap people — distressed in ten thousand ways, entangled without end, unable to free themselves until death. So those who cultivate the Dao must find a way to cut off worldly ties; only then will they obtain quick results. Those in the world who study the Dao for decades without any progress have all failed to shake off worldly encumbrances.

Note now: although the preceding explanation is correct, it is not the original meaning of “illusory realms”; because I was speaking to beginners, I spoke simply. In fact the so-called “illusory realms” are all kinds of scenes that the yin demons in the body take the opportunity to stir up secretly: some arouse infatuation, some cause fear, some give rise to anger and hatred, some bring grief, some make one mistakenly believe one has gained spiritual powers, some lead one astray onto a wrong path, even to the point of the spirit and consciousness becoming confused and injuring one’s own limbs; having by chance seen or heard something, one falsely claims to have met a sage — all of this kind are illusory realms and must be swept away. Without passing under the eye of the Dharma, they are hard to distinguish in the end; that is why students must follow a teacher. Those in the world who have studied the Dao for decades without any demonic obstacles are those who have never truly practiced.

§2
Original (Chinese)

心心守灵药，息息返乾初。

With every thought guard the numinous medicine; with every breath return to the beginning of Qian.

Chen Yingning's noteon §2
Original note (Chinese)

“灵药”即是妙有，妙有即是真息。“心心守灵药”者，心依于息也。“乾初”即是真空，真空即是道心。“息息返乾初”者，息依于心也。初学修炼，虽能心息相依，然为时不久，又复分离。至于胎息时，则心心息息长相依也。

“乾初”者，指乾卦未画之初，非谓乾之初爻。《明道篇》云：“观乾未画是何形，一画才成万象生。”然则乾初者，岂非太极阴阳未判之象乎？

“The numinous medicine” is wondrous existence, and wondrous existence is the True Breath. “With every thought guard the numinous medicine” means the mind relying on the breath. “The beginning of Qian” is true emptiness, and true emptiness is the mind of the Dao. “With every breath return to the beginning of Qian” means the breath relying on the mind. Those beginning to cultivate, though they can achieve mind and breath relying on each other, do so only for a short time before they separate again. When it comes to embryonic breathing, mind and breath, thought after thought and breath after breath, rely on each other for a long time.

“The beginning of Qian” refers to before the hexagram Qian was drawn, not to the first line of Qian. The Treatise on Illuminating the Dao says: “Look: before Qian was drawn, what shape was it? As soon as one stroke is drawn, the myriad images are born.” So is not the beginning of Qian the image of the Great Ultimate before yin and yang had divided?

§3
Original (Chinese)

炁复通三岛，神忘合太虚。

The breath returns and passes through the three isles; the spirit forgets and unites with the Great Void.

Chen Yingning's noteon §3
Original note (Chinese)

“三岛”者，比喻人身上中下三丹田。老子曰：“归根曰静，静曰复命。”即“炁复”之义。人身本自太虚中来，一落色相，则有障碍，而不能与太虚相合。惟有道者，能忘一切色相，色相既除，则与太虚相合矣。

天隐子者，道家之流也，其言曰：“人之修真，不能顿悟，必须渐而行之。一曰斋戒，澡身虚心。二曰安处，深居静室。三曰存想，收心复性。四曰坐忘，遗形忘我。五曰神解，万法通神。”全篇约千余言，未能毕录，此其纲领也。又司马子微《坐忘论》亦可读。此等工夫甚难，非朝夕可至，然有志者事竟成，惟视人之毅力如何耳。

“The three isles” are a metaphor for the upper, middle, and lower Cinnabar Fields of the human body. Laozi said: “Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called returning to life.” This is the meaning of “the breath returns.” The human body originally came from the Great Void; once it falls into form and appearance, there are obstructions and it cannot unite with the Great Void. Only those who have the Dao can forget all form and appearance; once form and appearance are removed, one unites with the Great Void.

Tianyinzi was of the Daoist school, and he said: “In cultivating reality, people cannot awaken suddenly; they must proceed gradually. The first is called fasting and abstinence: bathe the body and empty the mind. The second is called dwelling in peace: live secluded in a quiet room. The third is called visualization: gather in the mind and restore the nature. The fourth is called sitting in forgetfulness: leave the form behind and forget the self. The fifth is called spiritual release: the myriad methods all penetrated by the spirit.” The whole piece is about a thousand-odd characters and cannot all be copied here; this is its outline. Sima Ziwei’s Treatise on Sitting in Forgetfulness is also worth reading. Such work is very difficult and cannot be reached in a day; but where there is a will there is a way — it depends only on a person’s perseverance.

§4
Original (Chinese)

若来与若去，无处不真如。

As if coming and as if going, there is nowhere that is not suchness.

Chen Yingning's noteon §4
Original note (Chinese)

“真如”者，佛家之名词。佛典云：“如来藏含有二义，一为生灭门，一为真如门。心无生灭，即真如矣；若背真如，即生灭矣。”又云：“真谓真实非虚妄；如谓如常无变易。”

“Suchness” (zhenru 真如) is a Buddhist term. The Buddhist scriptures say: “The womb of the Thus-Come contains two meanings: one is the gate of arising and ceasing, the other the gate of suchness. When the mind has no arising and ceasing, that is suchness; if one turns away from suchness, that is arising and ceasing.” They also say: “Zhen means real and not false; ru means constant and unchanging.”

Key Terms

  • Illusory realms: in the shallow sense, the worldly circumstances that trap people; in the original sense, all kinds of scenes secretly stirred up by the yin demons in the body; see realm of yin / realm of the aggregates.
  • Eye of the Dharma: the discernment that can tell the true from the illusory, meaning an enlightened teacher.
  • Numinous medicine, wondrous existence: that is, the True Breath.
  • Beginning of Qian, true emptiness, mind of the Dao: before the hexagram Qian was drawn, the image of the Great Ultimate before yin and yang divided.
  • Mind and breath relying on each other: the mind relying on the breath, the breath relying on the mind; relying on each other for long is embryonic breathing.
  • Three isles: the upper, middle, and lower Cinnabar Fields.
  • “Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called returning to life”: Laozi, chapter 16, the meaning of “the breath returns.”
  • Form and appearance (sexiang 色相): everything that has form and appearance, a Buddhist term.
  • Tianyinzi’s five gradual gates: fasting and abstinence, dwelling in peace, visualization, sitting in forgetfulness, spiritual release.
  • Suchness: a Buddhist term, real and not false, constant and unchanging; when the mind has no arising and ceasing, that is suchness.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Chen Yingning’s second explanation of “illusory realms” is a very important safety checklist: if in quiet sitting there appear strong infatuation, fear, anger, or grief, a feeling that you have gained spiritual powers, visions of “sages,” or even confusion of the spirit and consciousness — none of these is an achievement; they are illusory realms to be swept away. If such experiences persist or affect your normal life, stop practicing and seek professional help.
  2. “Without passing under the eye of the Dharma, they are hard to distinguish in the end; that is why students must follow a teacher.” When practicing alone and you meet an experience you are unsure about, it is better to treat it as an illusory realm and put it down.
  3. For mind and breath relying on each other, first seek a short while, then seek long. For beginners it is normal for mind and breath to rely on each other for a while and then separate; there is no need to hurry; lengthen it slowly.
  4. Tianyinzi’s five steps are a good sequence: first fasting and abstinence (cleanliness of body and mind), then dwelling in peace (having a quiet place), then visualization and sitting in forgetfulness, and finally spiritual release. Do not skip steps.

Translator's Notes

  1. The Treatise on Illuminating the Dao (Mingdao pian 明道篇): an alchemical book by Wang Weiyi of the Yuan dynasty.
  2. Tianyinzi: the author of the Tang Daoist book Tianyinzi; by one account, Sima Chengzhen (Ziwei) himself.
  3. Sima Ziwei: the Tang Daoist Sima Chengzhen, author of the Treatise on Sitting in Forgetfulness.
  4. The passage “the womb of the Thus-Come contains two meanings”: its idea comes from the “one mind, two gates” (the gate of the mind as suchness, the gate of the mind as arising and ceasing) of the Awakening of Faith in the Mahāyāna.
