1 · Beginning: Stopping Thoughts and Regulating the Mind — why women’s practice adds massage

About This Chapter

The first step of women’s practice is to stop thoughts, and the next is to regulate the mind; only when thoughts have stopped and the mind is regulated can massage be done. Why does women’s practice add massage? Because women take blood as their foundation and their nature tends toward yin, and yin likes coolness; without massage to gently move the breath, one easily sinks into pure yin; yin is cool, and coolness becomes like ice, brewing illnesses such as congealed phlegm and stagnant blood, and the work becomes hard to do. But one must begin with stopping thoughts and regulating the mind: women belong to Kun, and Kun hides a true fire; when the fire lies hidden, it is auspicious; if the fire breaks out and scorches metal, and one sets things in motion without first regulating the mind, there is the danger of “the rooster flying up to heaven.” Qiu Ling’s note explains this image vividly: the “rooster” is a bird that returns to roost at dusk; if it is pressed too hard it flies up into the sky — a metaphor for dry fire forced to rush upward.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

女功进步，初则止念，继则调心。念止心调，便可从事按摩矣！法忌避炎就凉。盖女以血为本者，其性偏阴，阴性喜凉，不假按摩以微行气机，则易沦入纯阴。阴则凉，凉则冰如，不加之以动运，酿成痰凝血瘀等病，而功难行矣！然须从止念调心始。女属坤，而坤藏真火，火伏则吉；火发烁金，不调而运。金遭火逼，则有“翰音登天”之象。故女修诀从止念调心始。止念调心，功不厌多，亦不忌久行者，静中有动也。

In women’s practice, the first step is to stop thoughts, and the next is to regulate the mind. When thoughts have stopped and the mind is regulated, one can then engage in massage! The method forbids escaping warmth to seek coolness. For women take blood as their foundation; their nature tends toward yin, and yin likes coolness; without massage to move the breath slightly, they easily sink into pure yin. Yin is cool, and coolness becomes like ice; if it is not set in motion, it brews illnesses such as congealed phlegm and stagnant blood, and the work becomes hard to do! Yet one must begin with stopping thoughts and regulating the mind. Women belong to Kun, and Kun hides a true fire; when the fire lies hidden, it is auspicious. If the fire breaks out and scorches metal, and one sets things in motion without regulating the mind, then metal is pressed by fire, and there is the image of “the rooster flying up to heaven.” Therefore the formula of women’s cultivation begins with stopping thoughts and regulating the mind. Of stopping thoughts and regulating the mind one can never do too much, nor is there any harm in doing it for a long time, for there is movement within its stillness.

Qiu Ling's noteon §1
Original note (Chinese)

入手止念调心，与男功相同。《唱道真言》说：“炼丹先要炼心。炼心之法，以去闲思妄想为清净法门。”此即止调念调心之意。此功与男功不同者，乃根据女性偏阴喜凉之特质，于念止心调之后续行按摩，以行气机。初学往往有机燥生景象，盖静则阴凝，不动则阳郁，切勿求得凉快于一时，必须加功用按摩疏通气机，则燥自释。但按摩不宜过猛，过猛则有燥金（肺）之弊，故有“翰音登天”之戒。“翰音”喻下午五时至七时栖息之禽，逼之甚则飞天，喻燥火被逼上冲。

Beginning with stopping thoughts and regulating the mind is the same as in men’s practice. True Words for Chanting the Dao says: “To refine the elixir, first refine the mind. The method of refining the mind takes the removal of idle thoughts and deluded fancies as the gate of purity and stillness.” This is the meaning of stopping thoughts and regulating the mind. What differs from men’s practice is that, in accordance with women’s characteristic tendency toward yin and liking for coolness, after thoughts have stopped and the mind is regulated, one goes on to massage in order to move the breath. Beginners often experience a dry heat in the movement of breath, because in stillness yin congeals and without movement yang is pent up. On no account seek momentary coolness; one must add the work of massage to free the movement of breath, and the dryness will then ease by itself. But massage should not be too vigorous; if it is too vigorous, there is the harm of drying the metal (the lungs) — hence the warning about “the rooster flying up to heaven.” The “rooster” is a metaphor for a bird that returns to roost between five and seven in the evening; if it is pressed too hard, it flies up into the sky — a metaphor for dry fire forced to rush upward.

Key Terms

  • First step (jinbu 进步): in this section, the step of beginning, not “progress.”
  • Stopping thoughts, regulating the mind: the two beginning steps of women’s practice, the same as in men’s practice.
  • Massage: a step particular to women’s practice, used to move the breath slightly.
  • Escaping warmth to seek coolness: forbidden in women’s practice.
  • Pure yin: excessive yin, cool and congealed.
  • Congealed phlegm and stagnant blood: Chinese medical terms for turbid phlegm gathering and blood flow stagnating.
  • Kun hides a true fire: women belong to Kun, and within Kun there is a true fire.
  • The fire breaking out and scorching metal: the fire rising and damaging the metal (the lungs).
  • The rooster flying up to heaven: from the top line of the hexagram Zhongfu in the Book of Changes, “the rooster’s cry rises up to heaven”; Qiu Ling’s note: a metaphor for dry fire forced to rush upward.
  • Movement within stillness: stopping thoughts and regulating the mind is stillness, yet within it the breath moves.

Putting It into Practice

  1. First stop thoughts, then regulate the mind, and only then massage: the order cannot be reversed. If you massage or move the breath before the mind has become still, “the fire breaks out and scorches metal” all too easily.
  2. Do not seek coolness: when women become still in practice, they easily get cold hands and feet, or on the contrary a dry heat. Qiu Ling’s note says: when there is dry heat, do not seek momentary coolness; free the movement of breath with massage, and the dryness will ease by itself.
  3. Massage should be gentle: “it should not be too vigorous”; too much vigor dries the lungs, and one may feel the breath rushing upward.
  4. There is no harm in doing much stopping of thoughts and regulating of the mind: “one can never do too much, nor is there any harm in doing it for a long time” — this is the safest part; doing much of it, and for a long time, is good.

Translator's Notes

  1. “The first step” (nügong jinbu 女功进步): jinbu here does not mean “progress” as in modern Chinese, but “entering the steps, beginning,” that is, the first step, matching the section title “Beginning: Stopping Thoughts and Regulating the Mind.”
  2. In Qiu Ling’s note, the phrase 此即止调念调心之意 seems to contain an extra character; it should read 此即止念调心之意, “this is the meaning of stopping thoughts and regulating the mind.”
  3. “Rooster” (hanyin 翰音): originally a name for the chicken (Book of Rites: “the chicken is called hanyin”); Qiu Ling’s note explains it as a bird that roosts at dusk, a metaphor for dry fire rushing upward, and the translation follows his explanation.
