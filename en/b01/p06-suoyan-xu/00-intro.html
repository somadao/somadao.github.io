What this book is

Further Fragmentary Words (Suoyan xu 琐言续) was dictated by Shen Taixu (Shen Zhenyang) of the Qing dynasty, recorded by Min Xiaogen, edited by Xue Xinxiang and published by Lu Liuxi. Shen Taixu had earlier dictated another book, Fragmentary Words by the River (Heshang suoyan 河上琐言); hence this one is called Further Fragmentary Words. The title “Living Formulas for Practice in the Four Seasons” and the subheadings of each section were added by Qiu Ling.

The preceding parts mostly teach the “constant method” — a fixed order of practice. This part teaches the “living method”: how the same work should change in winter and spring and in summer and autumn; what the atmosphere of each of the twelve double-hours of the day is; how to begin at the zi and wu hours; and how to adjust when the body is ill. Qiu Ling says it “has quite original insights.”

Qiu Ling's editorial note

《琐言续》为清代沈太虚（沈真扬）口授，闵小艮述，薛心香订，陆柳溪刊。沈太虚原另有口授《河上琐言》一书，故此书定名《琐言续》。现用书名《四时行功活诀》及文内小题均乃编注者所加。

本书论述道教北宗龙门气功在四季、一日二十四时特别子午时以及身患疾病时的活用，颇有独到之处，故介绍给读者。

Further Fragmentary Words was dictated by Shen Taixu (Shen Zhenyang) of the Qing dynasty, recorded by Min Xiaogen, edited by Xue Xinxiang and published by Lu Liuxi. Shen Taixu had also dictated another book, Fragmentary Words by the River, so this book was titled Further Fragmentary Words. The title now used, “Living Formulas for Practice in the Four Seasons,” and the subheadings in the text were all added by me (Qiu Ling).

This book discusses the flexible use of the qigong of the Longmen lineage of the Northern School of Daoism in the four seasons and the twenty-four hours of the day — especially the zi and wu hours — and when the body is ill. It has quite original insights, and so I present it to readers.

What this part is about (in one sentence)

Cultivation has only three general rules: keep the body upright, keep the mind empty, keep the intention genuine. On top of these three, adjust rising and descending, releasing and gathering flexibly according to the season, the hour and the state of the body — in winter and spring emphasize “passing through behind” (the breath rising along the back), in summer and autumn “opening in front” (the breath descending along the front).

Reading tips

  1. Sections 1 and 8 echo each other from beginning to end and form the outline of the whole part. The three sentences “keep the body upright, keep the mind empty, keep the intention genuine” suit everyone who sits in stillness. We suggest reading Sections 1 and 8 first.
  2. Section 4, “Methods for Treating Illness,” is a record of the ancients’ experience and cannot replace medical care. The accumulated cold, much phlegm, inner heat, hot blood and so on mentioned in the text are concepts of Chinese medicine and of the alchemists, and do not correspond one to one with modern medical diagnoses. If you are ill, please see a doctor first.
  3. This part contains quite a few forceful operations: holding the breath, “contracting the gen” (contracting as if holding back urine and stool), “raising the buttocks,” breaking through the passes with the intention, seeking heavy sweating, and so on. In his note to Section 7, Qiu Ling says plainly that contracting the gen is “like holding back urine and stool.” Practice these methods only under the guidance of an experienced teacher; do not force them on your own.
  4. Sections 5 and 6 speak of many “numinous states” and “transformed scenes.” The text repeatedly stresses “beware of clinging,” “set it aside and do not cling,” “beware of alarm and doubt” — this is the most important point in reading these two sections.
  5. The text is written from the point of view of a male practitioner (“the male root,” “the outer kidneys”); for women’s alchemy, see the next part, The Precious Raft of the Women’s School of Dual Cultivation.
