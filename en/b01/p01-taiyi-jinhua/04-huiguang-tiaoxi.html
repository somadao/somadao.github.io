Chapter 4 · Reverting the Light and Regulating the Breath

Chapter 4 · Reverting the Light and Regulating the Breath — mind and breath relying on each other; listening to the breath you cannot hear

About This Chapter

Reverting the Light on its own is not enough: as soon as beginners sit down, they either grow drowsy or become distracted. The remedy this chapter offers is regulating the breath (tiaoxi 调息). The mind and the breath are fundamentally one — each thought corresponds to a breath — so rather than forcing thoughts down, it is better to entrust the mind to the breath and let mind and breath rely on each other. The concrete method is called “listening to the breath”: what you listen to is the breath’s silence. When the breath becomes fine enough, it suddenly seems to stop — and that is when the True Breath (zhenxi 真息) appears. The chapter also analyzes in detail the difference between torpor and distraction and how to cure each.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

《宗旨》只要纯心行去，不求验而验自至。大约初机病痛，昏沉、散乱二种尽之。却此机窍，无过寄心于息。息者，自心也。自心为息。心一动而即有气。气本心之所化也。吾人动念至速，霎顷起一妄念，即一呼吸应之。故内呼吸与外呼吸如声响之相应。一日有几万息，即有几万妄念。神明漏尽，如木槁灰死矣。然则欲无念乎？不能无念也。欲无息乎？不能无息也。莫若即其病而为药，则心息相依是已。故回光必兼之调息。此法全用耳光。一是目光，一是耳光。目光者，外日月交光也。耳光者，内日月交精也。然精即光之凝定处，同出而异名也。故聪明总一灵光而已。

With this Secret, you need only practice wholeheartedly; do not seek results, and results will come by themselves. Broadly speaking, the troubles of beginners are all covered by two: torpor and distraction. The key to getting rid of them is nothing other than entrusting the mind to the breath. The character for breath, xi 息, is made of zi 自 (“self”) above and xin 心 (“mind”) below: one’s own mind is the breath. As soon as the mind moves, there is breath; breath is originally a transformation of the mind. Our thoughts move extremely fast: in an instant a deluded thought arises, and a breath answers it. So the inner breathing (our thoughts) and the outer breathing answer each other like a sound and its echo. In a day there are tens of thousands of breaths, and so tens of thousands of deluded thoughts. In this way the spiritual clarity leaks away completely, and one becomes like dead wood and cold ashes. Then should one try to have no thoughts? One cannot have no thoughts. Should one try to have no breath? One cannot have no breath. Better to take the illness itself as the medicine — that is, to let mind and breath rely on each other. So Reverting the Light must go together with regulating the breath. This method relies entirely on the “light of the ears.” There is the light of the eyes and the light of the ears. The light of the eyes is the outer sun and moon — the two eyes — joining their light. The light of the ears is the inner sun and moon joining their essence. But essence is simply where the light congeals and settles; they come from the same source and differ only in name. So keen hearing and clear sight are, in the end, one and the same spiritual light.

§2
Original (Chinese)

坐时用目垂帘后，定个准则，便放下。然竟放又恐不能，即存心于听息。息之出入，不可使耳闻，听惟听其无声。一有声，即粗浮而不入细。当耐心，轻轻微微，愈放愈微，愈微愈静，久之忽然微者遽断，此则真息现在，而心体可识矣！盖心细则息细，心一则动气也。息细则心细，气一则动心也。定心必先养气者，亦以心无处入手，故缘气为之端倪，所谓纯气之守也。

When you sit, after lowering the curtains of the eyes, set the reference and then let it go. But if you fear you cannot let go completely at once, keep the mind on listening to the breath. Do not let the ears hear the breath going in and out; listen only to its silence. As soon as there is a sound, the breath is coarse and shallow and does not enter the subtle. Be patient: lightly, faintly, the more you let go the fainter it gets, the fainter the stiller. After a long time, the faint breath suddenly stops altogether — then the True Breath is present, and the substance of the mind can be recognized! For when the mind is fine, the breath is fine; when the mind is unified, it moves the breath. Conversely, when the breath is fine, the mind is fine; when the breath is unified, it moves the mind. To settle the mind one must first nourish the breath, because there is nowhere to take hold of the mind directly; so one uses the breath as the starting thread. This is what Zhuangzi calls “guarding the pure breath.”

§3
Original (Chinese)

散乱者神驰也，昏沉者神未清也。散乱易治，昏沉难医。譬之病焉，有痛有痒者，药之可也。昏沉则麻木不仁之症也。散者可以收之，乱者可以整之，若昏沉，则蠢蠢焉，冥冥焉。散乱尚有方所，至昏沉全是魄用事也。散乱尚有魂在，至昏沉则纯阴为主矣。

Distraction is the spirit racing off; torpor is the spirit not yet clear. Distraction is easy to cure; torpor is hard to treat. Compare it to illness: a disease that hurts or itches can be treated with medicine, but torpor is a numbness that feels nothing. What is scattered can be gathered, what is disordered can be put in order; but torpor is just dull and murky. Distraction at least has a direction you can find; in torpor, the po soul is entirely in charge. In distraction the hun soul is still present; in torpor pure yin has taken over.

§4
Original (Chinese)

静坐时欲睡去，便是昏沉。却昏沉，只有调息。息即口鼻出入之息，虽非真息，而真息之出入，亦于此寄焉。凡坐须要静心纯气。心何以静？用在息上。息之出入，惟心自知，不可使耳闻。不闻则细，细则清；闻则粗，粗则浊，浊则昏沉而欲睡，自然之理也。虽然，心用在息上，又要善会，亦是不用之用，只要微微照听可耳。

Wanting to fall asleep during quiet sitting — that is torpor. The only way to get rid of torpor is to regulate the breath. The “breath” here is the breath going in and out of the mouth and nose. It is not the True Breath, but the True Breath also goes in and out by way of it. Whenever you sit, you must still the mind and purify the breath. How is the mind stilled? By applying it to the breath. Let only the mind itself know the breath going in and out; do not let the ears hear it. When it is not heard, it is fine; when it is fine, it is clear. When it is heard, it is coarse; when it is coarse, it is turbid; when it is turbid, you become drowsy and want to sleep. That is the natural course of things. Even so, applying the mind to the breath must be understood well: it is a “use without using” — just shine on it and listen to it, very faintly.

§5
Original (Chinese)

何谓照？即眼光自照，目惟内视而不外视。不外视而惺然者，即内视也，非实有内视。何谓听？即耳光自听，耳惟内听而不外听。听者听其无声，视者视其无形。目不外视，耳不外听，则闭而欲内驰。惟内视内听，则既不外肆，又不内驰，而中不昏沉矣。此即日月交精交光者也。

What is “shining”? The light of the eyes shines on itself: the eyes look only inward, not outward. Not looking outward and yet being wide awake — that is “inner seeing”; there is no actual act of looking inward. What is “listening”? The light of the ears listens to itself: the ears listen only inward, not outward. Listening listens to the soundless; seeing sees the formless. If the eyes simply do not look outward and the ears simply do not listen outward, the doors are merely shut and the mind tends to run about inside. Only with inner seeing and inner listening does it neither spill outward nor run about inward, and in between there is no torpor. This is the sun and moon joining their essence and joining their light.

Qiu Ling's noteon the whole chapter
Original note (Chinese)

此章结合回光，论《庄子·人间世篇》所载庄子听息法之具体运用。庄子说：“颜回曰：‘敢问心斋。‘仲尼曰：‘若一志，无听之以耳而听之以心，无听之以心而听之以气！听止于耳，心止于符。气也者，虚而待物者也。唯道集虚。虚者，心斋也’。”意思是说，练功时意念要专一，不是用耳听呼吸之声音，而是用心听鼻中之气息。不是用心听鼻中之气息，而是心气打成一片，渐渐进入混沌的境界，听也停止了，于是心息相依，神气合一（即“心止于符”，“符”作“神”解），真息出现，最后进入虚无的境界，虚无就是心斋。

本章所述“息之出入，不可使耳闻，惟听其无声”，“惟心自知”，去昏沉、散乱之法在于内视内听，实出于庄子听息法，此法乃道家学说的优秀遗产之一。

This chapter, in connection with Reverting the Light, discusses the practical use of Zhuangzi’s method of listening to the breath, recorded in the chapter “In the World of Men” of the Zhuangzi. Zhuangzi writes: Yan Hui said, “May I ask what the fasting of the mind is?” Confucius said, “Unify your will. Do not listen with your ears, but listen with your mind; do not listen with your mind, but listen with your breath! The ears stop at hearing sounds; the mind stops at matching things. The breath is empty and receives all things. The Dao gathers only in emptiness. This emptiness is the fasting of the mind.” Qiu Ling explains: this means that in practice the intention must be unified. You do not listen with your ears to the sound of your breathing, but listen with your mind to the breath in the nose; going further, you do not even listen with your mind to the breath in the nose, but mind and breath become one piece and gradually enter a state of undifferentiated chaos, until even “listening” stops. Then mind and breath rely on each other and spirit and breath unite — this is “the mind stops at matching” (xin zhi yu fu 心止于符, where fu, “matching,” can be understood as “spirit”) — the True Breath appears, and finally one enters the state of emptiness and non-being. Emptiness and non-being are the fasting of the mind.

What this chapter says — “do not let the ears hear the breath going in and out; listen only to its silence,” “let only the mind itself know it” — and its method of removing torpor and distraction through inner seeing and inner listening actually derive from Zhuangzi’s method of listening to the breath. This method is one of the finest legacies of Daoist thought.

Key Terms

  • regulating the breath / mind and breath relying on each other / listening to the breath: the core method of this chapter.
  • The character xi 息: xi (breath) = zi 自 (self) + xin 心 (mind); “one’s own mind is the breath.”
  • Inner breathing and outer breathing: in this chapter, “inner breathing” means our thoughts and “outer breathing” the breath of the mouth and nose; the two answer each other like sound and echo. Note that in other alchemical books “inner breathing” usually means the embryonic breathing of the Cinnabar Field — a different meaning.
  • Light of the eyes, light of the ears: the light of the eyes is “the outer sun and moon joining their light” (the two eyes being sun and moon); the light of the ears is “the inner sun and moon joining their essence.”
  • Keen hearing and clear sight (congming 聪明): the ordinary word for “intelligent,” literally “sharp-eared and bright-eyed”; the text uses it to show that ears and eyes share one spiritual light.
  • True Breath (zhenxi): the inner breath that appears when the breathing of the mouth and nose becomes so fine it seems to stop.
  • Guarding the pure breath: from Zhuangzi, “Mastering Life,” meaning to keep the breath pure and unified.
  • Torpor and distraction: distraction is the spirit racing off, torpor is the spirit not yet clear; torpor is harder to cure.
  • Hun and po: in distraction the hun is still present; in torpor the po is entirely in charge.
  • Inner seeing, inner listening: not looking outward and yet being wide awake is inner seeing; there is no actual act of looking inward.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Sequence: lower the curtains → set the reference → let go → if you cannot let go, place the mind on the breath (listening to the breath).
  2. Listen to the “silence”: the breath should be so fine the ears cannot hear it; if you can hear it, it is too coarse. The point is not to control the breath, but to “know” it and let it become finer and finer by itself.
  3. Use without using: with the mind on the breath, just “shine on it and listen faintly.” Do not stare at it, and do not count too forcefully.
  4. The True Breath is not produced by holding the breath: “the faint breath suddenly stops” happens by itself when stillness reaches a certain depth. Holding the breath in order to make it “stop” is a mistake.
  5. Remedies: drowsy (torpor) → has the breath become coarse and turbid? Make it fine and clear; you may lift the eyelids slightly. Mind wandering (distraction) → bring it back and rest it on the breath.
  6. Shutting the doors is not inner seeing: merely closing the eyes and blocking the ears lets the mind run about inside. While shutting the doors, keep a wakeful awareness.

Translator's Notes

  1. “This method relies entirely on the light of the ears… the light of the eyes is the outer sun and moon joining their light; the light of the ears is the inner sun and moon joining their essence”: alchemical texts often compare the two eyes to the sun and moon. Reverting the Light is the light of the two eyes meeting outwardly; listening to the breath works inwardly, so that spirit and breath unite within — hence “joining their essence.” The practice of regulating the breath lies entirely in “listening,” which is why the text says it “relies entirely on the light of the ears.” This is the more common interpretation, offered for reference.
  2. “When the mind is unified, it moves the breath… when the breath is unified, it moves the mind”: an adaptation of Mencius 2A: “When the will is unified it moves the breath; when the breath is unified it moves the will.”
  3. “There is no actual act of looking inward”: this stresses that inner seeing is not an act of turning the eyeballs inward; it is simply not looking outward while the mind remains wide awake.
  4. On the Zhuangzi passage quoted by Qiu Ling: the line “the mind stops at matching” (xin zhi yu fu 心止于符) has been rendered in different ways by translators of the Zhuangzi; James Legge (1891), for instance, takes it to mean that the mind’s work is the matching of what is presented to it. We follow Qiu Ling’s own reading, in which fu is understood as “spirit.”
