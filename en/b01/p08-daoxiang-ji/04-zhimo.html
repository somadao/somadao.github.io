4 · The Method of Subduing Demons

4 · The Method of Subduing Demons — the demon of sleep is the first barrier; drowsiness and restlessness

Listen to this chapterENEnglish

About This Chapter

Qiu Ling says this section is “the essential formula for entering the gate”: many people want to practice but cannot keep it up, or keep it up without results, because they are trapped by drowsiness and restlessness.

The author says that those who refine the elixir take the “demon of sleep” as the first barrier. This demon is my own yin breath: with much yin one sleeps, with much yang one is awake. It is not that one may not sleep; it is that in quiet sitting drowsiness is what must most be avoided. The remedy: in quiet sitting, concentrate the subtle spirit in the Cavity of Breath, with mind and eyes resting there, calm and at ease; when a stray thought arises, shine the clear light of nature on it, and it vanishes as it arises — one arising, one illumination; one illumination, one vanishing — until “arising, nothing arises; illuminating, nothing is illuminated.” Then the demon of sleep has nothing to use its strength on, and only then can one enter great stillness; entering great stillness, one can see true movement; seeing true movement, one can gather and circulate the Celestial Circuit.

The second passage analyzes it more thoroughly: in sitting one knows only to “concentrate the spirit,” but not “how to concentrate it,” and so thoughts arise and cannot be stopped, the spirit races off and cannot be brought back; if thoughts cannot be stopped, contemplation cannot be correct; if the spirit cannot be brought back, stopping has no place to rest. The result is that one either falls into drowsiness or becomes restless. So the Dao has no other method than “stillness and illumination each finding their place” — finding where to rest and not leaving it for an instant, neither clinging nor separating, neither sticking nor slipping away. Loosen a little too much, and one enters vague emptiness; leave a little too much trace, and one fails through stiffness. It should be as clear as the autumn moon reflected in a limpid spring, as warm and harmonious as the rising sun on red plum blossoms.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

炼丹之士以睡魔为第一关头。须知此魔乃我之阴气。阴多则睡，阳多则醒。坐则入于睡乡，非丹家所宜。能打破此关，余无难事矣！然非不令寝眠，惟静坐时最忌昏沉，故丹经云：“运罢河车君再睡。”观此可知矣。但此睡魔，制伏自有善方，每于静坐时，凝我微细之神于气穴，心目所在，坦坦然然。惟当此理欲相争，不能无妄念发生，但念一生，即以朗朗性光照之，亦随生随灭。每一生一照，一照一灭，使生无所生，照无所照，则睡魔无所用力，始获入大静。入得大静，始能见得真动。见得真动，始可采运周天。到时神而明之，自能入妙。

For those who refine the elixir, the demon of sleep is the first barrier. Know that this demon is my own yin breath: with much yin one sleeps, with much yang one is awake. To fall into the land of sleep as soon as one sits is not fitting for an adept of the elixir. If one can break through this barrier, nothing else is difficult! Yet it is not that one may not sleep; only in quiet sitting is drowsiness most to be avoided. So the alchemical scriptures say: “Finish turning the River Chariot, sir, then go to sleep.” From this you can understand. But for this demon of sleep there is a good method of subduing it: each time you sit quietly, concentrate my subtle spirit in the Cavity of Breath, with mind and eyes resting there, open and at ease. Only, at this time heavenly principle and selfish desire contend, and stray thoughts cannot fail to arise; but as soon as a thought arises, shine the clear light of nature on it, and it too vanishes as it arises. Each arising, one illumination; each illumination, one vanishing — so that arising, nothing arises, and illuminating, nothing is illuminated. Then the demon of sleep has nothing to use its strength on, and only then can one enter great stillness. Having entered great stillness, one can see true movement. Having seen true movement, one can gather the medicine and circulate the Celestial Circuit. When the time comes, one grasps it by the spirit and naturally enters the wondrous state.

§2
Original (Chinese)

当坐时，只知凝神，不知所以凝之之法，是以念生而不能止，神驰而不能收。念不能止，则观不能正，神不能收，则止不得所，是以不入昏沉，即生烦躁。入昏沉则神无所依，颠倒梦想无所不至矣！生烦躁则性不耐久，而旋坐旋起，不得真定矣！故道无他术，只在寂照得所，得其所止，则须臾莫离，不即不离，始能不沾不脱。稍放空，则入于荒渺，稍着迹，则败于凝滞。非如秋月映澄泉，不足朗彻其妙性，非如旭日映红梅，不足融和其祖气。独惜光阴虚掷，不能力行实践。

When sitting, one knows only to concentrate the spirit, but not the method by which to concentrate it; so thoughts arise and cannot be stopped, and the spirit races off and cannot be brought back. If thoughts cannot be stopped, contemplation cannot be correct; if the spirit cannot be brought back, stopping has nowhere to rest. So one either falls into drowsiness or becomes restless. Falling into drowsiness, the spirit has nothing to rely on, and there is no limit to the topsy-turvy dreams and fancies! Becoming restless, one’s temper cannot last, one sits and rises again at once, and does not obtain true absorption! So the Dao has no other art: it lies only in stillness and illumination each finding their place. Having found where it should rest, one does not leave it for an instant; neither clinging nor separating, only then can one neither stick nor slip away. Loosen a little too much, and one enters vague emptiness; leave a little too much trace, and one fails through stiffness. Unless it is like the autumn moon reflected in a limpid spring, it cannot make its wondrous nature clear and bright; unless it is like the rising sun shining on red plum blossoms, it cannot make its ancestral breath warm and harmonious. It is only a pity that (many) waste their time in vain and cannot exert themselves to put it into practice.

Qiu Ling's noteon §1–§2
Original note (Chinese)

初学者每因昏沉与烦躁而不能入静，为魔障所制，遂至半途而废，此许多人虽有练功之心而未能坚持，或虽坚持而未能收效之由。此段教人捷法以制魔障，克服昏沉与烦躁，实为入门之要诀。

Beginners often cannot enter stillness because of drowsiness and restlessness; held back by these demonic obstacles, they give up halfway. This is why many people, though they have the wish to practice, cannot keep it up, or keep it up without results. This passage teaches a quick method for subduing demonic obstacles and overcoming drowsiness and restlessness; it is truly the essential formula for entering the gate.

Key Terms

  • Demon of sleep: drowsiness and sleepiness in quiet sitting; “this demon is my own yin breath.”
  • “Finish turning the River Chariot, sir, then go to sleep”: a saying from the alchemical scriptures, meaning do not sleep while practicing; sleep after you have finished. The River Chariot is the turning of the Celestial Circuit.
  • Open and at ease (tantan ranran 坦坦然然): calm, natural, not tense.
  • Heavenly principle and selfish desire contend: hence stray thoughts are unavoidable.
  • The clear light of nature: the bright light of awareness; see light of Nature.
  • One arising, one illumination; one illumination, one vanishing: as soon as a thought arises it is illuminated, and when illuminated it vanishes.
  • Arising, nothing arises; illuminating, nothing is illuminated: thoughts no longer arise, and illumination is no longer needed.
  • Great stillness, true movement: having entered great stillness, one first sees true movement.
  • Drowsiness and scatteredness: this section speaks of “drowsiness” and “restlessness.”
  • Stillness and illumination each finding their place: both stillness and illumination have something to rest on; that is, stopping and contemplating.
  • Vague emptiness (huangmiao 荒渺): boundless and vague, falling into emptiness.
  • Stiffness (ningzhi 凝滞): leaving traces, grasping.
  • The autumn moon in a limpid spring, the rising sun on red plum blossoms: the first is a metaphor for clarity, the second for warm harmony.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Dealing with dozing off: it is not that you may not sleep, but that you should not be drowsy while sitting. When you are short of sleep, sleep first, and sit once you have slept enough (see the “clear” formula of Section 3).
  2. One arising, one illumination; one illumination, one vanishing: when a thought comes up, do not fight it or suppress it; simply “see” it clearly, and it vanishes by itself. The more you illuminate, the fewer thoughts arise, until finally “arising, nothing arises; illuminating, nothing is illuminated.”
  3. Drowsiness and restlessness are a pair: both stem from not knowing how to “concentrate.” When the spirit has nowhere to rest, it either sinks down (drowsiness) or runs outward (restlessness).
  4. The formula for the right measure: loosen a little too much, and one enters vague emptiness; leave a little too much trace, and one fails through stiffness. — Too loose and it goes empty; too tight and it goes rigid.
  5. Two images you can take into sitting: the autumn moon reflected in a clear spring (bright clarity), the rising sun on red plum blossoms (gentle warmth).

Translator's Notes

  1. “When the time comes, one grasps it by the spirit” (dao shi shen er ming zhi 到时神而明之): echoing the Appended Statements of the Book of Changes, “to grasp it by the spirit and make it clear depends on the person”; it means that at that time one will naturally understand intuitively.
  2. “It is only a pity that (many) waste their time in vain and cannot exert themselves to put it into practice”: the original has no subject; “many” is supplied, following the vernacular translation.
