Introduction to Part Eight

What this book is

The Collection of Daoxiang (Daoxiang ji 道乡集) was written by Shi Conglong, the Recluse of Mount Lao; it gathers what he said when teaching his disciples, and was printed in the early years of the Republic. Compared with the preceding parts, its greatest feature is that it speaks plainly, with few metaphors: on concentrating the spirit, the Mysterious Pass, the Celestial Circuit or nurturing the embryo, it does not go round in circles but tells you directly what to do, what will happen when you do it, and what will happen if you do it wrong. Qiu Ling judges that it contains “many genuine insights, unlike other alchemical books with their many metaphors and their love of the strange and wonderful, which make them hard to read through.”

The passages of the original are not very connected; Qiu Ling selected the best of them, kept the original wording, and rearranged them by content into nine sections.

Qiu Ling's editorial note

《道乡集》为崂山逸叟史从龙撰，乃其将训迪门人之语集而成帙，于民国初年刻印问世。此书以寻常平实语言，深入细致地介绍道教传统养生功法，其中不少真知灼见，不似其它丹书诸多设喻，矜奇好怪，难以卒读。但此书上下多不连属，杂乱无章，兹撮拾其精华，削去其疵累，保持原文，分门别类重新编排，以便于学者阅读研究。

The Collection of Daoxiang was written by Shi Conglong, the Recluse of Mount Lao, who gathered into a volume the words with which he instructed his disciples; it was printed and published in the early years of the Republic. In ordinary, plain language it presents the traditional Daoist methods of nourishing life in depth and detail, and contains many genuine insights, unlike other alchemical books with their many metaphors and their love of the strange and wonderful, which make them hard to read through. But its passages are mostly unconnected and in disorder; so I have picked out the best of it, removed its blemishes, kept the original text, and rearranged it by category, for the convenience of students in reading and study.

What this part is about (in one sentence)

The Dao is only the two words “movement and stillness,” in fact only the one word “stillness”: when stillness reaches its extreme, there is naturally a movement, and this movement is the primordial breath of Prior Heaven, the opening of the Mysterious Pass, the production of the Medicine; at that moment concentrate the spirit on the place of movement and let the breathing follow it back to the root, so that the three families — spirit, breath and respiration — unite as one. This is the whole of the work; from the beginning through nurturing the embryo to sending out the spirit, it is all this one principle.

Reading tips

  1. The first six sections are the essence of this part; anyone can read and use them. In particular, “what true embryonic breathing is” in Section 2, the formula of the word “clear” in Section 3, the analysis of drowsiness and restlessness in Section 4 and “the Mysterious Pass is sought in movement” in Section 6 are all very solid words of experience.
  2. One standard runs through the whole part: naturalness. The author says again and again: “as soon as there is any forcing, it is not the correct way,” “neither sticking nor letting go, neither near nor apart,” “if it does not move, leading it is useless; if it is not still, fixing it does harm.” Keep this standard in mind when reading the later sections.
  3. Section 7, “the Great Medicine passing the gates,” contains old methods such as tightly contracting the grain path, pressing the perineum with a “ritual implement” and clamping the nose with a “wooden clip,” and Section 8 speaks of “ceasing to eat” and “stopping the breath”; these are all records of practitioners of old and must on no account be imitated on one’s own. We have added safety notes at the relevant places.
  4. Sections 8 and 9 (nurturing the embryo, sending out the spirit) belong to very high stages; Qiu Ling says: “Sending out the spirit is the highest ideal of the qigong of the Daoist school for nourishing life; I record it so that readers can glimpse the whole course of the method, and for reference.”
