Author’s Preface

Front Matter · Qiu Ling’s Preface

Translation

The alchemical scriptures and Daoist books of our country are as vast as a sea of mist. They hold a great deal of the essence and the subtle points of qigong, but dragons and snakes are mixed together: the essential stands beside the worthless, science beside superstition. They truly need further excavation and sorting, and this is an important task for us if we are to inherit and carry forward the precious qigong heritage of our motherland.

Many qigong enthusiasts, wishing to deepen their own practice, to explore the secrets of the higher methods and to understand the true meaning of ancient Chinese qigong, are eager to study some of the old qigong books, but they often meet with many difficulties. Until now very few of these old books have been reprinted and openly published — one could wear out iron shoes looking for them. Even when one happens to borrow an old copy or two, the texts are mostly written in metaphors and veiled language, full of code names and secret words, deliberately mystifying, so that the reader is dazzled and confused, can hardly read to the end, and closes the book with a sigh at how hard it is to read and how hard to choose. In recent years Shanghai and Chengdu have reprinted the Daoist Canon (Daozang 道藏) and the Essentials of the Daoist Canon (Daozang jiyao 道藏辑要) respectively. This is great news — a treasure of our culture made available again — but because these collections are enormous, are not sold in separate volumes and are very expensive, ordinary individuals cannot afford them. For this reason I have long wished to edit and annotate a selection of old qigong books to meet readers’ needs; but I was always busy with other things, and rare and secret editions suitable for general readers were not easy to obtain, so this wish long remained unfulfilled.

Recently my Illustrated Popular Chinese Qigong (Tujie Zhongguo liuxing qigong 图解中国流行气功), with its sequel, was published in Hong Kong and Guangzhou respectively (the mainland edition is titled Selected Popular Chinese Qigong, Zhongguo liuxing qigong xuan 中国流行气功选), and was well received by readers. This made me feel all the more that a selection of old qigong books with annotations was needed. So I went through my old drafts, revised and supplemented them, continued to collect rare and secret editions of the old qigong texts, sorted and annotated them, and at last completed this book, which I have named Selected Ancient Chinese Qigong Texts, Annotated (Zhongguo gudai qigong xuanzhu 中国古代气功选注).

From the many old qigong books, this volume selects nine alchemical texts (including one appendix). All are rare and secret editions of Daoist qigong; they are relatively plain and easy to understand, practical and reliable, explain the keys of the methods quite clearly, each has its own character, and together they include advanced methods. The Secret of the Golden Flower of Patriarch Lü (Lüzu jinhua zongzhi 吕祖金华宗旨) and the Heart Talks of the Two Idlers (Erlan xinhua 二懒心话) discuss the method of Reverting the Light with penetrating insight, and are rare works for the study of the body’s light patterns. Perfected Yin’s Immortal Scripture of Opening and Closing (Yin zhenren hepi xianjing 尹真人阖辟仙经) and Perfected Yin’s Secret Teachings: Questions and Answers in the Liaoyang Hall (Yin zhenren Liaoyang dian wenda mishou pian 尹真人寥阳殿问答秘授篇) present the whole course of the inner nourishing-life practice of the Longmen (Dragon Gate) lineage of Daoism, and have original things to say about both method and principle. Further Fragmentary Words (Suoyan xu 琐言续) presents living formulas for treating illness through practice in the four seasons, according to different natural conditions and bodily states. I especially recommend to students two alchemical books, the Collection of Daoxiang (Daoxiang ji 道乡集) and the Essentials of the Great Accomplishment (Dacheng jieyao 大成捷要). The former, in ordinary and plain language, explains the Daoist methods in a way that is both deep and accessible; it fits the teachings exactly and is full of genuine insight. The latter gathers the essence of the alchemical books of all ages and is a grand compilation of what the ancients learned from practice. In addition, two works on women’s alchemy are included. Of these, Chen Yingning’s Commentary on Sun Bu’er’s Poems on the Stages of Women’s Alchemy (Sun Bu’er nüdan cidi shi zhu 孙不二女丹次第诗注), included as an appendix, is worth reading for both the poems and the commentary; it can be called a gem among the old qigong books, and I hope students will give it their attention.

The highest level of Daoist inner nourishing-life practice is the sending out of the Yang Spirit, at the stage of refining Spirit and returning to Emptiness. Modern books and periodicals mostly avoid this level altogether, so students who wish to glimpse what it contains cannot do so. I considered that this level is the highest aim and ideal of Daoist practitioners, and that many qigong masters through the ages have held that the so-called Yang Spirit is nothing but a mass of true breath condensed together — something with a material nature, not superstition or absurdity. So this book keeps all the passages in the old texts that deal with this level. Although the old alchemical texts use all sorts of metaphors to make their teachings seem mysterious, in reality there is nothing especially supernatural about them: they are simply an art of nourishing life that advocates ascetic inner refinement.

In annotating this book, whenever I came to a state that my own practice had not yet reached, I found it hard to set pen to paper. I could only gather what earlier writers had said and explain the scriptures by the scriptures, or else leave the passage without a note. I did not dare to guess at it wildly, and I ask the reader’s understanding.

In compiling this book I received the help of fellow practitioners and friends, who went to some trouble to provide texts and suggestions, and the elder Wu kindly wrote a preface. To all of them I express my sincere thanks. I hope that through this book readers may glimpse the marrow and the whole of Daoist qigong, understand the essence of ancient Chinese qigong, and find help in studying classic qigong works such as the Token for the Agreement of the Three According to the Book of Changes (Zhouyi cantong qi 周易参同契) and Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian 悟真篇), and benefit in their practice. That would make me very happy. My learning is poor and my practice shallow; errors and omissions in the editing and annotation are inevitable, and I hope that worthy scholars at home and abroad will correct them.

Qiu Ling, 7 July 1987, Guangzhou
