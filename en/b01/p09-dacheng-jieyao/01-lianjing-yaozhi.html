I.1 · The Essentials of Refining Essence into Breath

Records of Experience in Refining Essence into Breath, Section 1 · The Essentials of Refining Essence into Breath — the general outline of the hundred-day foundation

About This Chapter

This section is the general outline of the first group of the whole text, “Records of Experience in Refining Essence into Breath.” In a passage of parallel prose it summarizes the hundred days of laying the foundation from beginning to end: first understand five matters, then distinguish six timings; regulate the outer medicine with wind and fire, civil and martial, and refine the lesser medicine with the fire tallies of the Celestial Circuit; when the fire phasing is sufficient, the Great Medicine passes the Three Passes and descends to the middle field, and one returns to the state in which “the original substance is not yet broken.”

The most important part of Qiu Ling’s note is its first sentence: the “hundred days” is only an approximate figure, said of the ancients practicing day and night without stopping; people today need not be bound by this limit. He also lists “understanding the five matters” and “distinguishing the six timings” item by item, and quotes Wu Chongxu’s own account of laying his foundation as an example. There are many terms in this section, which later sections will unfold one by one; here it is enough to get an overall impression.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

夫道之初节炼精化炁功夫，名曰小成筑基。欲出疾病，却衰老，延寿考，返童颜，当行百日筑基之功。明五事，则入手有准，辨六候而运行无差。

The work of the first stage of the Dao, refining Essence into Breath, is called “the lesser accomplishment of laying the foundation.” Those who wish to be rid of illness, ward off old age, lengthen their years, and regain a youthful face should do the work of the hundred days of laying the foundation. Understand the “five matters,” and one’s beginning has a sure standard; distinguish the “six timings,” and the circulation will go without error.

§2
Original (Chinese)

调元精以炼外药，须凭风火文武之妙。采真种以炼小药，要假周天火符之玄机。火足药灵，龟头缩，而丹放毫光。意采眸取，六根震，而五龙捧圣。

To regulate the original essence and refine the outer medicine, one must rely on the wonders of wind and fire, civil and martial; to gather the true seed and refine the lesser medicine, one must make use of the mysterious mechanism of the fire tallies of the Celestial Circuit. When the fire is sufficient, the medicine becomes potent, the glans retracts, and the elixir emits a hair-fine light. Gathering with the intention and taking with the light of the eyes, the six roots shake — this is “five dragons bearing up the sage.”

§3
Original (Chinese)

透三关，过九窍，永成金刚不坏之体；升乾鼎，降坤炉，定作长生不老之人。此百日筑基之功，已返到本体未破、乾坤交泰之地。

Penetrating the Three Passes and going through the nine openings, one becomes forever a diamond body that does not decay; rising to the Qian cauldron and descending to the Kun furnace, one will surely become a person of long life who does not grow old. When this work of the hundred days of laying the foundation is complete, one has already returned to the state in which the original substance is not yet broken, and Qian and Kun are in harmonious union.

Qiu Ling's noteon §1–§3
Original note (Chinese)

此段概述道教丹功百日筑基要点。百日之约数，乃指古人练功日夜不辍而言，今人一般难以做到，当不受此限。

伍冲虚在《仙佛合宗》一书中对门人谈其练功经验云：“我于万历壬寅初试百日关，于家守一月调息，次一月精进，时至神知，运一周天。自是以来，一夕行过三、五周天，又至十余周天，精尽化炁，火候足矣，遂得止火之景而止之。约两月之余，终三月之季。古称百日筑基者信哉。昔曹还阳真人下功夫时，昼夜功勤，不五十日而火足，采其大药。五日而得火珠驰骤上冲下突，有自然投关之妙。”此段伍冲虚氏谈其百日筑基成功之经过。

明五事为：第一事明先天三宝精、气、神；第二事明炼己还虚；第三事明凝神气穴；第四事明机动调药；第五事明药产采炼三经过似之经过。

辨六候为：第一，采药之候；第二，封固之候；第三，起小周天之候；第四，进退颠倒之候；第五，沐浴之候；第六，火足止火之候。

药物喻真气。伍冲虚《天仙正理》说：“药本一炁也。”“外药者何也？盖古云：金丹内药自外来，以祖炁从。生身时，虽隐藏于丹田，却有向外发生之时，即取此发生于外者，复还于内。是以虽从内生，却从外来，故谓之外药。炼成还丹，斯谓之内药，又谓大药。”

六根指耳、目、鼻、舌、身、意。

五龙捧圣喻用意念引大药过关。《道乡集》云：“如五龙捧圣者，五即土数，龙即真意，圣即大药也。概言以真意而采取，捧过三关九窍，至乾顶而后降中田，正所谓乾坤交媾罢，一点落黄庭也。”乾鼎指泥丸穴，坤炉指下丹田。

This passage outlines the main points of the hundred days of laying the foundation in Daoist alchemical practice. “A hundred days” is an approximate figure, said of the ancients practicing day and night without stopping; people today can generally hardly do this, and should not be bound by this limit.

Wu Chongxu, in the book Common Origin of Immortals and Buddhas, spoke to his disciples of his own experience of practice: “In the renyin year of the Wanli reign (1602) I first tried the hundred-day pass. At home, for the first month I kept to regulating the breath; for the second month I advanced with vigor; when the time came, the spirit knew it of itself, and I ran one Celestial Circuit. From then on, in one night I could run three or five circuits, then more than ten; the essence was entirely transformed into breath, the fire phasing was sufficient, and so the signs for stopping the fire appeared and I stopped it. In all it took about two months and more, ending at the close of the third month. The ancients’ talk of a hundred days of laying the foundation is indeed to be believed. Formerly, when the Perfected Cao Huanyang was doing the work, he toiled day and night, and in less than fifty days the fire was sufficient and he gathered his Great Medicine; after five more days he obtained the fiery pearl racing, rushing up and thrusting down, with the wonder of entering the passes of itself.” This passage is Mr. Wu Chongxu’s account of how he succeeded in his hundred days of laying the foundation.

“Understanding the five matters” is: first, understanding the three treasures of Prior Heaven — essence, breath, and spirit; second, understanding refining the self to return to emptiness; third, understanding concentrating the spirit in the Cavity of Breath; fourth, understanding regulating the medicine when the mechanism moves; fifth, understanding the whole course of the medicine’s production, gathering, and refining.

“Distinguishing the six timings” is: first, the timing of gathering the medicine; second, the timing of sealing; third, the timing of starting the Lesser Celestial Circuit; fourth, the timing of advancing, withdrawing, and reversing; fifth, the timing of ablution; sixth, the timing of stopping the fire when it is sufficient.

“Medicine” is a metaphor for the true breath. Wu Chongxu’s True Principles of the Celestial Immortals says: “The medicine is originally one breath.” “What is the outer medicine? The ancients said: the inner medicine of the golden elixir comes from outside, following the ancestral breath. At birth, though it lies hidden in the Cinnabar Field, there are times when it issues outward; one takes what has issued outward and returns it within. So although it is born from within, it comes back from outside; hence it is called the outer medicine. Once refined into the Returned Elixir, it is then called the inner medicine, also called the Great Medicine.”

“The six roots” are the ear, eye, nose, tongue, body, and mind.

“Five dragons bearing up the sage” is a metaphor for guiding the Great Medicine through the passes with the mind’s intention. The Collection of Daoxiang says: “Take ‘five dragons bearing up the sage’: five is the number of earth, the dragon is the True Intention, and the sage is the Great Medicine. In short, it means gathering with the True Intention, bearing it up through the Three Passes and Nine Openings, to the Qian summit and then down to the middle field — exactly what is meant by ‘When Qian and Kun have finished their union, one drop falls into the Yellow Court.'” The Qian cauldron means the Muddy Pellet point; the Kun furnace means the lower Cinnabar Field.

Key Terms

  • Refining Essence into Breath: the first stage of the alchemical method, also called “the lesser accomplishment of laying the foundation” or “the hundred days of laying the foundation”; see the three transmutations.
  • Understanding the five matters: the three treasures of essence, breath, and spirit; refining the self to return to emptiness; concentrating the spirit in the Cavity of Breath; regulating the medicine when the mechanism moves; the course of the medicine’s production, gathering, and refining.
  • Distinguishing the six timings: gathering the medicine, sealing, starting the Lesser Celestial Circuit, advancing, withdrawing, and reversing, ablution, stopping the fire when it is sufficient. See fire phasing, ablution, stopping the fire.
  • Outer medicine, lesser medicine, Great Medicine: all are “one breath,” named differently by stage. See Medicine.
  • Wind and fire, civil and martial: wind is respiration, fire is spirit; civil is non-action, martial is action. See Section 3 for details, and Wind of Xun.
  • The glans retracting: the bodily sign that the fire is sufficient, also called the horse-like retraction.
  • The shaking of the six roots, five dragons bearing up the sage: gathering the Great Medicine and the Great Medicine passing the gates; see Sections 9 and 10 for details.
  • Qian cauldron, Kun furnace: the Muddy Pellet and the lower Cinnabar Field.
  • The original substance not yet broken, Qian and Kun in harmonious union: returning to a pure and complete state not yet depleted by Posterior Heaven; see Qian and Kun.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Do not be daunted by the “hundred days,” nor let it hurry you along. Qiu Ling says plainly that the hundred days is an approximate figure reached by the ancients practicing day and night without stopping. Today’s practitioner should keep to his or her own pace and simply persevere.
  2. The order of “the five matters” is the order of entering the practice. First understand what essence, breath, and spirit are, then quiet the mind (refining the self to return to emptiness), and only then speak of concentrating the spirit in the Cavity of Breath; only when there is stirring in the body does one speak of regulating and gathering the medicine. If the earlier step has not been done well, none of the later terms can be put to use.
  3. What is noteworthy in Wu Chongxu’s own account is “at home, for the first month I kept to regulating the breath.” However high the attainment, the first month is still spent honestly regulating the breath.

Translator's Notes

  1. In Qiu Ling’s note, “fifth, understanding the course of the medicine’s production, gathering, and refining, three courses resembling the course” (第五事明药产采炼三经过似之经过) is corrupt: Wu Chongxu’s original lists the five matters as “the three treasures of Prior Heaven, refining the self to return to emptiness, concentrating the spirit in the Cavity of Breath, regulating the medicine when the mechanism moves, the production, gathering, and refining of the medicine”; the words 三经过似之 are probably a printing error. The translation follows “understanding the whole course of the medicine’s production, gathering, and refining,” and the original text keeps the reading of the base text.
  2. The renyin year of the Wanli reign is the thirtieth year of Wanli in the Ming (1602). Cao Huanyang is Cao Changhua, Wu Chongxu’s teacher.
  3. “The fiery pearl racing, rushing up and thrusting down, with the wonder of entering the passes of itself”: the scene of the Great Medicine stirring and rushing of itself toward the Three Passes; see Section 10, “A True Account of the Great Medicine Passing the Gates.”
  4. “Nine openings”: here a general term for the passes and openings of the whole body, not necessarily to be taken as exactly nine.
  5. Qiu Ling sets the original text of this section as a single passage; we have divided it by content into three short passages, for ease of comparison and reading aloud.
