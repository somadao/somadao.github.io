I.3 · The Wondrous Use of Civil and Martial Wind and Fire

Records of Experience in Refining Essence into Breath, Section 3 · The Wondrous Use of Civil and Martial Wind and Fire — the four-word formula of planting, gathering, refining, and nourishing

Listen to this chapterENEnglish

Safety note (translator): What this section calls “martial wind and martial fire,” “fierce cooking and urgent refining,” and “rousing the spirit, fixing the gaze, stirring up the Wind of Xun, fanning up the furnace flames” are methods of intensifying breathing and concentration that the ancients used briefly at particular moments. In the last section of this group, Qiu Ling points out clearly that if the martial fire is too heavy and the breathing coarse and unregulated, one draws in cold air and suffers abdominal pain and diarrhea. Beginners should rely mainly on the civil fire (natural, gentle breathing), should not hold the breath, and should not forcefully pump the belly; this is especially unsuitable for those with heart or lung disease or high blood pressure.

About This Chapter

This section is about the fire phasing and is the most concrete set of operating instructions in the first group. It first gives a few definitions: wind is the breath of respiration, fire is the empty, numinous spirit; civil is wind and fire without action, martial is wind and fire with action.

The method is this: each night after the zi hour (midnight), at the extreme of stillness, one wakes naturally and the outer kidney rises (the return of the one yang) — this is the moment when the outer medicine arises. At this time, civil fire is used at the two ends, and martial fire in the middle when the original essence is at its strongest — rousing the spirit, fixing the gaze on the Cinnabar Field, and using the breath to stir it up, turning the true breath that is about to flow downward back into original breath. Once the original breath has returned to its place, one immediately stops the martial fire of action and switches to the civil fire of non-action, gently illuminating, as if holding and as if forgetting, until one enters the chaotic, dim, and dark.

Qiu Ling’s note sums up the whole book’s treatment of this passage in the four-word formula “planting, gathering, refining, nourishing.” It is very clearly ordered, and is the part of this section most worth rereading.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

入手功夫，每当半夜子时以后，虚极静笃，天然醒觉。一阳来复，外肾兴起之时，元精吐露，外药发生之际，即当行调药之功，运动风吹火炼之玄机。

The work of beginning: each night after the zi hour, in utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness, one wakes naturally. When the one yang returns and the outer kidney rises, it is the moment when the original essence is disclosed and the outer medicine arises; one should then do the work of regulating the medicine, setting in motion the mysterious mechanism of blowing with wind and refining with fire.

§2
Original (Chinese)

夫风者呼吸之气，火者虚灵之神。文者无为之风火，武者有为之风火。盖无为之文风文火，用在调外药前后两头；而有为之武风武火，用在调外药元精正旺之时也。

Wind is the breath of respiration; fire is the empty, numinous spirit. Civil is wind and fire without action; martial is wind and fire with action. The civil wind and civil fire of non-action are used at the two ends, before and after, of regulating the outer medicine; the martial wind and martial fire of action are used when, in regulating the outer medicine, the original essence is at its strongest.

§3
Original (Chinese)

盖真气动时，最易下流顺出，必用真意眸光凝入命宫气穴之中，主照统摄，真气即下行而化为元精。非呼吸之息，以风助火，以火销金，而元精必不能复化为元气，归宿坤炉本宫中矣。

For when the true breath stirs, it most easily flows downward and out; one must concentrate the True Intention and the light of the eyes into the Cavity of Breath of the palace of life, presiding over it, illuminating it, holding it together. Once the true breath goes downward, it turns into original essence; if one does not use the breath of respiration — using wind to help fire, and fire to melt metal — the original essence will surely not be able to turn back into original breath and return to rest in its own palace, the Kun furnace.

§4
Original (Chinese)

然用呼吸之息，在丹田之中，一出一入，必须提起精神，目光窥定，一意不散，万缘皆空，鼓动巽风，煽起炉焰，使息息归根，方合猛烹急炼之神功。而炉中之意，切莫着于呼吸，只专意于元气，不过借呼吸之机，以为采取烹炼之旨也。

Yet when using the breath of respiration, going out and coming in within the Cinnabar Field, one must rouse the spirit, fix the gaze, keep a single intention without scattering, with all conditions empty, stir up the Wind of Xun and fan up the furnace flames, letting every breath return to the root — only this accords with the divine work of “fierce cooking and urgent refining.” But the intention in the furnace must never be attached to the respiration; it is fixed solely on the original breath, and merely borrows the mechanism of respiration for the purpose of gathering, cooking, and refining.

§5
Original (Chinese)

调外药中间用武火锻炼，元气归宿本宫，即当止住有为之风火，再用无为之神火时刻温养，以真意轻轻主照，若存若忘，勿令间断，安神于气穴之内，知而不守，使自然之吹嘘绵绵不绝，念兹在兹，先存后亡，而入于混沌杳冥者也。

In the middle of regulating the outer medicine, one refines with martial fire; once the original breath has returned to rest in its own palace, one should stop the wind and fire of action and again use the spirit-fire of non-action to warm and nourish it at every moment: preside over it and illuminate it lightly with the True Intention, as if holding and as if forgetting, never letting it be interrupted; settle the spirit within the Cavity of Breath, knowing without guarding, letting the natural blowing and breathing continue unbroken, every thought here, first holding and later forgetting, and so enter the chaotic, dim, and dark.

§6
Original (Chinese)

吹嘘之气，乃后天之呼吸，引动先天之气机，神不离于气穴，自然往来无穷，行住坐卧不离方寸之地。盖文火温养是处常也。武火锻炼是达变也。

The breath of blowing and breathing is the respiration of Posterior Heaven, used to draw out the mechanism of the breath of Prior Heaven; when the spirit does not leave the Cavity of Breath, it naturally comes and goes without end, and whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying, one does not leave this square inch. Warm nourishing with the civil fire is keeping to the constant; refining with the martial fire is responding to change.

Qiu Ling's noteon §1–§6
Original note (Chinese)

此言调外药之火候。火候者乃口鼻呼吸和真息（真人之呼吸，又名胎息）之运用。文风文火重在真息，武风武火重在口鼻呼吸。

入手调药功夫有种、采、炼、养四字诀。现把本书对这四字诀的解释综述如下：

种者行文风文火，凝神入气穴，返观内照，静之又静，沉之又沉，虚极静笃是也。只待混沌开基，元炁发动。

采者行武风武火，待到混沌开基，一阳来复，外肾兴起，即活子时，继续神住气穴，吸则有心，呼出无意，心力提起，目光射定，煽开炉焰，以外肾消缩净尽为止，谓之采药归炉。

炼者行半文半武之风火，药归炉后，仍神存气穴，呼吸并用，升则有心，降则有意，吸谓采取，呼谓烹炼，悠扬条畅，将丹田气机煽开，炼到丹田自吹自嘘为止，从有为之呼吸以至无为之吹嘘，使之精尽化为元炁，归宿于命宫。

养者又名沐浴，行文风文火，止住有为之呼吸，用自然之吹嘘熏蒸温养，安神于气穴，时以真意守之，缓缓以呼吸嘘之，使炉中火种绵绵不绝，悠悠常存，心息相依，神气皆归静定，不知不觉入于混沌杳冥之中。

This speaks of the fire phasing for regulating the outer medicine. The fire phasing is the use of the respiration of mouth and nose and of the True Breath (the breathing of the Perfected, also called embryonic breathing). Civil wind and civil fire put the weight on the True Breath; martial wind and martial fire put the weight on the respiration of mouth and nose.

The work of regulating the medicine at the beginning has a four-word formula: planting, gathering, refining, nourishing. Here is a summary of this book’s explanation of the four words:

Planting: practice the civil wind and civil fire, concentrate the spirit in the Cavity of Breath, turn the gaze back and illuminate within, still and again still, sinking and again sinking — that is, utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness. Simply wait for the foundation to open out of chaos and the original breath to stir.

Gathering: practice the martial wind and martial fire. When the foundation has opened out of chaos, the one yang has returned, and the outer kidney has risen — that is, the Living Zi Hour has come — continue to keep the spirit in the Cavity of Breath, with intent on the inhalation and without intent on the exhalation; rouse the mind’s strength, fix the gaze like an arrow, fan open the furnace flames, until the outer kidney has completely subsided — this is called “gathering the medicine and returning it to the vessel” (returning it to the furnace).

Refining: practice a wind and fire half civil and half martial. After the medicine has returned to the furnace, the spirit still remains in the Cavity of Breath; exhalation and inhalation are both used, with intent in rising and with intention in descending; the inhalation is called gathering, the exhalation cooking and refining; leisurely and smooth, fan open the mechanism of breath in the Cinnabar Field, refining until the Cinnabar Field blows and breathes by itself — passing from the respiration of action to the blowing and breathing of non-action, so that the essence is entirely transformed into original breath and returns to rest in the palace of life.

Nourishing: also called ablution. Practice the civil wind and civil fire, stop the respiration of action, and warm, steam, and nourish with natural blowing and breathing; settle the spirit in the Cavity of Breath, guard it at all times with the True Intention, and breathe on it slowly and gently, so that the seed of fire in the furnace continues unbroken and remains ever present, mind and breath relying on each other, spirit and breath both returning to settled stillness, and without knowing it one enters the chaotic, dim, and dark.

Key Terms

  • Wind, fire: wind is the breath of respiration, fire is the empty, numinous spirit; see Wind of Xun, fire phasing.
  • Civil fire, martial fire: wind and fire without action and with action. Qiu Ling’s note: civil fire puts the weight on the True Breath, martial fire on the respiration of mouth and nose.
  • Outer kidney: the male genitals; its rising is regarded as the bodily signal of the return of the one yang.
  • The Cavity of Breath of the palace of life, the Kun furnace as its own palace: both mean the lower Cinnabar Field.
  • Using wind to help fire, fire to melt metal: using respiration to help the spirit, and using the spirit to “melt” the downward-flowing essence back into original breath. “Metal” here is a metaphor for the original essence.
  • Fierce cooking and urgent refining: the concentrated use of martial fire.
  • Knowing without guarding, first holding and later forgetting: the essentials of warm nourishing with the civil fire.
  • The square inch: here, the Cavity of Breath.
  • Keeping to the constant, responding to change: civil fire is the constant method, martial fire the method for change.
  • Planting, gathering, refining, nourishing: the four-word formula for regulating the medicine, as summarized by Qiu Ling.
  • True Breath, embryonic breathing: the breathing of the Perfected.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Civil fire is the constant; martial fire is for change. The author himself says so clearly: ordinarily one always warms and nourishes with civil fire; martial fire is used only briefly at particular moments. For today’s practitioner, almost all the time should be spent on the two words “planting” and “nourishing”: concentrating the spirit, stillness, sinking, as if holding and as if forgetting.
  2. “The intention in the furnace must never be attached to the respiration.” Even when the breath is used, attention is not on the breathing itself but on the point of warmth and stability that the breathing brings about. Watching, counting, regulating, and controlling the breath is the most common deviation.
  3. “Stopping action” is harder than “using action,” and more important. As soon as you feel warmth and stability in the lower abdomen, put down all technique at once and return to “knowing without guarding.”
  4. Whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying, do not leave the square inch. This is how quiet sitting is brought into daily life: while walking or working, now and then gently bring your attention back to the lower abdomen, for just a moment.

Translator's Notes

  1. “Once the true breath goes downward, it turns into original essence”: this sentence explains why one must concentrate, illuminate, and hold it together — once the true breath flows downward, it turns into original essence, and the original essence can turn back into original breath only through the wind of respiration and the fire of the spirit.
  2. “Gathering the medicine and returning it to the furnace”: the alchemical books also write “gathering the medicine and returning it to the vessel”; see the glossary entry Medicine.
  3. “Until the outer kidney has completely subsided”: this book in many places takes the rising and subsiding of the outer kidney as markers of the fire phasing, a characteristic of men’s alchemical methods.
  4. The original has two passages; we have divided them by content into six short passages.
