I.7 · The Use of the True Intention in the Lesser Celestial Circuit

Records of Experience in Refining Essence into Breath, Section 7 · The Use of the True Intention in the Lesser Celestial Circuit — the six faults of the fire phasing and “firmness and softness balanced”

Listen to this chapterENEnglish

About This Chapter

This section is the most “universal” passage in the whole first group; what it discusses are in fact problems that every form of quiet practice runs into. The author lists six faults of the fire phasing:

FaultConsequenceThe author’s definition
Thoughts arisingThe fire flares (parching)It need not be the mind running outward; the slightest stray thought while raising the fire counts
Intention scatteringThe fire grows coldIt need not be drowsiness; the slightest inattention while raising the fire counts
Eyes looking outwardThe spirit races off and harms the hunIt is not looking about at random; opening and closing the eyes now and then counts
Ears listening outwardThe essence scatters and harms the poIt is not listening about at random; knowing that wind is blowing or rain falling counts
Breathing hurriedDiffuse, with nowhere to returnIt is not violence; wanting quick success is pulling up shoots to help them grow
Breathing stoppedBroken and without strengthIt is not holding the breath; wanting to sit and reap is waiting by the tree stump for a hare

The remedy is to rouse the spirit while not a single thought arises, letting one point of numinous light lead the original breath along the Conception and Governing vessels, “neither sticking nor slipping away, neither close nor apart, neither slow nor hasty, neither existent nor nonexistent” — firmness and softness balanced, just right.

Qiu Ling’s note defines the “True Intention”: the True Intention is a special kind of intention that presides over the circulation of the true breath in utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness, anciently called “correct awareness within emptiness.” He explains the metaphor “Wu and Ji as matchmaker” from Awakening to Reality, and quotes a passage of Huang Yuanji: within non-action there must be action, within not-knowing there must be awareness; only then does one neither fall into emptiness nor cling to existence.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

火符之利弊，不可不知也。一在念不可起，念起则火炎。一在意不可散，意散则火冷。一在目不可外视，外视则神驰而伤魂。一在耳不可外听，外听则精散而伤魄。一在呼吸不可骤，骤则散漫无归。一在呼吸不可停，停则断续无力。忽断忽续，或燥或寒，种种弊端为害，若不小心谨防危险，万无一成。

The benefits and faults of the fire tallies must be known. First, thoughts must not arise; when thoughts arise, the fire flares. Second, the intention must not scatter; when the intention scatters, the fire grows cold. Third, the eyes must not look outward; when they look outward, the spirit races off and harms the hun. Fourth, the ears must not listen outward; when they listen outward, the essence scatters and harms the po. Fifth, the breathing must not be hurried; when hurried, it is diffuse and has nowhere to return. Sixth, the breathing must not stop; when it stops, it is broken and without strength. Now broken, now continuing, now parching, now cold — all kinds of faults do harm; if one does not carefully guard against these dangers, there is not one chance in ten thousand of success.

§2
Original (Chinese)

然念起不必是外驰，就起阳火中，稍有妄想，便为念起。意散不必是神昏，就起火时稍有不经心，便为意散。外视非邪视也，而时闭时睁，便伤其魂。外听非乱听也，而知风知雨，便伤其魄。骤非燥暴之骤，心欲速成，便是揠苗助长。停非留住之停，意欲坐获，便是守株待兔。

Yet “thoughts arising” need not be the mind running outward; while raising the yang fire, the slightest stray thought is thoughts arising. “Intention scattering” need not be the spirit growing dull; while raising the fire, the slightest inattention is intention scattering. “Looking outward” is not looking improperly; merely opening and closing the eyes now and then harms the hun. “Listening outward” is not listening about at random; merely knowing that wind is blowing or rain falling harms the po. “Hurried” is not the hurriedness of violent haste; wanting in one’s heart to succeed quickly is pulling up the shoots to help them grow. “Stopped” is not the stopping of holding the breath; wanting in one’s intention to sit and reap is waiting by the tree stump for a hare.

§3
Original (Chinese)

欲除此数弊，法在运炼周天时振其精神，奋其志气，一念不起，一意不散，内不知有我，外不知有物，主敬存诚，一志凝神，使一灵性光，率领无炁，循行任督，无勤无惰，毋忘毋助，进阳火如赤日之后升，退阴符似皓月之前降，随息上升下降，历历如见。

To remove these several faults, the method is this: when running and refining the Celestial Circuit, rouse your spirit and stir up your resolve; let not a single thought arise and not a single intention scatter; within, do not know that there is a self; without, do not know that there are things; hold to reverence and preserve sincerity, with single-minded resolve concentrate the spirit, and let the one numinous light of nature lead the original breath along the Conception and Governing vessels, neither overdiligent nor lax, neither forgetting nor helping; advancing the yang fire is like the red sun rising behind, withdrawing the yin tally like the bright moon descending in front, rising and descending with the breath, as clearly as if seen with one’s own eyes.

§4
Original (Chinese)

不粘不脱，不即不离，不缓不急，不有不无，安安闲闲，绵绵密密，空空洞洞，悠悠荡荡，速无躐等之弊，缓无停滞之患，刚柔得中，神气合一，上下一贯，前后同轨，我只一念归乎中道而行，自然恰到好处，当无过不及，而无泛滥、流窜、停留、濡滞之弊也。

Neither sticking nor slipping away, neither close nor apart, neither slow nor hasty, neither existent nor nonexistent; at peace and at leisure, continuous and dense, empty and hollow, unhurried and free; when fast, without the fault of skipping steps; when slow, without the trouble of stagnation; firmness and softness balanced, spirit and breath united as one, above and below in one line, front and back on the same track. I simply let this one thought return to the middle way and proceed, and it is naturally just right, with neither excess nor deficiency, and without the faults of overflowing, straying, lingering, or sluggishness.

Qiu Ling's noteon §1–§4
Original note (Chinese)

真意是虚极静笃的气功态中主宰真气运行的一种特殊意念。古称虚无中之正觉，返观内照为其妙用。张伯端《悟真篇》云：“本因戊己为媒妁，遂使夫妻镇合欢。”戊己属土，为真意的代号。夫妻即坎离、精神的代称。动极而静之际，忽来真意以主持之，此意属阴，谓之己土；静极而动之际，则用真意收摄之，此意属阳，谓之戊土。其实真意不过以动静之基分为戊、己之土而已。

黄元吉《道德经注释》谈及真意与真气之关系时指出：“万象咸空，一灵独照，此为真意。一觉而动，一阳发生，是为元气。采药炼丹不过炼此性命二者。若无真意，性将何依？若无真气，命将何由修？以真意采真气，两者浑化为一，即返于太极之初，斯谓之丹。故无为之中又要有作有为，无知之内又要有知有觉，方不堕空，不著有，迨至功力弥深，空即是色，色即是空，久之空色两忘，浑然物化，斯与道大适矣。”

The True Intention is a special kind of intention that presides over the circulation of the true breath in the qigong state of utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness. The ancients called it “correct awareness within emptiness,” and turning the gaze back and illuminating within is its wondrous use. Zhang Boduan’s Awakening to Reality says: “Originally because Wu and Ji served as matchmaker, husband and wife were made to be ever joyfully united.” Wu and Ji belong to earth and are a code name for the True Intention. Husband and wife are code names for Kan and Li, essence and spirit. At the moment when movement at its extreme turns to stillness, the True Intention suddenly comes to preside over it; this intention belongs to yin and is called Ji earth. At the moment when stillness at its extreme turns to movement, one uses the True Intention to gather it in; this intention belongs to yang and is called Wu earth. In fact the True Intention is merely divided into Wu earth and Ji earth according to the two bases of movement and stillness.

Huang Yuanji, in his Commentary on the Daode jing, discussing the relationship between the True Intention and the true breath, points out: “When the myriad images are all empty and one numinous point illuminates alone, this is the True Intention. When with a flash of awareness there is movement and the one yang arises, this is the original breath. Gathering the medicine and refining the elixir is nothing but refining these two, nature and life. Without the True Intention, on what would nature rely? Without the true breath, by what means would life be cultivated? Gathering the true breath with the True Intention, the two blend and transform into one, and one returns to the beginning of the Great Ultimate — this is called the elixir. So within non-action there must also be doing and acting; within not-knowing there must also be knowing and awareness; only then does one neither fall into emptiness nor cling to existence. When the power of the work grows ever deeper, emptiness is form and form is emptiness; in time emptiness and form are both forgotten, one is wholly transformed with things, and this is to be in complete accord with the Dao.”

Key Terms

  • The fire flaring, the fire growing cold: when thoughts arise the fire is too parching; when the intention scatters the fire is too cold.
  • Hun and po: looking outward harms the hun; listening outward harms the po.
  • Pulling up shoots to help them grow, waiting by the tree stump for a hare: metaphors for the two attitudes of “hurrying” and “stopping,” respectively.
  • Holding to reverence and preserving sincerity: a Neo-Confucian expression, maintaining reverence and nurturing sincere intention.
  • The one numinous light of nature: the bright, illuminating awareness of the Original Spirit; see light of Nature.
  • Advancing the yang fire like the red sun rising behind, withdrawing the yin tally like the bright moon descending in front: the yang fire rises along the Governing Vessel in the back, the yin tally descends along the Conception Vessel in front; see Conception and Governing vessels.
  • Neither sticking nor slipping away, neither close nor apart: the middle way of the fire phasing, in agreement with the Collection of Daoxiang.
  • Skipping steps (liedeng 躐等): advancing rashly beyond one’s level. Sluggishness (ruzhi 濡滞): slowness and delay.
  • True Intention: Qiu Ling’s definition — a special kind of intention presiding over the circulation of the true breath in utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness, “correct awareness within emptiness.”
  • Wu and Ji, Wu earth, Ji earth: code names for the True Intention; when movement at its extreme turns to stillness it is Ji earth, when stillness at its extreme turns to movement it is Wu earth. See the entry True Intention.
  • Husband and wife: code names for Kan and Li, essence and spirit; see the conjunction of Kan and Li.

Putting It into Practice

  1. The definitions of the six faults are more useful than the six faults themselves. The author sets the standard very finely: “the slightest stray thought” is thoughts arising, “the slightest inattention” is intention scattering, “knowing that wind is blowing or rain falling” is listening outward. This is not a demand for perfection, but a way of telling you that deviations are often very subtle — once you know this, you can gently come back again and again.
  2. “Wanting to succeed quickly” and “wanting to sit and reap” are a pair. The first uses too much force, the second waits idly; both are problems of attitude, not of technique.
  3. “Firmness and softness balanced” is the overall key. The spirit must be roused (rouse your spirit), yet the mind must be let go (not a single thought arising) — roused but not tense, let go but not scattered.
  4. Huang Yuanji’s words are worth remembering: within non-action there must be action, within not-knowing there must be awareness. Quiet sitting is not staring blankly; only with a point of clear awareness present does one not fall into dull emptiness.

Translator's Notes

  1. “Lead the original breath” (率领无炁): 无 (“no”) should be 元 (“original”), an error due to similar shapes; the translation follows 元炁, while the original text keeps the reading of the base text.
  2. “Originally because Wu and Ji served as matchmaker, husband and wife were made to be ever joyfully united”: a line from one of the regulated verses in seven-character lines of Zhang Boduan’s Awakening to Reality; 镇 means “long, constantly.”
  3. “Harming the hun,” “harming the po”: in Chinese medicine the liver stores the hun and opens into the eyes, and the lungs store the po; this book’s saying that looking outward harms the hun and listening outward (scattering the essence) harms the po is the alchemists’ view.
  4. Huang Yuanji’s Commentary on the Daode jing is his Lectures on the Daode jing (Daode jing jiangyi 道德经讲义); see the third book on this site, Huang Yuanji of the Qing: Heart-Methods of Quiet Practice for Nourishing Life, Annotated.
  5. The original text of this section is one passage; we have divided it into four short passages.
