Chapter 31 · Tranquil Detachment Above All

Weapons are instruments of ill omen, and victory is nothing to admire; in internal alchemy, purposeful firing is only a last resort to restore the frail and aged, requiring a calm, gentle heart throughout

About This Chapter

On the surface, Chapter 31 of the Daodejing discusses the art of warfare, yet its true heart lies in restraint and opposition to war: weapons are ominous instruments that bring disaster; in daily life a noble person honors the left, and only in war shifts honor to the right; even when forced to fight and victorious, one must never treat killing as glory, but meet victory with the grief of a funeral.

As usual, Huang Yuanji unfolds his commentary on two levels:

  1. Governing the world: When a sage governs all under heaven, the Way and Virtue come first, administration and education second, and armed force is only a last resort. Even when compelled to march out against tyranny and protect the people, the army maintains strict discipline, makes no boast of martial might, and keeps a serene, detached mind; after victory, seeing the people displaced and slain, the ruler feels only sorrow and never parades slaughter as a glorious achievement.
  2. Internal alchemy: Here lies the chapter's most penetrating turn—Huang Yuanji compares "wielding arms" directly to the alchemist's purposeful practice of "approaching the furnace and applying fire" to harvest and refine. Advancing the yang fire, withdrawing the yin tally, and intense simmering and rapid refining are not the natural norm of the Great Way; they are expedients designed to "support frailty and remedy depletion" in those grown old, weak, and heavily steeped in acquired habits. Just as a dirty house must be swept before welcoming a guest, purposeful effort serves only to clear away impurities. Even when one must apply purposeful fire phasing, the heart must remain calm, detached, and gentle, lest agitated mundane fire flare up and injure inner nature and life.

Safety note: In its alchemical section, this chapter mentions traditional fire phasing terms such as "intense simmering and rapid refining" and "advancing the fire and withdrawing the tally," yet Huang Yuanji immediately warns that clinging to forceful firing easily stirs up agitated Posterior Heaven mundane fire, harming body and mind. Daily quiet sitting should prioritize natural relaxation and tranquil detachment—never hold the breath, push forcefully, or try to coerce bodily sensations.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：夫佳兵者，不祥之器，物或恶之，故有道者不处。是以君子居则贵左，用兵则贵右。兵者，不祥之器，非君子之器，不得已而用之，恬淡为上，胜而不美。而美之者，是乐杀人也。夫乐杀人者，不可得志于天下矣。故吉事尚左，凶事尚右。是以偏将军处左，上将军处右，以丧礼处之。杀人众多，以悲哀泣之；战胜，以丧礼处之。（佳者，利也。）

The Supreme Patriarch says: Sharp, finely wrought weapons are instruments of ill omen; all creatures detest them, and therefore one who possesses the Way does not dwell among them. Accordingly, in ordinary life the noble person honors the left, whereas in wielding arms he honors the right.

Weapons are instruments of ill omen, not the instruments of a noble person. Only when left with no alternative does one employ them, placing tranquil detachment above all else; even in victory one does not regard it as something beautiful. To admire victory as beautiful is to take delight in killing human beings. And one who delights in killing can never fulfill his aspirations in the world.

Therefore, on auspicious occasions the left is esteemed, and on inauspicious occasions the right is esteemed. In military formation, the lieutenant general takes his place on the left while the commanding general stands on the right—which is to conduct war according to the rites of mourning. When many have been slain, one weeps over them in grief and sorrow; even when a battle is won, one treats it with the rites of a funeral. (Note: jia here means sharp and finely wrought.)

§2
Original (Chinese)

圣人之治天下也，道德为上，政教次之。至不得已而兴征伐之师，备干戈之用，「长子帅师，弟子舆尸」，为贞为凶，《易》所深戒也。而况逞虎视之雄，奋鹰扬之烈，耀兵革于疆场，肆威武于边鄙，以侵伐为利用，以争战为能事者乎？如此用兵，非弭乱也，实佳兵也。夫佳兵者，不祥之器。古人以止戈为武，此则以穷兵为能，非君子常用之器也。君子常用之器为何？道也，德也，好生恶杀也。若言兵，则杀机见矣。夫杀伐声张，河山震动，虽鸡犬亦为之不安，惨何极乎？况蚯蚓尚且贪生，蝼蚁亦知畏死，物之至微至蠢者犹深恶之，何论乎人？是以有道之士，不屑处也。凡物贵阳而贱阴，左为阳，生气也；右为阴，杀机也。是以君子之居，平常尚左，独至用兵之际，则不尚左而尚右，其贱兵可知矣。就令除残去暴，伐罪吊民，悬正正之旗，布堂堂之阵，要属不祥之器，圣王所不乐耳。

When a sage governs all under heaven, the Way and Virtue come first, with governance and moral instruction second. Even when compelled with no alternative to raise an expeditionary army and take up shields and halberds, the Book of Changes (Hexagram Shi, "The Army") already delivers a solemn warning: "When the seasoned elder leads the army, it is upright; if untried juniors meddle, they bring back corpses in carts—misfortune." If even legitimate warfare demands such extreme caution, what then of those who flaunt a tiger's fierce glare, whip up a hawk's swooping fury, parade arms and armor on the battlefield, display raw martial might along the borders, treat invasion as a handy instrument, and boast of waging war as their highest skill? Using armies like this is not quelling disorder—it is sheer infatuation with sharp weapons.

Sharp, finely wrought weapons are instruments of ill omen. When the ancients formed the character wu ("martial," 武) by combining "stop" (止) and "halberd" (戈), they meant that true martial prowess lies in stopping war; treating endless warmongering as prowess is never the instrument a noble person uses in daily life. What instruments does a noble person rely on? The Way, Virtue, and a heart that cherishes life and detests killing. The very mention of arms already reveals an impulse to kill. Once the clamor of war breaks out, rivers and mountains tremble, and even chickens and dogs in ordinary homes know no peace—how extreme is that tragedy! Even earthworms cling to life and mole crickets and ants fear death; when the tiniest, simplest creatures deeply abhor slaughter, how much more do human beings? Therefore a person of the Way disdains to dwell in such affairs.

Throughout the world, yang is honored and yin is held lower: the left belongs to yang, the breath of generation; the right belongs to yin, the impulse of killing. Thus in ordinary life the noble person honors the left, yet on the single occasion of going to war he shifts honor from left to right—making clear how lowly the ancients held military force. Even if you march out to uproot cruelty, punish the guilty, and console the suffering populace—unfurled beneath banners of strict rectitude and arrayed in open, stately ranks—weapons remain in the end instruments of ill omen, something a sage-king takes no pleasure in using.

§3
Original (Chinese)

夫国家承平，固无需乎武备。一旦边陲告急，畔乱频生，万不得已而用兵，亦惟是步伍整齐，赏罚严肃。凡师行之处，乐供壶浆，兵过之乡，仍安耕凿，所谓「克柔克刚，以威以德」者，于此可验矣。不逞兵威，不夸将略，惟是恬淡无为，从容自得，虽处戎马纷争之地，俨具步武安祥之风。以此攻城，何城不克？以此制敌，何敌不摧？其胜有必然者。虽然，其胜也亦兵家之常，乌得谓钟鼎铭勋，旗常纪绩，遂以此为后世美观乎？倘以此为美观，是必忍万姓之荼毒，博一己之功名，无生人之德，而有杀人之心，亦奚可哉？夫乐杀人者，其心残忍，其法森严，不能大度以容人，常苛刻而自是，斯人也，不可得志于天下，如得志于天下，苍生无遗类矣。古者吉事尚左，凶事尚右。彼偏将军，将之次也，反居其左；上将军，将之上也，转居其右。亦知专杀伐之权者，为上将军，而偏将军必禀命于其上，不得逞杀伐之威，是以丧礼处军礼矣。夫岂若国书对垒，命士卒咸歌送葬之词也哉！此谨慎小心之至也。又曰「杀人众多，以悲哀泣之」者何？明战伐之事，伤彼苍好生之心，实出于无可奈何。故吊古战场者，睹此尸满城濠，血盈沟壑，天地一若含愁，草木一若生悲，而况于人乎？即使战而胜，群酋率服，万姓乂安，而反己思维，觉宇下苍苍赤子，遭锋镝而流离者半，死亡者亦半，心滋戚矣，何敢以奏凯还朝，歌功颂德，而自炫其才能耶？念及此而毫无德色，反多戚容，仍以丧礼处之而已矣。孟子曰：「我善为陈，我善为战，大罪也；惟国君好仁，天下无敌焉。」又曰：「威天下不以兵革之利。」足见神武不杀，仁者无敌，允为治世之良模。而用兵，非圣人之常道，王者所不贵也。

When a state enjoys peace, there is naturally no need for warlike preparations. Should the frontier suddenly face emergency and rebellions break out everywhere, leaving no alternative but to deploy troops, the sole aim is orderly ranks and impartial, strict rewards and punishments. Wherever the army marches, the people gladly bring out food and drink to welcome it; in the villages through which it passes, farmers go on tilling fields and digging wells in peace. The ancient saying "capable of gentleness and firmness alike, combining awe with virtue" is truly verified here. Making no display of military intimidation and boasting nothing of tactical genius, maintaining only tranquil detachment, non-action, and unhurried composure—even amid the clash of warhorses, one carries the serene bearing of measured, peaceful steps. With such an army, what walled city cannot be taken? What foe cannot be broken? Victory follows as a matter of course.

Even so, winning a battle is merely the ordinary duty of a commander; how could anyone engrave his exploits on bronze bells and tripods or inscribe them on royal banners and hold that up as a splendid spectacle for posterity? To regard victory as something splendid means hardening one's heart to the bitter suffering of ten thousand families just to buy personal fame—possessing no virtue that nurtures life, only a mind bent on killing. How could that ever be acceptable? Anyone who delights in killing has a cruel heart and harsh, merciless laws; lacking magnanimity to bear with others, he is constantly severe, arrogant, and self-righteous. Such a person cannot fulfill his will in the world—and if he ever did gain sway over it, not a single survivor would remain among the common people.

In antiquity, auspicious rites honored the left and inauspicious rites honored the right. The lieutenant general, lower in rank, stood on the left; the supreme commander, highest in rank, stood instead on the right. Why? Because full authority over killing belonged to the supreme commander, whereas the lieutenant had to await orders from above and could not unleash violence on his own—this arranged military protocol on the model of funeral rites. This is nothing like the Qi general Guo Shu facing his enemy in battle and ordering his soldiers to chant dirges as a fatalistic wager; rather, it expresses the utmost caution and reverence for life.

Why does the classic add, "When many have been slain, weep over them in grief and sorrow"? To show that warfare wounds Heaven's own love of life and is undertaken only under bitter compulsion. Thus anyone who visits an ancient battlefield and beholds corpses filling the moats and blood flooding the ravines feels as though heaven and earth themselves were shrouded in grief and grass and trees wept in sorrow—how much more a feeling human being! Even if the war ends in victory, enemy chieftains submit, and the people settle back in peace, when the ruler reflects quietly within himself and realizes that beneath heaven's vault, half the innocent children of the land were scattered by blades and arrows and the other half perished, his heart grows heavier still. How could he dare march back to court to victory anthems, listening to hymns of praise and parading his prowess? Thinking of this, he shows not the slightest look of self-congratulation, only a sorrowful countenance, meeting victory still with the solemn heart of a funeral. Mencius said, "To boast, 'I am skilled at arraying troops; I am skilled at waging war,' is a grave crime; only when a ruler loves humaneness has he no enemy under heaven." He also said, "One does not awe the world through the sharpness of weapons and armor." Clearly, possessing awesome strength without resorting to slaughter—so that the humane have no rival—is the true model for governing the world. Waging war is never the sage's constant Way, nor something a true king prizes.

§4
Original (Chinese)

此喻临炉用火，实为老弱之人，扶衰救弊，不得已而为之，何敢矜奇立异，自诩为功耶？彼旁门左道，以进火退符，采药炼丹，一切有作有为之法，视为神仙之道，误矣，远矣！然少壮之体，不须采炼之工，可以得药结丹，而衰老之躯，气质物欲，濡染已久，不加猛烹急炼之功，则气质不化，物欲难除，以污浊之身而欲行无为自然之道，安可得乎？是犹屋宇不洁，嘉宾难迎。人须扫除身中污垢，而后色相俱空，尘根悉拔，本来真性自在个中。虽然勉强修持，亦要安然自在，方不动后天凡火，有伤性命，故太上以恬淡为上，胜而不美。否则有后天而无先天，仅凡气而无真气，一腔火性，其能久耶？故曰：美之者，是以杀人为乐也。以杀人为乐，则杀机满腹，乌足为天下之主，受天下之福？其不可得志于天下也必矣。是知修炼之士，虽用作为工夫，亦要有仁慈恻怛、谦下柔和之心，斯后天中方有先天。古人火候无爻策，药物无斤两，顺天而动，率性以行，虽有作为，亦不为害也。

Transferred to inner cultivation, Laozi is speaking in metaphor: the alchemist's purposeful work of "approaching the furnace and applying fire" is just like waging war—it is devised specifically for the aged and frail whose primordial vitality is depleted, to shore up decline and remedy imbalances. It is undertaken solely because there is no other choice; how could one dare boast of marvels and parade it as a personal triumph?

Those followers of heterodox side-paths mistake advancing the yang fire, withdrawing the yin tally, harvesting the Medicine, refining the elixir, and every sort of deliberate, contrived technique for the Great Way of the immortals itself—what a grievous error, and how far from the truth! Yet on the other hand, while a youthful, vigorous body whose vitality is unbroken needs no deliberate harvesting and refining to obtain the Medicine and crystallize the Elixir through quiet nourishment, an aging frame has been steeped far too long in biased temperament and worldly desires. Without applying vigorous work of "intense simmering and rapid refining," stubborn temperament cannot be transformed and clinging desires cannot be cleared away; carrying an impure body and mind while talking idly of effortless non-action—how could one ever reach it? It is like a house piled with dust and filth: an honored guest cannot possibly be welcomed inside. One must first sweep every trace of grime out of body and mind; once all attachment to form and appearances is emptied and every root of worldly dust pulled out, one's original True Nature reveals itself naturally within.

Even so, during the stage where one must exert deliberate effort, the heart must remain calm, unhurried, and at ease—only then will one avoid stirring up agitated Posterior Heaven mundane fire that scorches and injures inner nature and life. That is why the Supreme Patriarch insists on "tranquil detachment above all; victorious, yet not admiring it." Otherwise, one ends up with only Posterior Heaven exertion and no Prior Heaven vitality, stirring up only coarse mundane breath and no True Breath—a belly full of parched, restless fire. How could such a body endure for long?

Hence the classic says, "To admire victory as beautiful is to delight in killing." In cultivation it is the same: someone obsessed with violent firing and chasing intense sensations has a belly brimming with destructive harshness—how could he be fit to master his own body and mind and enjoy tranquil blessings? Such practice is guaranteed to fail. From this we understand that even when a cultivator employs purposeful techniques for a season, he must always keep a heart of compassion, tenderness, humility, and gentleness; only then does Prior Heaven vitality emerge from within Posterior Heaven effort. As the ancients said, "fire phasing has no fixed hexagram counts, and the medicinal ingredients have no rigid weights." Moving in accord with Heaven's timing and acting in harmony with inner nature, even purposeful effort brings no harm.

Key Terms

  • Sharp weapons (jiabing): finely honed arms; by extension, glorifying military force and fierce aggression. On the alchemical level, Huang Yuanji uses this as a metaphor for forced, aggressive, purposeful firing.
  • fire phasing: the alchemical pacing of Spirit, intention, and breathing—adjusting tightness and ease, gentle and vigorous heat. This chapter stresses that "fire phasing has no fixed hexagram counts," rejecting rigid mechanical schedules or forced heating.
  • Advancing the fire and withdrawing the tally (jinhuo tuifu): an alchemical term for the purposeful phases of raising yang ("advancing yang fire") and lowering yin ("withdrawing the yin tally") during the minor Celestial Circuit or daily refinement.
  • Posterior Heaven mundane fire (houtian fanhuo): dry, agitated heat stirred up by impatience, greed for results, or forceful mental pushing—unlike the clear, harmonious True Fire of Prior Heaven, it readily harms body and mind.
  • Prior Heaven / Posterior Heaven: this chapter points out that although an aging body must borrow Posterior Heaven purposeful work to sweep away accumulated impurities, only by keeping a calm, gentle heart can "Prior Heaven emerge within Posterior Heaven."
  • Medicine: in internal alchemy, the Prior Heaven vitality (True Essence and True Breath) born naturally from deep stillness. This chapter states that "the Medicine has no rigid weights," warning against clinging to fixed numbers.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Restraint shows deeper steadiness than a display of force. Whether handling affairs or communicating with others, holding back one's sharp edge, acting only when truly necessary, and refraining from gloating afterward are the marks of genuine grounding.
  2. Means are always means—never mistake the tool for the goal. Wellness methods, breathing exercises, and meditation techniques are all like "sweeping the house": their purpose is to make body and mind clear and quiet, not to obsess over the techniques themselves or show them off.
  3. Clear out agitation before anything else in practice. If quiet sitting or breath work leaves you restless, tight-chested, overheated, or impatient for dramatic sensations, you have stirred up "mundane fire"—relax immediately, step back, and return to tranquil ease.
  4. Bring compassion and gentleness to self-discipline. Breaking entrenched habits and building discipline indeed require a touch of "intense simmering" resolve, yet the undertone must stay kind and patient toward yourself and others; excessive harshness only wounds the body and spoils the work.

Translator's Notes

  1. "When the seasoned elder leads the army, untried juniors bring back corpses in carts" (zhangzi shuaishi, dizi yushi): from the sixth-five line of Hexagram Shi ("The Army") in the Book of Changes, warning that command must be entrusted solely to a seasoned, virtuous general; letting impulsive juniors interfere leads to defeat and cartloads of the dead.
  2. "Stopping the halberd is the meaning of martial" (zhige weiwu): from the Zuo Commentary (12th year of Duke Xuan), where King Zhuang of Chu explains the graph 武 as composed of 止 ("stop") and 戈 ("halberd"), arguing that the true purpose of martial power is to end war rather than glorify slaughter.
  3. "Engraving exploits on bronze bells and tripods, inscribing deeds on royal banners": ancient rulers commemorated great ministers' achievements by casting inscriptions on bronze bells and ritual tripods (zhongding) or writing them on the sun-and-moon royal standard (qichang).
  4. "Guo Shu facing his enemy and ordering his troops to chant dirges": an allusion to the Battle of Ailing in the Zuo Commentary (11th year of Duke Ai). Before the decisive clash between Qi and Wu, the Qi general Gongsun Xia under commander Guo Shu ordered his soldiers to sing the funeral dirge Yubin to display fatalistic resolve; Qi suffered a crushing defeat and Guo Shu was killed. Huang Yuanji cites this to contrast Laozi's "treating victory with funeral rites," which reflects utmost caution and reverence for life rather than reckless fatalism.
  5. Two quotations from Mencius: "To boast, 'I am skilled at arraying troops; I am skilled at waging war,' is a grave crime..." is from Mencius 7B (Jinxin II), where chen (陈) stands for zhen ("battle array"); "One does not awe the world through the sharpness of weapons and armor" is from Mencius 2B (Gongsun Chou II).
  6. Textual collation: In the classic text, "inauspicious occasions honor the right" (shangyou, 尚右) was misprinted as 尚有 in the base edition, and "weep in grief and sorrow" (bei'ai, 悲哀) as 悲衰; both are corrected against standard editions and Huang's commentary below. Several obvious scribal errors in the commentary (such as 旗常, 荼毒, 一己, 乂安, 矜奇立异, 彼旁门, 采药, 屋宇) have likewise been emended from context.
