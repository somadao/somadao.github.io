Chapter 33 · Knowing Others Is Wisdom

Knowing others and knowing oneself belong to illuminating mind and seeing nature, while overcoming others and overcoming oneself lie in refining the self and establishing life; knowing sufficiency to stop the fire and persevering in warm nourishment bring enduring survival beyond death

About This Chapter

Chapter 33 of the Daodejing consists of just eight brief clauses, every one a timeless maxim: "Knowing others is wisdom; knowing oneself is clarity. Overcoming others is strength; overcoming oneself is true power. Knowing sufficiency is wealth; pressing forward with vigor is resolve. Not losing one's place is endurance; dying without perishing is longevity." Most readers take it as a string of moral aphorisms, yet Huang Yuanji reads in it a complete map of the Golden Elixir from start to finish:

  1. Cultivating inner nature (knowing others, knowing oneself): Understanding the Heavenly Principle at the source of human life is "wisdom"; sweeping away selfish desires to behold one's original face directly is "clarity"—this is the work of illuminating the mind and seeing inner nature.
  2. Cultivating life and refining the self (overcoming others, overcoming oneself): Conquering external material desires is merely "strength"; peeling away layer after layer of Posterior Heaven yin temperament until body and mind shine pure as jade is true "overcoming oneself."
  3. fire phasing and completion (knowing sufficiency, pressing forward, not losing one's place, dying without perishing): Knowing when the fire is full so as to practice stopping the fire and warm nourishment is "knowing sufficiency is wealth"; continuing that warm nourishment steadily and seamlessly without a single stray thought is "pressing forward with vigor" and "not losing one's place"; and finally, when the physical form falls away while the true Spirit endures whole and intact, that is "dying without perishing is longevity."

Safety note: This chapter discusses stopping the fire, the three transmutations (refining Essence into Breath, Breath into Spirit), and deep absorption in warm nourishment, stressing that "failing to stop the fire when the fire is full brings peril instead." Everyday practitioners should never cling to intense energy sensations or force themselves through grueling sessions; once mind feels clear, breath feels full, and body feels calmly at ease, relax naturally into gentle rest and know when to stop.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：知人者智，自知者明，胜人者有力，自胜者强，知足者富，强行者有志，不失其所者久，死而不亡者寿。

The Supreme Patriarch says: One who knows others has wisdom; one who knows himself has clarity. One who overcomes others has strength; one who overcomes himself has true power. One who knows sufficiency is wealthy; one who presses forward with vigor has resolve. One who does not lose his place endures; one who dies without perishing has true longevity.

§2
Original (Chinese)

修身之道，不外性命。人欲尽性立命，必先存心养性，保命全形，于以修之炼之，积之累之，则本性长圆，天命在我矣。然欲尽心，必先知性。知得人生之本，纯乎天理，不杂人欲，谓之睿智。由此遏欲存理，时时省察，刻刻防闲，务令私欲尽净，天理流行，洞见本来面目，惺惺不昧，了了常昭，即是圆明妙觉。此非外面之想像，乃自家之真知，他人莫能喻也，故曰「知人者智，自知者明」。若欲立命，必先炼己。炼己有两端：一曰物欲，物欲不除，天真难现，舍此而欲得药结丹，亦犹嘉谷杂荑稗之中，不先芟夷，势必苗莠并植，非先胜人欲，常操常存，则有定守，未必有定力也，故曰「胜人者有力」。一曰气质，气质不化，身何由固？所以剥肤存液，剥液存神，剥神还虚，层层剥尽，方能与道合真。苟非精固气壮，能战退群阴，扫除六贼，致令一身内外精莹如玉，变化凡躯，炼成仙体哉？故曰「自胜者强」。

The Way of self-cultivation comes down to nothing other than inner nature and life. Whoever wishes to fulfill inner nature and establish life must first preserve the mind and nourish nature, safeguard life and keep the form whole—cultivating and refining step by step, accumulating merit and work day by day—until original nature remains forever round and complete and Heavenly destiny rests in one's own hands.

Yet to exhaust the full potential of the mind, one must first know inner nature. Realizing that the root of human life is pure Heavenly Principle, unmixed with selfish human desires, is called penetrating wisdom. Starting from this understanding, one curbs desire and preserves principle, examining oneself at every hour and keeping watch at every moment, striving until selfish desires are washed completely clean, Heavenly Principle flows freely, and one sees straight through to one's original face—wide awake and unclouded, lucid and forever radiant. That is round, bright, wondrous awakening. It is no external conjecture spun in the head, but intimate self-knowledge that no outsider can taste for you. Thus the classic says, "One who knows others has wisdom; one who knows himself has clarity."

As for establishing life, one must first pass through the gate of refining the self, which has two dimensions:

The first is material desire. If material desire is not cleared away, innate purity cannot appear. Trying to obtain the Medicine and crystallize the Elixir without first clearing desire is like good grain growing amid tare and darnel: if you do not uproot the weeds first, crop and weeds inevitably crowd together. Therefore one must first overcome acquired human desires, holding firm and keeping watch constantly—otherwise, even if you know the rules, you will lack genuine steadiness when tested. This is what the classic means by "One who overcomes others has strength" (overcoming human desires shows real strength).

The second is constitutional temperament. If one's biased, turbid temperament is not transformed, how can the body ever become truly firm and enduring? Hence one peels away layer after layer like stripping bamboo shoots: peeling away coarse attachment to flesh to preserve luminous fluid; peeling away attachment to fluid to preserve Spirit; peeling away even attachment to Spirit to return to Emptiness—stripping every layer clean until one merges with the reality of the Way. Unless one's Essence is rock-solid and one's Breath is vigorous, how could one drive back the hordes of yin dross, sweep out the "six thieves" (the six senses chasing external objects), render the entire body crystal-clear as fine jade inside and out, and transmute a mortal frame into an immortal body? Only one who conquers his own ingrained temperament truly earns the words, "One who overcomes himself has true power."

§3
Original (Chinese)

如是性已了矣，命已立矣，功不于此尽乎？道不于此成乎？虽然，起火有时，止火有候。若当火足之时，不行止火之功，精必随气之动而动，故知止养丹，如贫者之积财而富，常觉有余。既知止火，尤要进火以养丹，退火以温丹。非有志修士，断不能绵绵密密，不贰不息如此也。《易》曰：「天行健，君子以自强不息。」其即此「强行者有志」之谓欤？自此温养之后，但安神息，一任天然，无一时一刻之失所，子思子谓「至诚无息，不息则久」者此也。至若凡身脱化，真灵飞升，亦犹凡人之死。但凡人之死，死则神散；而圣人之死，死犹神完，形虽死而神如生，乌得不与天地同寿耶？

At this point, since inner nature is fulfilled and life is established, is the work not finished and the Way accomplished? Not so fast! Cultivation has its strict timing: lighting the fire has its proper hour, and stopping the fire has its exact moment. If, when the fire has reached fullness, one fails to practice stopping the fire, the Essence so carefully gathered will inevitably stir and disperse again alongside the agitated movement of Breath. Therefore, knowing when to stop and quietly nourish the Elixir is like a poor person steadily laying away earnings until he grows rich, always feeling an ample surplus—this is "One who knows sufficiency is wealthy."

Having learned when to stop the fire, one must further master the delicate rhythm of warm nourishment: gently advancing subtle fire when the Elixir needs feeding, and withdrawing heat to keep it softly warm. Without deep, unwavering resolve, a cultivator could never sustain such continuous, seamless, single-minded perseverance. As the Book of Changes says, "Heaven's movement is ever vigorous; thus the noble person strengthens himself without ceasing"—does this not speak precisely of "One who presses forward with vigor has resolve"?

After this long period of warm nourishment, one simply lets Spirit and Breath rest quietly together, leaving all to natural creation without straying from one's true home for a single instant. This is what Master Zisi meant by "Utmost sincerity never ceases; never ceasing, it endures"—which explains "One who does not lose his place endures." Finally, when the mortal body is shed and the true Spirit transcends, outwardly it looks much like ordinary human death. Yet when an ordinary person dies, his Spirit scatters; when a sage departs, his Spirit remains whole and complete. Though the physical form dies, Spirit lives on undiminished—how could he not share the longevity of Heaven and Earth?

§4
Original (Chinese)

此云知人道，胜人欲，犹是穷理尽性一边之学，惟性见心明，洞彻本原，神强气壮，煅尽阴滓，始能了性而立命，性命不分两途，复还于混沌未开之天，而阴神尽灭，阳神完成矣。其间炼精化气，炼气化神，尚有止火养丹。《悟真》云：「若也持盈未已心，不免一朝遭殆辱。」此之谓也。夫炼精化气，为入胎之始；炼气化神，为成胎之终。不知止火，则气不入于胎，精虽炼而为气，犹可因气之动而复化为精；且不知止火，则神不凝于虚空，气虽炼而成神，犹可因神之动而复化为气。故「知足常足，终身不辱」，太上之言，非欺我也。至若神归大定，气亦因之大定，百年之久，浑同一日，一念游移，即同走丹。如此任重道远，非强行有志者，不能常止其所，历久而不敝也。三昧火化，立上凌霄，虽死犹生，其精神直与天地同寿。金丹始终，尽于此矣。

To sum up: knowing the human Way and overcoming human desires still lean mainly toward the side of fathoming principle and cultivating mind-nature. Only when nature is directly seen and the mind is luminous—penetrating to the very source of life—while Spirit is strong, Breath is vigorous, and every last trace of yin dross has been smelted away, can one both fulfill nature and establish life. At that point, nature and life are no longer two separate paths; they return together to the undivided Primordial Heaven before chaos parted, shadowy yin consciousness vanishes entirely, and the pure, radiant Yang Spirit is brought to completion.

Throughout this journey—from refining Essence into Breath to refining Breath into Spirit—there stands the crucial juncture of stopping the fire to nourish the Elixir. As the Wuzhen pian warns, "If the heart still cannot stop even when the vessel is full, one will inevitably suffer peril and disgrace one day." That refers to this very point.

Specifically, refining Essence into Breath marks the beginning of entering the embryo, while refining Breath into Spirit marks the completion of the embryo. During refining Essence into Breath, if one does not know when to stop the fire, Breath cannot settle into the embryo—and Essence already refined into Breath will revert back into mundane Essence through the continued agitation of Breath. Likewise, during refining Breath into Spirit, if one does not know when to stop the fire, Spirit cannot condense into pure Emptiness—and Breath already refined into Spirit will disperse back into Breath through the stirring of thought. Thus the Supreme Patriarch's words—"Knowing sufficiency brings constant sufficiency, sparing one disgrace all life long"—do not deceive us in the least.

Once Spirit enters deep absorption, Breath enters deep absorption with it: a hundred years pass as seamlessly as a single day, yet a single wandering thought is like leaking the Elixir. With a burden so heavy and a road so long, only a person of fierce, steadfast resolve can remain anchored in his true place across long years without wearing down. At the end, thoroughly transmuted by the Samādhi True Fire, one rises beyond mortal bounds—alive even in death, one's Spirit matching the lifespan of Heaven and Earth. The entire course of the Golden Elixir, from start to finish, is contained right here.

Key Terms

  • inner nature and life: inner nature (xing) refers to luminous awareness and original essence of mind, while life (ming) refers to vital energy and bodily foundation; this chapter assigns "knowing others / knowing oneself" to cultivating nature and "overcoming others / overcoming oneself" to establishing life, culminating in their unity.
  • refining the self: the foundational starting work of internal alchemy, divided here into two layers—conquering external "material desires" and transforming internal "temperament."
  • Six thieves (liuzei): a traditional Buddhist and Daoist term for the six sense faculties (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body, and thinking mind) chasing external stimuli and draining Spirit.
  • stopping the fire: the critical turning point at which purposeful harvesting and firing cease once the fire is full, shifting into quiet warm nourishment so gathered vitality does not spill back out from overdoing it.
  • the three transmutations: the three progressive stages of internal alchemy—refining Essence into Breath, Breath into Spirit, and Spirit into Emptiness (summarized here as peeling flesh to keep fluid, peeling fluid to keep Spirit, and peeling Spirit to return to Emptiness).
  • Samādhi True Fire: pure yang spiritual fire arising naturally in profound absorption, symbolizing the final perfecting heat that completely smelts away Posterior Heaven yin dross.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Distinguish true self-knowledge from mental speculation. Reading piles of theory is only "external imagination"; catching your own greed, anger, or clinging in the very moment it arises is true "knowing oneself is clarity."
  2. Changing habits takes two steps: cut external temptations, then soften your temperament. Stepping away from draining environments and distractions is "overcoming desire"; patiently grinding down impatience, touchiness, and competitiveness is the deeper work of "transforming temperament."
  3. Everything has its moment to "stop the fire." In work, exercise, and meditation alike, stop and rest while you still feel full and refreshed; forcing that extra twenty percent rarely adds value and usually scatters all the vitality you just built up.
  4. True power is quiet continuity, not bursts of enthusiasm. Setting aside ten steady minutes every day to settle your breath and check your mind does far more to keep you "not losing your place" than occasional marathon sessions.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Good grain growing amid tare and darnel; unless weeded out first, crop and weeds inevitably grow side by side": tibai (荑稗) are wild grasses resembling grain, shanyi (芟夷) means to cut down and clear away, and you (莠) refers to foxtail weeds—a metaphor showing that true nature cannot flourish unless desires are cleared first.
  2. "Heaven's movement is ever vigorous; thus the noble person strengthens himself without ceasing": from the Image Commentary (Xiangzhuan) to Hexagram Qian in the Book of Changes.
  3. "Utmost sincerity never ceases; never ceasing, it endures": from Chapter 26 of the Doctrine of the Mean.
  4. Quotation from the Wuzhen pian: "If the heart still cannot stop even when the vessel is full, one will inevitably suffer peril and disgrace one day" comes from a seven-character quatrain in Zhang Boduan's Folio on Awakening to Reality, warning practitioners to know when to stop the fire rather than press ahead out of greed.
  5. Textual collation: Minor scribal errors in the base edition—不味 for 不昧, 荷非 for 苟非, 故日 for 故曰, 不式不息 for 不贰不息, 日 for 曰, and 阴泽 for 阴滓—have been emended according to context.
