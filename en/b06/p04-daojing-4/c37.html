Chapter 37 · The Way Forever Acts Without Striving

Planting the root in non-action and guiding function with purposeful action, forgetting even the Nameless Uncarved Block; in internal alchemy, resetting the furnace after the Jade Fluid Elixir to melt away cleverness and sharp edges

About This Chapter

Chapter 37 closes the Classic of the Way (Daojing), capping the first half of the Daodejing in barely thirty-odd characters: "The Way forever acts without striving, yet leaves nothing undone." If rulers hold fast to this constant Way, the myriad things transform of themselves; if desire stirs after transformation, one calms it with the "Nameless Uncarved Block"; and in the end, even the wish to hold onto the Nameless Uncarved Block dissolves—free of desire and completely still, the entire world naturally sets itself right.

Huang Yuanji interprets "non-action" (wuwei) and the "Nameless Uncarved Block" with rare precision, guarding equally against dead emptiness and spiritual vanity:

  1. Governing the world: Non-action is never the "dead emptiness" of doing nothing at all; rather, it means "planting the root in non-action and guiding function with purposeful action"—stillness and movement as one, forever tranquil yet forever awake. When prosperity breeds restless desires, the sage steadies human hearts with the pure, uncontrived "Nameless Uncarved Block," until even the trace of "I am transforming the people through simplicity" vanishes, reaching supreme harmony where neither ruler nor subjects feel forced.
  2. Internal alchemy: Here lies the chapter's sharpest warning to practitioners. Huang Yuanji likens "lords and kings" to the sovereign human mind-body and "the myriad things" to the five senses, limbs, blood, breath, and spirit. When early cultivation bears fruit—energy surges, clarity sharpens, and the Jade Fluid Elixir takes form—practitioners are most prone to "desire stirring after transformation": boasting of foreseeing past and future to display psychic powers, or craving worldly glory to parade their cleverness! All of this comes from immature fire phasing and untransformed temperament. One must set up the furnace and cauldron anew, cast even the "Nameless Uncarved Block" itself back into the fire to melt away completely, and forget both cleverness and desire—only then does one return to the One Breath of Prior Heaven.

Safety note: This chapter specifically warns that when quiet practice first shows results—bringing a clearer mind and brisker energy—it is very easy to slip into arrogance or an itch to boast of "foresight" and "special powers" ("desire stirring after transformation"). At such times, pulling in one's sharp edges and returning to plain simplicity is essential—never mythologize yourself.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：道常无为而无不为。侯王若能守，万物皆自化。化而欲作，吾将镇之以无名之朴。无名之朴，亦将不欲。不欲以静，天下将自正。

The Supreme Patriarch says: The Way forever acts without striving, yet leaves nothing undone. If lords and kings can guard it, the myriad things will all transform of themselves. If, after transforming, desire begins to stir, I will calm it with the Nameless Uncarved Block. Even the Nameless Uncarved Block itself will become free of desire. Free of desire and therefore still, all under heaven will naturally set itself right.

§2
Original (Chinese)

道虽自然无为，然著于无为，又成顽空之学，须以无为植其本，有为端其用，无为而有为，有为仍无为，斯体立而用行，道全而德备矣。所谓「常应常静」、「常寂常惺」、「放之则弥纶六合，卷之则潜伏寸衷」，即此冲漠无朕之时，有此坐照无遗之概。虽曰无为，而有为寓其中；虽曰有为，而无为赅其内，斯大道在我，大本常存。任尊贵王侯，若无此道为根本，则万物皆隔阂而难化。惟能持守此道，则天下人物，性情相感，声气相通，自默化潜移，而太平有象矣。虽然，承平日久，古道难敦，此亦情所必至，理有固然，无足怪也。及创造频仍，繁华举起，人心愈险，祸乱弥多，此又天地之气数，人所不能逃者。惟圣人具保泰持盈之法，久安长治之谋，于文物初开之世，而以无为无作、无思无虑、浑然无名之太朴，为之修诸己而措诸民，导于前而引于后，纯乎天，不杂以人，所以内镇宫庭，外镇天下。《屯》之初九曰：「盘桓，利居贞」，为草昧初开者之一镇也。夫石蕴玉而山辉，水怀珠而川媚，凡朴之镇，犹且如此，况无名之朴，合民物而一，为之镇乎？倘不归浑穆，断难使会极归极，咸登衽席之安。惟不识不知，顺帝之则，浑忘道德，不识天人，斯为得之。故曰：「无名之朴，亦将不欲，不欲以静，天下自正。」此殆恬淡无欲，郅治无为，上不知所为化，下不知所为应，上与下两相安于无为之道，有不知其然而然者。舜之无为而治，所以独隆千古也。为民上者，可不以无为为本哉？

Though the Way is naturally without striving, clinging rigidly to "non-action" and ignoring everything collapses into the teaching of "dead emptiness." Instead, one must plant the root in non-action and guide its function with purposeful action—acting within non-action, and remaining unstriving even while acting. Then substance stands firm and function moves smoothly, and both the Way and Virtue are complete. What the ancients called "forever responding yet forever still," "forever tranquil yet forever wide awake," and "unrolled, it fills the six directions; rolled up, it hides away inside a square inch of heart" describes precisely this: right in the vast, quiet, traceless void sits a lucid presence that illuminates everything without omission. Call it non-action, yet purposeful action dwells inside it; call it purposeful action, yet non-action encompasses it wholly. Then the Great Way abides in oneself and the great root stands unshaken.

No matter how exalted a king or lord may be, without this Way as his root, all things under heaven remain estranged and impervious to transformation. Only by steadfastly guarding this Way do people and creatures everywhere resonate in feeling and communicate in spirit, silently transformed until the visage of Great Peace naturally appears.

Even so, after long peace, ancient simplicity grows hard to preserve—that is an inevitable human tendency and a natural law, nothing to marvel at. Once clever inventions multiply and luxury flourishes, human hearts grow ever more treacherous and disasters ever more frequent; this too belongs to the turning cycles of heaven and earth, which ordinary people cannot escape. Only the sage possesses the art of preserving harmony and holding fullness, the foresight for enduring peace: at the very dawn of refined civilization, he takes the uncontrived, uncalculating, undivided "Nameless Uncarved Block," refines it first in himself and then extends it to the people—leading ahead and guiding behind, purely in accord with Heaven and unmixed with human artifice—thereby steadying the court within and calming the realm without.

The first yang line of Hexagram Zhun ("Difficulty at the Beginning") in the Book of Changes says: "Hesitating and pausing, it is beneficial to remain steadfast and upright"—a calming anchor offered at the raw dawn of a new era. Think of it: when a hillside holds jade inside its rock, the whole mountain glows; when a river cradles pearls beneath its surface, the whole stream looks radiant. If even an ordinary uncarved gem brings such steadiness and splendor, how much more the Nameless Uncarved Block uniting people and things as one to anchor the realm! Unless the age returns to that deep, wholesome simplicity, it is impossible to draw all hearts back to the true standard and bring everyone safely to peaceful rest.

Only by realizing the Classic of Poetry's ideal—"without scheming, without calculating, naturally following Heaven's pattern"—completely forgetting even the labels "Way" and "Virtue" and no longer dividing "Heaven" from "humanity," does one truly attain it. Hence the classic says: "Even the Nameless Uncarved Block itself will become free of desire; free of desire and therefore still, all under heaven will naturally set itself right." This is the summit of tranquil desirelessness and effortless governance: those above do not feel they are actively transforming anyone, those below do not feel they are straining to comply, and ruler and people alike rest effortlessly in the Way of non-action without even knowing why it is so. That is why Emperor Shun's effortless rule stands unmatched across the ages. How could anyone placed over the people fail to make non-action his root?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此论治世之道，无为为本。修身之道，亦不外此。侯王比人之身，至尊至贵。俗云「一劫人身万劫难，既得人身遇已奇」矣，又闻正法，不更美乎？于此不修，则精神必耗，身命难延，一转眼间，气息泯灭，又不知为鬼为蜮，或兽或禽，轮回六道，辗转不停，何时才得出头？今逢法筵大展，大道宏开，可不急急修持，而令岁月之蹉跎耶？万物比人身中五官百体，血气精神。能守此无为常道，则诸虑自息，百骸俱理，肌肤润泽，毛发晶莹，不啻金相玉质。侯王能守，万物自化，比一心内照，则变化通灵。然火候未纯，气质尚在。当此精神大整，智慧频生，或好谈过去未来以逞其才，或喜语建功立业以夸于世，种种作为，皆由道德未纯之故。惟此玉液丹成，重安炉鼎，再辟乾坤，仍以无名太朴，倾于八卦炉中，内用天然神火，外加增减凡炉，久久火化，连无名之朴亦浑忘焉。此无知无欲，恬然淡然，则凡身变化，自返还于先天一气，而仙道成矣。所谓「不欲以静，天下将自正」者，太上治世修身之道，其一以贯之者欤？

Above we discussed making non-action the root of governing the world; the Way of cultivating oneself is no different. "Lords and kings" symbolize this supremely precious human body. As the common saying goes, "Lose a human body for one kalpa and it takes ten thousand kalpas to regain; simply being born human is already a rare wonder." Now that we have not only gained a human body but also heard the orthodox teaching, is that not doubly blessed? If with such rare fortune we still refuse to cultivate, our Spirit and vitality will inevitably drain away and our lifespan will not endure. In the blink of an eye, breath ceases—and who knows what ghostly or creaturely form awaits, tumbling endlessly through the six realms of rebirth with no day of release in sight? Meeting today a grand Dharma assembly where the Great Way is openly expounded, how could we fail to cultivate earnestly and instead let the precious years slip through our fingers?

"The myriad things" symbolize the five sense organs, hundred bones, blood, breath, and spirit within our own body. When one guards this constant Way of non-action, tangled anxieties quiet down of themselves, every joint and limb falls into harmony, the skin turns moist and glowing, and hair grows bright and lustrous—nothing short of golden radiance and jade-like purity. "If lords and kings can guard it, the myriad things will all transform of themselves" is a metaphor for a single mind shining calmly inward so the entire body transforms and turns nimble and clear.

Yet at this very juncture, one's fire phasing is not yet pure and acquired temperament still lingers. Seeing one's energy surge briskly and sharp insights bubble up one after another, a practitioner easily slips into "desire stirring after transformation": either growing fond of discoursing on past and future to show off cleverness and foresight, or itching to talk grandly of heroic achievements to win worldly applause. All such restless displays stem from one cause—inner Way and Virtue are not yet pure.

Therefore, once the preliminary "Jade Fluid Elixir" is accomplished, one must never stop in complacency; instead, set up the furnace and cauldron anew and open Qian and Kun afresh, pouring this undivided "Nameless Uncarved Block" back into the Eight-Trigram furnace of body and mind. Inside, nourish gently with the natural, uncontrived Spirit-fire; outside, adjust the ordinary fire carefully by adding and reducing according to the timing. Over long, patient tempering, even the very thought of "I am guarding the Nameless Uncarved Block" melts away and is completely forgotten! Reaching this state of pure unknowing and desirelessness, serene and detached, the mortal frame transforms of itself, returning to the One Breath of Prior Heaven—and the Way of the immortals is complete. "Free of desire and therefore still, all under heaven will naturally set itself right"—truly, the Supreme Patriarch's Way of governing the world and his Way of cultivating the self are threaded together by one single principle!

Key Terms

  • Dead emptiness (wankong): clinging rigidly to "thinking nothing and doing nothing," a dry, lifeless blankness devoid of vitality or lucid awareness. Huang Yuanji counters dead emptiness with "planting the root in non-action and guiding function with purposeful action."
  • Forever tranquil yet forever awake (changji changxing): a meditative stillness in which the mind rests unmoving yet stays crystal-clear—neither sunk in torpor nor scattered in restlessness.
  • Nameless Uncarved Block (wuming zhi pu): the unpolished, uncontrived wholeness that cannot be boxed in by labels. In cultivation it first calms newly stirred restless desires, and in the end even attachment to "simplicity" itself must be forgotten.
  • Accomplishment of the Jade Fluid Elixir (yuye dancheng): an initial milestone in internal alchemy (the minor returning elixir), marked by ample vitality, sweet nourishing fluids, and renewed physical and mental vigor—after which one must "reset the furnace and cauldron" to advance toward the Great Golden Fluid Elixir and returning Spirit to Emptiness.
  • fire phasing: here referring to the maturity of a cultivator's temperament refinement, mental regulation, and inner power. When fire phasing is not yet pure, surging energy and insight readily trigger vanity and restlessness.
  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: the single primordial vitality existing before yin and yang split apart. Returning to the One Breath of Prior Heaven means transcending acquired bias and leakage to regain undivided wholeness.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Tell "non-action" apart from passive slacking. Non-action is not lazy withdrawal ("dead emptiness"); it is doing what needs to be done cleanly and steadily from an unanxious, unforced inner stillness.
  2. When things start going well, steady yourself with simplicity. Right when a career picks up, study starts clicking, or meditation leaves you unusually energetic, it is easiest to grow inflated, lecture others, or show off—deliberately cooling down and returning to plain, grounded simplicity prevents most stumbles.
  3. The highest discipline forgets that it is "being disciplined." Advertising how "detached" or "simple" you are means you are still clutching the badge of simplicity; true quiet arrives only when simplicity becomes second nature with nothing left to prove.
  4. Cherish body and mind today instead of postponing cultivation to "someday." Huang Yuanji warns against "letting the years slip away"—nurturing your spirit does not wait for retirement or a mountain hermitage; cutting out a little needless drain and resting quietly each day already tends the root.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Unrolled, it fills the six directions; rolled up, it hides away inside a square inch of heart": adapted from Cheng Yi's remark quoted in Zhu Xi's Collected Commentaries on the Doctrine of the Mean ("Unrolled, it fills the six directions; rolled up, it withdraws into secrecy"). The "six directions" are north, south, east, west, above, and below; the "square inch" is the heart-mind.
  2. "Vast, quiet, and devoid of any visible trace" (chongmo wuzhen): from Cheng Yi's saying, "Vast and quiet without a trace, yet all ten thousand phenomena already stand arrayed within," describing the empty yet all-containing source.
  3. "The first yang line of Hexagram Zhun says: 'Hesitating and pausing, it is beneficial to remain steadfast and upright'": from the Book of Changes, where Zhun symbolizes the tender difficulty of new beginnings and urges calm, upright steadiness rather than rash haste.
  4. "When a hillside holds jade inside its rock, the whole mountain glows; when a river cradles pearls, the whole stream looks radiant": from Lu Ji's Rhapsody on Literature (Wenfu, Western Jin dynasty), originally describing how genuine inner substance makes writing shine, borrowed here for inner simplicity radiating harmony outward.
  5. "Drawing all hearts back to the true standard" (huiji guiji) and "peaceful rest" (renxi zhi an): huiji guiji comes from the Book of Documents ("Great Plan"), signifying universal convergence on the upright center; renxi (sleeping mats) metaphorically denotes safe, settled peacetime life.
  6. "Without scheming, without calculating, naturally following Heaven's pattern": from the Classic of Poetry ("Major Odes," Huangyi), praising effortless alignment with Heaven's order.
  7. "Supreme governance" (zhizhi) and "Dharma assembly" (fayan): zhizhi means ideal peacetime order; fayan originally denotes a lecture seat or Buddhist assembly for expounding the Dharma, here referring to the rare opportunity of hearing the Great Way openly taught.
  8. Textual collation: Base-edition misprints—顾空 for 顽空, 修诸已 for 修诸己, 祝无名之朴 for 况无名之朴, 独隆干古 for 独隆千古, and 法筳 for 法筵—have been corrected from context.
