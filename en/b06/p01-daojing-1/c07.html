Chapter 7 · Heaven Endures, Earth Lasts

Heaven and earth endure because they do not live for themselves, whereas worldly self-seeking drains essence and shortens life; by shedding selfishness and holding impartial fairness in obscurity and eminence alike, the sage places himself last yet stands first, sets himself aside yet abides

About This Chapter

Chapter 7 of the Daodejing might seem at first glance like a worldly maxim of "achieving private interest through selflessness," yet Huang Yuanji aims directly at two of the commonest human fixations—the worldly paradox of "clinging to life and thereby hastening death," and the pseudo-cultivator's "one-sided selfishness":

  1. Why heaven and earth endure (§2, §3): Heaven and earth never hoard vitality for themselves; instead they pour out the One Primordial True Breath without reserve to nourish all living beings. Because creatures unceasingly generate life, heaven and earth remain forever fresh across the ages.
  2. The worldly paradox of "scheming for life yet hastening death" (§2): Worldly people spend all day calculating for their sack of skin, chasing rank, pleasure, and gain—or even greedily hunting longevity tricks—until mental scheming drains Essence dry and weakens Breath; the more they fear death, the faster they burn themselves out.
  3. The sage's selflessness is no affected pose (§2): Placing oneself behind and holding bodily self-interest lightly is not feigned superiority; the sage's heart is genuinely clear and spacious, devoted to sweeping clean the mind-field and cultivating the root of life without fussing over fleshly cravings—and as a result his virtue naturally places him foremost and his spirit endures in harmony with heaven and earth.
  4. Refuting both "mountain quietism" and "worldly scheming" (§3): Huang Yuanji explicitly criticizes two extremes: fleeing into deep mountains to sit like dry wood while ignoring all human duty, and spending one's whole lifeblood calculating advantage amid social affairs. Both are merely forms of "serving one's private self," falling far short of the sage's impartial wholeness—"silent and serene when unoccupied, lucid and alert when affairs arise."

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：天长地久。天地所以能长且久者，以其不自生，故能长生。是以圣人后其身而身先，外其身而身存。非以其无私耶？惟其无私，故能成其私。

Lord Lao says: Heaven endures, and Earth lasts long. Why are heaven and earth able to endure so long? Because they do not exist for their own private life—therefore they can live endlessly. For this reason, the sage places himself behind others, and yet comes to stand foremost; he sets his own person aside, and yet his person is preserved. Is this not because he is selfless? Precisely because he has no private self-seeking, he fulfills his truest aim.

§2
Original (Chinese)

天地之气，浑浩流转，历亿万年而不敝者，皆由一元真宰默运其间，天地所以悠久无疆也。即发育万物，长养群黎，而生生不已，天地亦未尝不足，气机所以亘古不磨也。太上曰：「天长地久」，不诚然哉？然天地之能长且久者，其故何欤？以其不自生也。设有自生之心，则天地有情，天亦老矣。惟不自有其生，而以众生为生，是众生之生生不息，即天地之生生不息也，故曰长生。世人多昧此生生之理，不求生而求死，不求长生而求速死，陷溺于富贵功名，沉沦于声色货利，时时握算，刻刻经营，不数年而精枯气弱，魄散魂飞，费尽千辛，难享一世，营生反以寻死，可胜浩叹！是以圣人法天效地，不惟势利之场不肯驰逐，即延年益算之术亦不贪求，惟以大道为先，净扫心田，精修命蒂，举凡一切养身章身之具，在在不暇营谋，一似后其身、外其身者然，卒之，德立而同类莫超其上，名成而后世犹仰其型，非所谓「后其身而身先，外其身而身存」者乎？视世之自私其身，反戕其生者，诚高出万万倍。而圣人究非矫情立异也，自来情淡是好，清净为怀，不随俗而浮，不依形而立，阔然大公，一似天地之无私者焉。夫人多自私而戚戚于怀，圣无一私而皎皎物外，一片虚灵之象、空洞之神，常照耀而不可稍遏。向使区区以血肉躯、臭皮囊，时刻关心，昼夜系念，又乌能独先而不后、长存而不亡耶？惟其无私，故与天地合撰、日月并明，而能成其私也。后之修道者，欲此身不朽、此神不坏，须用刻苦工夫，摆脱尘垢，久久煅炼，自然干干净净，别有一重天地，另有一番世界，而不与世俗同生死也，何乐如之？

The Breath of heaven and earth rolls on in vast, undivided flow, enduring hundreds of millions of years without decay—solely because the One Primordial Breath presides silently within, rendering heaven and earth boundless in duration. Even while unceasingly nurturing the myriad things and rearing all peoples, heaven and earth never suffer the slightest depletion, and their vital momentum wears not a whit across eternity. When Lord Lao says "Heaven endures and Earth lasts," is that not utterly true?

Yet what is the secret of their endurance? Simply that they "do not live for themselves"—never hoarding vitality for private self-preservation. Suppose heaven and earth harbored a selfish impulse to cling to their own existence: caught in private attachment, even Heaven itself would grow old! Precisely because they never claim life as a private possession, but take the thriving of all creatures as their own life, the unceasing generation of all beings is the very unceasing life of heaven and earth—and thus it is called "long life."

Most people in the world remain blind to this principle of unceasing generation: claiming to fear death, they actively pursue death rather than life, swift ruin rather than longevity. Sinking into wealth, rank, and fame, drowning in sensual indulgences and profit, clutching their abacus every hour and scheming every minute—within a few short years Essence dries up, Breath grows feeble, and ethereal and corporeal souls scatter; after a thousand bitter exertions they can scarcely enjoy one peaceful lifetime! Striving for livelihood while actually hastening their own death—how deeply lamentable!

Therefore the sage models himself on heaven and earth. Not only does he refuse to race through arenas of power and gain, he does not even greedily chase minor arts for extending his years; putting the Great Way first, he sweeps clean the field of his heart and steadily cultivates the root-stem of life (joint cultivation of inner nature and life). As for all the luxuries meant to pamper or adorn the body, he has neither time nor inclination to scheme for them, appearing as though he puts himself last and treats his own body as something external. Yet in the end, his virtue stands so firm that none among his peers can surpass him, and his noble example lives on for generations to revere—is this not "placing himself behind yet standing foremost, setting himself aside yet remaining preserved"? Compared with those who selfishly pamper their bodies while actually wrecking their lives, he towers ten thousandfold higher!

And make no mistake: the sage does none of this as an affected posture to look unconventional. By nature his cravings are faint and his heart dwells in limpid quiet; he neither drifts with vulgar tides nor stakes his whole identity on this physical shell, dwelling in wide-open impartiality just like selfless heaven and earth. Ordinary folk are full of selfishness and forever fretting with anxious hearts; the sage harbors not a speck of selfishness and shines clear beyond worldly entanglements, with a luminous, vacuous Spirit radiating freely without hindrance. Had he instead fussed day and night over this fleshly frame and sack of skin, how could he ever stand foremost without falling behind or endure without perishing?

Precisely because he is utterly selfless, he matches heaven and earth in virtue and shines alongside sun and moon—unwittingly "fulfilling his own truest aim." Later seekers of the Way who wish body and spirit to escape decay must apply patient, earnest effort to shake off worldly grime and refine themselves over long years. Once refined utterly clean, another heaven and earth—a wholly new realm—opens naturally before them, no longer swept along in ordinary birth and death: what joy in the world could compare with this?

§3
Original (Chinese)

天地不言，全凭一元真气斡旋其间，所以周而复始，生机毫无止息，天地之久长，故历万古而常新也。圣人参天两地，养太和之气，一归浑沌之真，处则为圣功，出即为王道。何世之言修己者，但寻深山枯坐，毫不干一点人事，云治世者，纯用一腔心血，浑身在人物里握算？若此者，各执一偏，各为其私，非无事而寂寂、有事而惺惺者焉。圣人穷则清净无尘，而真形与山河并固；达则人物兼善，而幻身偕爵位俱轻。迨其后，名标宇宙，身独居先，功盖寰区，形存异世，非以其无私耶？学人能去其私，一空色相，永脱尘根，积功则留住人间，飞升则长存天壤，不私其身而卒得长生，较世之为身家计者，不啻云泥之判也。人可不绝外诱之私欤？

Heaven and earth utter not a word, relying solely on the One Primordial True Breath revolving quietly within; thus seasons cycle without end and vitality never pauses—and heaven and earth endure precisely because across ten thousand ages they are renewed every day. Standing as a partner to heaven and earth, the sage nurtures the harmonious Breath within and returns to undivided authenticity: in retirement it is the inner work of the sage; stepping out into public service it is the Kingly Way.

Why, then, do so many who talk of "cultivating themselves" merely hunt for a remote mountain cave to sit like dry stumps without touching a shred of human responsibility, while those who talk of "ordering the world" pour out their entire lifeblood calculating advantage amid social affairs? Both camps cling to one extreme and both serve their own private bias—neither embodies the sage's wholeness of being "silent and serene when unoccupied, lucid and alert when affairs arise"!

Look at the genuine sage: in obscurity and poverty, his heart remains spotless and his true inner form stands as firm as mountains and rivers; in eminence and influence, he benefits people and creatures alike while holding both this fleeting body and lofty rank equally light. In the end, his name shines across the cosmos and his virtue stands foremost; his deeds embrace the realm and his true spirit abides beyond ordinary ages—is this not all due to his selflessness?

Once a student truly strips away selfishness, sees through outward forms, and severs the roots of worldly craving, he may accumulate merit to stay and bless humanity, or upon full completion attain ascension to endure alongside heaven and earth. Never hoarding for his little self, he ultimately realizes true longevity—a gulf like clouds above and mud below compared with those who spend their lives scheming solely for personal gain. How could anyone fail to cut off the selfishness stirred up by external temptations?

Key Terms

  • Not living for oneself (bu zisheng): Laozi's expression for heaven and earth's selfless circulation of One Breath of Prior Heaven to nourish all creatures; in internal alchemy, also letting go of anxious ego-fixation on the physical shell so genuine vitality can flourish.
  • Root-stem of life (mingdi): The primordial energetic root sustaining life in joint cultivation of inner nature and life; "sweeping clean the mind-field and cultivating the root-stem of life" balances inner-nature and life-foundation work.
  • Partner to heaven and earth (can tian liang di): From the Book of Changes ("Discussion of the Trigrams"), used here for the sage whose virtue stands alongside heaven and earth in assisting cosmic nourishment.
  • Silent when unoccupied, lucid when engaged (wushi er jiji, youshi er xingxing): Huang Yuanji's summary of the sage's unity of stillness and motion—resting in clear emptiness without torpor when idle, responding brightly without selfish bias when active—refuting both dry mountain quietism and restless worldly scheming.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Beware the trap of "anxious wellness" harming health. Obsessing over illness, hoarding supplements, and tensely micromanaging every bodily symptom is often "harming life through selfishness"; easing up, keeping a spacious heart, and living regularly invite vitality back far more reliably.
  2. Work from "how this serves others" rather than "what I get out of it." In projects, leadership, and relationships, shifting focus from personal credit to genuine usefulness widens your path naturally ("putting oneself last yet standing foremost").
  3. Refuse the extremes of fleeing life or reducing life to calculation. Cultivation is not an excuse to shirk family or social responsibility, nor should worldly engagement turn you into a cold calculator; aim for the resilience of "serene when quiet, lucid when busy."
  4. Untie selfish knots once each evening. Before sleep, notice which thoughts today were merely your ego keeping score over status or face, gently set them down, and give your heart back to open stillness.

Translator's Notes

  1. "If Heaven had private feeling, even Heaven would grow old": Alluding to the Tang poet Li He's celebrated line ("Were Heaven possessed of feeling, Heaven too would age"), underscoring how private attachment drains enduring vitality.
  2. "Means of pampering and adorning the body" (yangshen zhangshen zhi ju): Yangshen refers to rich food and tonics for the flesh; zhangshen refers to fine robes and outward trappings of status.
  3. "Match heaven and earth in virtue and shine alongside sun and moon": Adapting the Book of Changes ("Wenyan" commentary on Qian): "The great person unites his virtue with heaven and earth, his brightness with sun and moon."
  4. "In obscurity... in eminence...": Adapting Mencius ("Jin Xin I"): "In obscurity, perfect oneself; in eminence, bring good to all under heaven"—used by Huang Yuanji to unite internal alchemy with Confucian public service.
  5. Textual note: Base-edition errors corrected: 不数年面精枯气弱 → 而精枯气弱, and 皎胶物外 → 皎皎物外.
