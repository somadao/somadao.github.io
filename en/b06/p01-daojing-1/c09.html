Chapter 9 · Work Done, Withdraw

When Essence is full and Breath ample, one must stop the fire and forget the method—grasping at fullness risks overturning the Elixir and scorching the body; advancing and withdrawing in rhythm with Heaven while concealing one's brilliance is the Great Way of stepping back when work is done

About This Chapter

Chapter 9 of the Daodejing is commonly read as worldly counsel for retiring gracefully from high office, yet Huang Yuanji unfolds it as an indispensable alchemical warning on "stopping the fire" and "forgetting the method":

  1. "Use the raft to cross the river; leave the boat behind upon reaching shore" (§2, §3): Before mastery is attained, one must diligently practice by the rules; once Essence is full and Breath ample, greedily refusing to stop the fire or basking in self-satisfaction leads at best to stalling halfway, and at worst to overturning the tripod and scorching oneself.
  2. "Holding and filling it" means stopping the fire when Essence is full (§2): Once prior-heaven Essence brims throughout the body and the leak-free sign of horse-like retraction appears, one must decisively stop the fire, return to undivided unknowing, and let go of any pride over "full Essence."
  3. "Hammering it sharp" means turning gentle when Breath is ample (§2): Once posterior turbidity is burned clean and pure yang Breath courses through sinews and bones with dragon-and-tiger vigor, one must at once soften Breath to infant-like suppleness—otherwise unchecked fire threatens to blaze out of control.
  4. Following Heaven's seasons, concealing brilliance amid the world (§2, §3): Even the four seasons step back once their work is done—how much more human affairs and cultivation! Citing the downfall of the arrogant Luan and Fan clans in the Spring and Autumn period, the tragic ends of Wen Zhong and Han Xin who failed to retire, and verses from the Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian), Huang urges practitioners to "finish one stage, release one stage" and hide their light quietly in ordinary life so boastfulness never draws slander or harm.

Safety note: This chapter discusses advanced internal alchemy experiences and fire-phasing cautions such as "stopping the fire when Essence is full," "martial and civil fire," and "outer kidney retracting like a child's (horse-like retraction)." General readers and beginners must never force "martial fire refining" or chase intense bodily sensations; Huang Yuanji's very point throughout this chapter is prefer moderation and knowing when to stop over impatient excess.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：持而盈之，不如其已。揣而锐之，不可长保。金玉满堂，莫之能守。富贵而骄，自贻其咎。功成名遂，身退，天之道。

Lord Lao says: Holding a vessel upright while pouring until it brims over is not as good as stopping in time; hammering a blade again and again for extreme sharpness cannot keep its edge for long. Even a hall stacked high with gold and jade—no one can guard it forever. Wealthy and noble yet arrogant—one brings calamity straight upon oneself. When work is accomplished and renown complete, stepping back into quiet retirement: such is the Way of Heaven.

§2
Original (Chinese)

古云：「过河须用筏，到岸不须舟。」又曰：「未得功时当学法，既得功时当忘法。」斯数语，诚修道之至要也。若修道行工，业已造精微广大之域，犹然兢兢致守，自诩学识高、涵养粹，未免骄心起而躁性生，不有退缩之患，即有悖谬之行。若此者，道何存焉？德何有焉？故太上曰：「持而盈之，不如其已；揣而锐之，不可长保。」修行人，当精未足之日，不得不千淘万汰，洗出我一点至粹之精，以为长生之本；若取得真阳，朝烹暮炼，先天之精，充满一身内外，则身如壁立千寻，意若寒潭秋月，外肾缩如童子，则无漏尽通之境证矣。斯时也，精满于身，不宜再进火符，即当止火不用，且宜无知无识，浑浑沦沦，顿忘乎精盈之境为得。若持盈不已，难免倾丹倒鼎之虞，不如早已之为愈也。当气未充之时，须千烧万炼，运起文武神火，煅炼先天一元真气出来，以为延寿之基。到得凡气炼尽，化为一片纯阳，至大至刚，贯穿乎一身筋骨之内，夭矫如龙，猛力如虎，此何如之精锐也！我当专气致柔，一如婴儿之沕穆无知，庶几长保其气，可至形神俱妙，与道合真。若揣锐不休，难免燎原遍野之虑，安望其长保乎？若是者，犹金玉满堂，莫之能守，一同富贵人家，怙侈灭义，骄奢凌人，如栾氏族灭，范氏家亡，要皆不自戒满除盈，以至横行不轨，自贻其咎。如此征之人事，而天道可知矣。试观当春而温，至夏则暑阳司令，而温和不在矣；至秋而凉，及冬则寒冷秉权，而西风无存矣。物育功成，时行名遂，天地于焉退藏，以畜阳和之德。倘冬寒而间春温，夏热而杂秋凉，即是天道反常，时节愆期，功成不退，适为乖戾之气，其有害于人者多矣。故曰：「功成名遂，身退，天之道也。」夫天且如是，而况于人乎？古来智士良臣，功业烂如，声名昭著，而不知退隐山林，如越之文种、汉之韩信，酿成杀身亡家之祸者不少。是以学道人，当精盈气足之候，不可不忘法忘形，以自败其道也。若未臻斯境者，又乌可舍法舍形哉？

An old saying teaches: "To cross the river you need a raft; once on the far shore you have no need to carry the boat." Another says: "Before attaining results, learn the method faithfully; once results are attained, forget the method." These few lines hold the utmost secret of cultivating the Way!

If a practitioner has already reached a subtle, expansive stage of cultivation yet still clings rigidly to his methods, secretly congratulating himself on his lofty insight and pure attainment, pride and restlessness will inevitably sprout—leading either to sliding backward or to erratic, perverse conduct. At that point, where is the Way? Where is virtue? Hence Lord Lao warns: "Holding and filling it to the brim is not as good as stopping; hammering it ever sharper cannot be long preserved."

How does this apply directly to practice? While Essence is not yet ample, the cultivator has no choice but to pan and wash it a thousand times until a single drop of utterly pure Essence stands clear as the root of longevity. Once true yang is gathered and refined morning and evening until prior-heaven Essence brims through the entire body—one's bearing steady as a thousand-fathom cliff, one's mind limpid as an autumn moon reflected in a cold pool, and the outer kidney naturally retracted like a child's (horse-like retraction)—then the leak-free state is realized.

At that exact juncture, with Essence brimming through the frame, one must no longer keep pushing fire phasing—one must immediately stop the fire! Return at once to unknowing, undivided wholeness and cleanly forget even the state of "full Essence." Refusing to stop and stubbornly clinging to fullness risks overturning the tripod and spoiling the Elixir; far better to stop in time!

Similarly, while true Breath is not yet full, one must temper and refine it through a thousand firings, applying martial and civil fires to forge forth the One Primordial True Breath as the foundation for extending life. Once all mundane turbidity is burned away and the whole frame turns into pure yang—vast, firm, coursing through sinews and bones, sinuous as a soaring dragon and mighty as a crouching tiger—what razor-sharp vigor that is! Yet right then one must concentrate Breath into utmost gentleness, returning to the serene, unknowing softness of a newborn infant, so that this Breath may be long preserved and carry one to the subtle perfection of body and spirit united with the Way. Keep hammering that keen edge without pause, and fire threatens to sweep across the plains and scorch oneself—how could it ever last?

Someone who refuses to stop and keeps forcing fullness and sharpness is just like a house stacked with gold and jade that no one can guard—or like wealthy noble houses that grow arrogant in luxury, cast aside righteousness, and trample others, such as the Luan clan wiped out and the Fan clan ruined in the state of Jin: all because they never checked their fullness, ran wild beyond bounds, and brought ruin on their own heads!

Look from human affairs up to the Way of Heaven, and the law is unmistakable. Spring brings gentle warmth, yet when summer arrives and scorching yang takes command, spring's mildness quietly withdraws; autumn brings crisp coolness, yet when winter takes the reins, the west wind lingers no longer. Once creatures are reared and each season has fulfilled its name, heaven and earth draw quietly inward in winter to store up next year's gentle yang virtue. Were winter's chill broken by unseasonable spring warmth, or summer's heat disrupted by autumn frost, Heaven's order would be thrown askew—refusing to step back once work is done turns into perverse, harmful weather that injures living beings! Therefore the classic declares: "Work accomplished and name complete, step back—such is the Way of Heaven."

If even Heaven acts thus, how much more must human beings? Throughout history, countless brilliant counselors and founding generals shone with dazzling deeds and towering fame yet could not bring themselves to retire to the hills—like Wen Zhong of Yue and Han Xin of Han—inviting execution and the ruin of their clans. Therefore, when a student of the Way reaches the stage where Essence brims and Breath is full, he must not fail to forget method and form lest he ruin his own attainment. Conversely, for one who has not yet reached that stage, how could he prematurely throw away method and discipline?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此教学人，修炼大道，做一节，丢一节，不可自足自满，怠心起而骄心生，祸不旋踵而至矣。即无渗漏之患，然亦半途而废，无由登彼岸以进于神化之域焉。《悟真》云：「未炼还丹须速炼，炼之还须知止足。若也持盈未已心，不免一朝遭殆辱。」足见道无止境，功无穷期，彼满假何为哉？古来修士，多罹杀身亡家之祸，皆由不知韬光养晦、混俗同尘之道也。丹经云：「修行混俗且和光，圆即圆兮方即方。隐显逆从人莫测，教人怎得见行藏？」是以有道高人，当深藏不露，随时俯仰，庶几不异不同，无好无恶，可以长保其身。否则，德修而谤兴，道高而毁来，虽由人之无良，亦自张扬太过。《易》曰：「慢藏诲盗，冶容诲淫」，诚自取也，又何怪自满者之招损乎？吾愿后之学者，未进步则依法行持，既深造，当止火不用，庶可免焚身之患欤！

This chapter teaches practitioners of the Great Way: "finish one stage, release one stage." Never grow complacent or self-satisfied over an achievement; the moment laxness and pride creep in, trouble arrives at your heels! Even if there is no immediate loss of vitality, you will stall halfway and never cross to the far shore of wondrous spiritual transformation.

Zhang Boduan's Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian) puts it vividly: "Before refining the Returned Elixir, hasten to refine it; once refined, you must know when to stop. Should your heart keep grasping at fullness without end, one day you will surely meet peril and disgrace." Clearly the Way knows no ceiling and cultivation knows no finish line—what good could smug pride ever do?

Across antiquity, many cultivators even suffered violent ruin of person and family solely because they did not understand concealing brilliance and mingling quietly with ordinary society. An alchemical verse advises: "In cultivation mingle with the world and soften your light—be round where roundness fits and square where squareness fits; hidden or visible, yielding or adapting, none can fathom you—how could anyone pry into your true stature?"

Thus a truly realized master hides deep without show, moving flexibly with the times—neither flaunting eccentricity nor sinking into vulgarity, free of extreme likes and dislikes—and thereby keeps himself safe for good. Otherwise, the louder one's claims of virtue, the fiercer the slander; the higher one's advertised attainment, the sharper the attacks. Though malicious tongues play their part, is it not also provoked by showing off too much? As the Book of Changes warns, "Careless storage invites thieves; flashy allure invites disrespect." We truly bring it on ourselves—why be surprised when complacency invites loss?

My earnest wish for later students is this: before advancing to mastery, practice faithfully according to the method; once you have reached deep maturity, decisively stop the fire—and thus you may be spared the peril of scorching yourself!

Key Terms

  • Stopping the fire: A critical fire-phasing juncture in internal alchemy—the moment Essence brims and Breath is full (or the Great Medicine matures), immediately halting intentional martial-fire refining and mental steering to shift into effortless, undivided nurturing lest excess fire spoil the Elixir.
  • Horse-like retraction: Referred to here as "the outer kidney retracting like a child's," traditionally regarded as a bodily sign that refining Essence into Breath has reached sufficient fire and the leak-free state is taking hold.
  • Use the raft to cross, leave the boat at shore ("finish one stage, release one stage"): A guiding principle of staged cultivation—each stage has its proper discipline, and once completed one must release the old method to move forward, neither abandoning it prematurely nor clinging to it past its time.
  • Complacent pride (manjia): Self-satisfaction and smugness over one's skill or attainment, universally regarded in moral and alchemical lineages as a primary cause of ruin.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Learn to brake "one step before the peak." Whether in intense work sprints, hard workouts, or seated practice, the moment energy feels full and vibrant is precisely when to ease off and shift into gentle recovery—never push yourself past overheating.
  2. Make "finish one stage, release one stage" a habit. Yesterday's wins, titles, and favorite formulas carried you across the last river, not necessarily the next; resetting to zero after a success keeps you from getting trapped in past glories.
  3. Hide your edge even more when things go well. "Careless storage invites thieves"—the more progress you make, the more discreet and grounded you should remain, investing your energy in deeper roots rather than broadcast boasting.
  4. Never throw away the boat before reaching shore. "Forgetting the method" is medicine for the mature practitioner, not an excuse for laziness; while the foundation is still shaky, keep putting in honest, disciplined work.

Translator's Notes

  1. "To cross the river you need a raft; once on the far shore you have no need of the boat": Adapting the Diamond Sutra's famous raft simile ("Even the Dharma must be relinquished, how much more what is not Dharma"), widely quoted by internal alchemists to explain stopping the fire and letting go of methods once a stage is complete.
  2. "The Luan clan wiped out and the Fan clan ruined": Episodes from the Zuo Commentary (Zuozhuan) on the Spring and Autumn state of Jin, where the powerful ministerial clans of Luan Ying and Fan Jishe fell through arrogance and overreach—classic warnings of "wealthy and noble yet arrogant."
  3. "Wen Zhong of Yue and Han Xin of Han": Wen Zhong, strategist to King Goujian of Yue, ignored Fan Li's advice to retire after conquering Wu and was forced to commit suicide; Han Xin, brilliant general of early Han, did not step down at the height of his fame and was executed.
  4. Verse from Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian): Adapting Northern Song master Zhang Boduan's celebrated quatrains warning cultivators to refine diligently yet know when to stop.
  5. "Careless storage invites thieves; flashy allure invites disrespect": From the Book of Changes, "Appended Remarks I," cited by Huang Yuanji to warn cultivators against ostentatious display.
  6. Textual note: Base-edition errors corrected: stray 一 in title removed, 日 → 曰 (four times), 筋肯 → 筋骨, 天矫 → 夭矫, 智土 → 智士, 声名如著 → 声名昭著, 念心起 → 怠心起, 何为战 → 何为哉, and 无艮 → 无良.
