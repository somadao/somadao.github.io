Chapter 3 · Not Displaying the Desirable

When cleverness is not paraded and objects of desire are not flaunted, the mind stays empty and Spirit still; emptying the mind to nourish Spirit, filling the belly to nourish Breath, softening ambition and strengthening the bones unite alchemical cultivation and effortless governance in one principle

About This Chapter

Modern readers often mistake Chapter 3 of the Daodejing for a doctrine of keeping the populace ignorant. Huang Yuanji cuts straight to the real meaning: this chapter is at once the Way of effortless governance and a major internal alchemy method of "emptying the mind, filling the belly, weakening ambition, and strengthening the bones":

  1. Good governance begins with desirelessness above (§2, §3): Ordinary people are not contentious or greedy by nature; contention is stirred up when rulers ostentatiously exalt "worthies," chase rare luxuries, and flaunt extravagance. When those above stay simple and modest in their desires, society naturally settles into clarity and harmony.
  2. "Emptying the mind, filling the belly, weakening ambition, strengthening the bones" as four alchemical keys (§2, §3): Embracing the One to empty the mind and nourish Spirit ("empty their minds"), gathering prior-heaven luminous yang Breath to replenish the lower abdomen and refine yin Essence ("fill their bellies"), concentrating Breath to attain infant-like suppleness and shed brittle arrogance ("weaken their ambitions"), until true Breath pervades sinews and bones in vast, buoyant strength ("strengthen their bones").
  3. "Without cunning knowledge or desire" does not mean stupidity (§2, §3): It does not seek to make people dull; rather, it draws even the sharpest minds away from boastful scheming back to genuine simplicity—able to meddle, yet choosing not to act recklessly.
  4. The true sense of "acting through non-action" (§2, §3): Living in the world, one cannot abstain from all affairs; carrying out what belongs to each situation in accord with natural principle, without meddling or forcing, looks like "action" on the outside while remaining completely "non-action" within.

Safety note: This chapter uses traditional alchemical expressions such as "emptying the mind and filling the belly" and "gathering luminous yang Breath to transform yin Essence," whose real essence is a clear mind, modest desires, calm breathing, and a supple yet steady body-mind. In practice, never hold your breath, deliberately push out the abdomen, or force breath downward by sheer willpower, which can cause bloating or chest tightness; simply keep body and mind relaxed and natural.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：不尚贤，使民不争；不贵难得之货，使民不为盗；不见可欲，使心不乱。是以圣人之治，虚其心，实其腹，弱其志，强其骨，常使民无知无欲，使夫知者不敢为也。为无为，则无不治。

Lord Lao says: Do not ostentatiously exalt the "worthy," and the people will not contend for rank and fame; do not prize rare, hard-to-get goods, and the people will not turn to theft; do not flaunt objects that inflame greed, and the mind will not be thrown into confusion.

Therefore, in governing the world (and governing his own body-mind), the sage empties the mind of clutter, fills the belly with primordial Breath, softens restless competitive ambition, and strengthens the inner bones and vitality. He continually guides people away from scheming cleverness and grasping desire, so that even the clever dare not meddle recklessly. Acting through non-action, there is nothing that is not well ordered.

§2
Original (Chinese)

圣人之治天下也，与其有为，不如无为，尤不如有为而无为。其化民成俗也，与其能感，不如能化，尤不如相安于无事之为得。是以尧舜恭己垂裳，而四方悉昭风动，此何如之化理哉？不过上无心而民自静，上无好而民自正，上无欲而民自定耳。否则，纷纷扰扰，自以为与民兴利除弊，而不知其扰民也实甚。故曰：民本无争也，而上争夺之；民本无贪也，而上贪婪之；民本无私无欲也，而上以奇技淫巧、鲜衣美食先导之。欲其不争不贪、无嗜无好也，得乎？苟能修其身，正其心，恬然淡然，毫无事事，不以贤能相尚，则民自安静而不争矣；不以难得之货为贵，则民重廉耻，而不为盗矣。且声色货利之场不一，属于目则无见无欲，己与民各适其自在之天，而虚灵活泼之神，自常应常静而不乱矣。此事岂异人任哉？惟圣人屏除耳目，斩断邪私，抱一以空其心。心空，则炼丹有本，由是而采天地灵阳之气，以化阴精，日积月累，自然阴精消灭，而阳气滋长，则实腹以全其形，所谓「以道凝身，以术延命」，即是超生拔死之法。而且专气致柔，如婴儿之力弱，不能持物者然。虽至柔也，而动则刚。观其浩浩渊渊，兀兀腾腾，真可包天地而入日月，贯金石而格鬼神，其气骨自有如是之强壮者。如此性修命立，彼浩然刚大之气，绰绰有余，一切知觉之心、嗜欲之性，不知消归何有。圣人以此修身，即以此治世。在己无知无欲，使民亦无知无欲，不但愚者混混沌沌，上合於穆之天，即聪明才智之儒，平日矜能恃智，惟恐以不逞为忧，至此亦淡恬无事，自忘其知识之私，一归浑朴。此能为而不为，非不能也，实不敢也。虽然，人生天地，不能逃虚空而独超物外，必有人伦日用之道，又乌得不为哉？然顺其自然，行所无事，虽有为，仍无为也，亦犹天不言而自化，四时代宣其教矣；帝无为而自治，百官代理其政矣。为者其迹，不为者其神，是以南面端拱，天下悉庆平成，猗欤盛哉！

In governing the realm, rather than intervening everywhere (forced action), the sage prefers quiet non-action—and higher still than rigid inaction is "acting while remaining utterly free of self-serving contrivance" (acting through non-action). In transforming customs, rather than merely stirring passing emotions, he prefers silent transformation—and highest of all is letting ruler and people dwell in mutual peace without needless fuss. Thus Yao and Shun simply kept themselves dignified with robes hanging loose, and the four quarters turned toward goodness like grass bending to the wind. What wondrous secret of governance was this? Simply that when those above harbor no scheming mind, the people grow calm of themselves; when those above nurse no eccentric partialities, the people grow upright of themselves; when those above are free of greed, the people grow settled of themselves.

Otherwise, bustling about in endless commotion and flattering oneself that one is promoting benefits and removing abuses for the people, one does not realize how grievously one harasses them! Hence it is said: the people are not contentious by nature—it is those above who provoke contention; the people are not greedy by nature—it is those above who model greed; the people are not full of selfish cravings by nature—it is those above who lead the way with exotic gadgets, ornate finery, and sumptuous banquets. Under such leadership, how could anyone expect the people not to quarrel, covet, or indulge?

If a ruler can first cultivate himself and rectify his mind, remaining serene, plain, and unmeddlesome—not parading "worthies" as a trophy for rivalry, the people naturally stay peaceful and uncontentious; not treating rare luxuries as priceless, the people cherish integrity and refuse to steal. Though scenes of sensual pleasure and gain abound in countless forms, even when they pass before the eye the mind neither fixates nor craves; self and others alike enjoy their natural ease, and the vacuous, lively Original Spirit responds freely while remaining ever calm and unruffled.

Can this work be handed off to someone else? Never! It begins within the sage himself: withdrawing eyes and ears from outward chasing, severing selfish whims, and embracing the One to empty the mind ("empty the mind"). Once the mind is empty, refining the Elixir has its root; from there one gathers the luminous yang Breath of heaven and earth to transform posterior yin Essence. Day after day, yin dross dissolves away while pure yang Breath steadily grows, filling the lower belly and keeping the bodily frame whole ("fill the belly"). This is what the ancients called "coagulating the body with the Way and prolonging life with the art"—the true method of transcending decay and mortality.

More than that, one concentrates Breath until it reaches utmost suppleness ("weaken ambition"), like a newborn infant whose soft hands cannot even grasp an object. Yet hidden inside that utmost softness is an invincible strength whenever it stirs: behold that vast, abysmal, buoyantly surging vitality—truly capable of encompassing heaven and earth, taking in sun and moon, piercing metal and stone, and moving spirits! Naturally one's inner backbone and vitality ("strengthen the bones") grow strong to this very degree.

With inner nature and life thus jointly cultivated and established, that vast, flood-like vitality abounds with plenty to spare, while restless calculating thoughts and grasping desires vanish without a trace. The exact same discipline by which the sage cultivates himself is how he orders the world. Free of cunning and greed himself, he brings the people into freedom from cunning and greed: not only do simple folk rest in natural wholeness aligned with the deep silent Heaven, but even brilliant, talented scholars—who ordinarily boast of their gifts and fret lest they miss a chance to show off—grow tranquil and unassuming, forgetting their private cleverness and returning together to unspoiled simplicity. This is being able to meddle yet refraining—not out of inability, but out of deep awe that dares not act rashly.

Even so, living between heaven and earth, one cannot flee into thin air outside human society; we all have family ties and daily responsibilities—how could we do nothing at all? The key is following nature and acting without forcing—just as Great Yu guided floodwaters rather than damming them up: outwardly there is action, yet inwardly it remains pure non-action. Just as Heaven speaks not a word yet transforms all things through the four seasons proclaiming its order, and the sage-emperor practices non-action while officials discharge the tasks of state: outward "action" is merely the trace, while inward "non-action" is the animating spirit. Seated serenely facing south with folded hands, all under heaven rejoices in peace and harmony—how magnificent!

§3
Original (Chinese)

道本平常，不矜新颖，不尚奇异。如国家尊贤，原是美事，若以此相夸相尚，则贤者固贤，而不肖者，亦将饰为贤。甚至贤以否为否，而不肖者又以贤为否，于是争端起矣。彼此互相标榜，迭为党援，而天下自此多事。国家理财，亦是常经，而若贵异物，宝远货，则民必梯山航海，冒险履危，不辞跋涉之苦、性命之忧，搜罗而致之朝廷。至求之不得，千方万计，虽奸盗劫夺，所不顾也。至于衣服饮食，亦日用之常，而若食必珍馐，衣求锦绣，见可欲而欲之，奢风何日已也？是以圣人内重外轻，必虚心以养神，实腹以养气，令神气打成一片，流行于一身之中，条畅融和，苏绵快乐，而志弱矣；且神静如岳，气行如泉，而骨强矣。常常抱一，刻刻守中，非独一己无欲无思，即聪明才智之士，亦观感而悉化，不敢妄有所为。或曰：有为则纷更致诮，无为则清净贻讥，为不为之间，亦几难矣。讵知，顺理而为，非冒昧以为，有为仍与无为等，所以孔子赞舜曰：无为而治者，其舜也欤？

The Way has always been thoroughly ordinary, never chasing novelty or exalting eccentricity. Honoring capable people in a state is in itself a fine thing; yet the moment it turns into loud boasting and competitive display, the truly worthy remain worthy while the unworthy frantically disguise themselves as worthy. Worse still, the worthy dismiss the unworthy while the unworthy slander the worthy—and factional strife erupts! Each side advertises itself and builds cliques, and the realm knows no more peace.

Managing state finances is likewise an ordinary necessity; yet if rulers prize bizarre curiosities and place exorbitant value on exotic goods from afar, people will scale cliffs and cross stormy seas, risking hardship and life itself to hunt them down for the court. When they fail to obtain them, they stop at nothing—even fraud, theft, and highway robbery. Clothing and food are ordinary daily needs; yet if every meal must be rare delicacies and every garment embroidered silk, craving everything tempting that meets the eye, when will extravagance ever end?

Therefore the sage weighs the inner heavy and the outer light: first emptying the mind to nourish Original Spirit ("emptying the mind"), then filling the belly to store true Breath ("filling the belly"), letting Spirit and Breath fuse completely and flow smoothly throughout the entire body—harmonious, open, softly suffused with quiet ease—and restless competitive drive naturally softens ("weakening ambition"). At the same time, Spirit rests still as a mountain peak while true Breath flows continuously like a spring—and inner backbone and resilience naturally grow mighty ("strengthening the bones").

Constantly embracing the One and guarding the center, not only does the sage remain free of selfish cravings and stray schemes, but even the sharpest intellects are quietly transformed by his grounded presence and dare not act rashly. Someone might ask: "If you intervene actively, constant meddling brings blame; if you do nothing at all, idle quietism draws ridicule—isn't the balance between acting and not acting terribly hard?" Little do they realize that acting in harmony with true principle, without blind impulse, makes outward "action" completely equal to "non-action." That is why Confucius exclaimed in praise of Emperor Shun: "One who governed through non-action—was that not Shun!"

Key Terms

  • Emptying the mind, filling the belly (xuxin shifu): A foundational internal alchemy metaphor—"emptying the mind" means letting go of scattered thoughts and selfish cravings to nourish Spirit; "filling the belly" means gathering One Breath of Prior Heaven in the lower abdomen to fortify form and life, never literally starving the mind or stuffing the stomach.
  • Weakening ambition, strengthening the bones (ruozhi qianggu): "Weakening ambition" means shedding brittle, competitive, status-driven posterior drive and returning to infant-like suppleness; "strengthening the bones" refers to the deep inner steadiness and abundant vitality born when Spirit and Breath pervade the body.
  • Guarding the center and embracing the One: Keeping Spirit from chasing outward stimuli and resting continually in the luminous central space so Essence, Breath, and Spirit return to unity.
  • Joint cultivation of inner nature and life: "Emptying the mind to nourish Spirit" emphasizes cultivating inner nature; "filling the belly to nourish Breath and transforming yin Essence with yang" emphasizes establishing life—only together do they yield unshakable balance.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Put your information stream and desires on a diet. "Not displaying the desirable keeps the mind undisturbed" is extraordinarily practical today: scrolling less through envy-inducing feeds and consumer showcases immediately calms half of one's restlessness.
  2. Act "in accord with principle," not "to show off." Whether leading a team or organizing daily life, respect natural patterns, cut back pointless busywork and performative meddling, and save energy for moves that flow with the grain.
  3. Cultivate "soft outside, rock-steady inside." Genuine confidence is not tense, combative willpower, but an infant-like ease on the surface paired with a Spirit "still as a mountain" within.
  4. Order your own heart before trying to fix the atmosphere. When complaining that everyone around you is hyper-competitive and restless, check whether you are fueling the fire; steady yourself first, step out of comparison, and your immediate circle gradually calms down.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Coagulating the body with the Way and prolonging life with the art": A traditional internal alchemy maxim referring to condensing Spirit through quiet alignment with the Way while fortifying bodily vitality through breath and essence cultivation—joint cultivation of nature and life.
  2. "Deep silent Heaven" (wumu zhi tian): Alluding to the Classic of Poetry ("Hymns of Zhou," Wei tian zhi ming): "The mandate of Heaven—how deep and unceasing!"
  3. "One who governed through non-action—was that not Shun!": From the Analects of Confucius, Book XV ("Wei Ling Gong"), praising Shun for appointing worthy ministers and sitting serenely facing south while the realm rested in peace.
  4. Textual note: Several printing errors in the base edition have been corrected: the missing character 骨 and misprinted 元 in the classic text (restored to 强其骨，常使民无知无欲), plus 不知 → 不如, 安请 → 安静, 已 → 己 (three instances), 气借 → 气骨, 棉山 → 梯山, 不静 → 不辞, 肯强 → 骨强, and 致销 → 致诮.
