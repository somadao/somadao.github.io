Chapter 1 · The Gate of All Marvels

The single awakening that stirs out of utmost stillness is the Mysterious Pass; observing marvels without desire and observing the opening with desire are stillness and movement sprung from one source, the starting point of all alchemical practice in this book

About This Chapter

Chapter 1 of the Daodejing has always been revered as the master outline of the entire classic. While philosophical commentaries tend to dwell on language and ontology, Huang Yuanji steps straight into the heart of practical cultivation, turning these brief fifty-nine characters into a starting map for internal alchemy:

  1. Two aspects of the Way (§2): The undivided primordial Breath before the cosmos splits is the "Nameless" (the beginning of heaven and earth); the harmonious primordial Breath that stirs out of utmost stillness to engender all creatures is the "Named" (the mother of the myriad things). Within utmost non-being dwells utmost being; within utmost emptiness lies utmost reality.
  2. First practical step (§3): Set down all scattered thoughts, lower the curtains of the eyes, and gaze inward toward the Cave of Emptiness below the heart and above the kidneys, letting Spirit and Breath naturally collect in the central palace and merge into one—entering the dim and obscure state, neither dull with sleep nor disturbed by wandering thought.
  3. The single awakening at the Mysterious Pass (§4–§6): Right in the midst of unknowing, utmost stillness, a spontaneous spark of lucid awareness suddenly stirs—this is the founding of the Supreme Ultimate, the yang arising at the Living Zi Hour, and the secret Mysterious Pass transmitted by the alchemists. Utter emptiness in stillness is "observing its marvels without desire"; clear awareness in nascent movement without falling into conceptual chatter is "observing its opening with desire." Stillness and movement issue from a single source: "mystery upon mystery, the gate of all marvels."

Safety note: This chapter discusses seated quieting of the mind, union of Spirit and Breath, and the experience of the "first stirring of the Mysterious Pass / Living Zi Hour." Beginners should simply sit at ease, breathe naturally, and avoid rigidly locking attention onto any bodily spot—still less force internal sensations or cling to visions of light. If you feel head pressure, chest tightness, palpitations, or insomnia, stop sitting at once, stand up and walk around, and return to ordinary daily rhythm.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：道可道，非常道；名可名，非常名。无名，天地之始；有名，万物之母。故常无欲，以观其妙；常有欲，以观其窍。此两者，同出而异名，同谓之玄。玄之又玄，众妙之门。

Lord Lao says: A "Way" that can be spelled out in words is not the eternal, unchanging Way; a "name" that can be assigned is not the eternal, unchanging Name. The "Nameless" chaotic emptiness is the origin before heaven and earth divided; the "Named" stirring of vital Breath is the mother who brings forth and nurtures the myriad things.

Therefore, one should ever remain in desireless, thought-free utmost stillness to observe the formless marvels of the Way's source; and again, at the first subtle stirring of vital movement, abide in that fine awareness to observe the mysterious opening from which vitality springs. These two—"observing marvels without desire" and "observing the opening with desire"—emerge from the exact same source while bearing different names; together they are called "mystery" (xuan). Profound beyond profundity, giving birth to mysterious vitality out of utmost stillness—this is the very gateway to all marvels in heaven and earth.

§2
Original (Chinese)

朱子云：「道，犹路也，人之所共由者也。」其实生天生地、生人生物，公共之理，故谓之道。天地未判以前，此道悬于太空；天地既辟而后，此道寄诸天壤。是道也，何道也？先天地而长存，后天地而不敝；生于天地之先，混于虚无之内，无可见，亦无可闻。故太上曰：以言夫道，费而且隐，实无可道。所可道者，皆道之发见耳，非真常之道也。以言其名，虚而无物，实无可名。所可名者，皆道之糟粕耳，非真常之名也。人不知道，曷观之《诗》乎？曰：「上天之载，无声无臭」，道不可以有言矣。又曰：「维天之命，於穆不已」，道不可以无称矣。须知至无之内，有至有者存；至虚之中，有至实者在，道真不可以方所形容也。太上慈悲度世，广为说法曰：鸿濛未兆之先，原是浑浑沦沦，绝无半点形象，虽曰无名，而天地、人物，咸毓个中，此所以为天地之始也。及其静之既久，气机一动，则有可名，而氤氤氲氲，一段太和元气，流行宇宙，养育群生，此所以为万物之母也。始者，天地未开之前，一团元气在抱是也；母者，天地既辟而后，一气化生万物是也。

Zhu Xi said, "The Way is like a road, the path traveled in common by all people." In fact, far more than for humanity alone, it is the shared fundamental principle that gives birth to heaven, earth, human beings, and all things—and for that reason is called the Way. Before heaven and earth separated, this Way hung suspended in the vast void; once heaven and earth opened, it lodged within heaven, earth, and all creatures.

What manner of Way is this? It endures prior to heaven and earth, and does not decay even after heaven and earth pass away; born before heaven and earth, blended into emptiness and non-being, it can neither be seen by the eye nor heard by the ear. Thus Lord Lao says: if you try to speak of this Way, its functions reach everywhere while its substance remains hidden—it truly cannot be spoken. Whatever words can articulate is merely an outward trace of the Way, not the true and constant Way. If you try to name it, it is vacuous and devoid of tangible things—it truly cannot be named. Whatever name can be pinned to it is merely the husks and dregs of the Way, not the true and constant Name.

If people fail to grasp what the Way is, why not look to the Classic of Poetry? It says, "The workings of High Heaven have neither sound nor scent"—showing that the Way cannot be spoken of as some tangible "being." Yet it also says, "The mandate of Heaven—how deep and unceasing!"—showing that the Way cannot be dismissed as dead "non-being" either. Know well that inside utmost non-being abides utmost being, and within utmost emptiness dwells utmost reality; the Way truly cannot be pinned to any location or shape.

Out of compassion to liberate the world, Lord Lao reluctantly unfolds it in words: before even the faintest sign appeared in primordial chaos, all was an undivided wholeness without a speck of form. Though called "Nameless," heaven, earth, humanity, and all things were already gestating inside it—hence it is called the "beginning of heaven and earth." Once that stillness has endured long enough and the vital impulse subtly stirs, there arises something that can be named: a rich, swirling expanse of harmonious primordial Breath (One Breath of Prior Heaven) flows throughout the cosmos to nourish and rear all living beings—hence it is called the "mother of the myriad things." In short, "beginning" is that intact sphere of primordial Breath embraced before heaven and earth opened; "mother" is that same Breath transforming into and nurturing all things once heaven and earth have divided.

§3
Original (Chinese)

学人下手之初，别无他术，惟一心端坐，万念胥捐，垂帘观照，心之下、肾之上，仿佛有个虚无窟子，神神相照，息息常归，任其一往一来，但以神气两者，凝注中宫为主。不顷刻间，神气打成一片矣。于是听其混混沌沌，不起一明觉心，久之，恍恍惚惚，入于无何有之乡焉。斯时也，不知神之入气、气之归神，浑然一无人无我、何地何天景象，而又非昏瞶也，若使昏瞶，适成枯木死灰。修士于此，当灭动心，莫灭照心，惟是智而若愚，慧而不用。于无知无觉之际，忽然一觉而动，即太极开基。须知此一觉中，自自然然，不由感附，才是我本来真觉。

When a student first sets to work, there is no esoteric trick—simply sit upright with a single mind, lay aside ten thousand tangled thoughts, gently lower the curtains of the eyes, and gaze softly inward at the open space below the heart and above the kidneys, as if there were a luminous, hollow Cave of Emptiness there. Let Spirit illuminate that space calmly and let each breath naturally return there, allowing inhalation and exhalation to come and go on their own while taking the gentle gathering of Spirit and Breath in the central palace as the sole focus.

Before long, Spirit and Breath meld seamlessly into one. At that point, let body and mind rest in undivided wholeness without deliberately raising a self-conscious thought of "now I am lucid, now I am observing." After a while, you slip into the dim and obscure state, as if entering the "Land of Nothing-at-All" (a boundless, unobstructed clarity).

In that moment, you no longer know whether Spirit has entered Breath or Breath has returned to Spirit; all merges into one undivided expanse where neither self nor other, neither earth nor sky is felt. Yet beware: this is by no means drowsy stupor (torpor)—once you sink into foggy drowsiness, you turn into dry wood and dead ashes. Here the practitioner must extinguish the restless, discursive "moving mind" while never extinguishing the luminous, wakeful "illuminating mind": possess wisdom yet appear simple, hold intelligence yet refrain from actively wielding it.

Right in the middle of that unknowing, insensible stillness, a single spark of clear awareness suddenly stirs of itself—like the first stroke dividing chaos, founding the Supreme Ultimate. Recognize it unmistakably: this single awakening arises altogether spontaneously from within, never provoked by external objects or impressions. That alone is our original true awareness (the first manifestation of Original Spirit and True Intention).

§4
Original (Chinese)

道家谓之玄关妙窍，只在一呼一吸之间。其吸而入也，则为阴、为静、为无；其呼而出也，则为阳、为动、为有，即此一息之微，亦有妙窍。人欲修成正觉，惟此一觉而动之时，有个实实在在、的的确确、无念虑、无渣滓一个本来人在。故曰：天地有此一觉，而生万物；人身有此一觉，而结金丹。但此一觉，有如电光石火，当前则是，转眼即非，所争只毫厘间耳。学者务于平时审得清，临机方把得住。古来大觉如来，亦无非此一觉积累而成也。

Daoists call this most crucial point the wondrous opening of the Mysterious Pass, and it lies nowhere else than between one exhalation and one inhalation. As breath draws inward, it belongs to yin, stillness, and "non-being"; as breath flows outward, it belongs to yang, movement, and "being." Even within a single subtle breath, this wondrous opening is already present.

Whoever wishes to cultivate genuine awakening depends wholly on that instant when stillness suddenly stirs into awareness: right then, a real, unmistakable, thought-free, unstained "original person" stands revealed. Hence the ancients say: "When heaven and earth have this one awakening, the myriad things are born; when the human body has this one awakening, the Golden Elixir congeals."

Yet this awakening flashes by like lightning or a spark struck from flint: meet it right in the present moment and it is true; blink and let it slip into posterior discursive thought and it is already altered—the gap between true and false is barely a hair's breadth. The student must examine it clearly in ordinary practice so as to hold fast when the moment arrives. Even the great awakened Buddhas and perfected sages of antiquity accomplished nothing other than accumulating and sustaining this single true awakening.

§5
Original (Chinese)

修士兴工，不从有欲无欲、观妙观窍下手，又从何处以为本乎？虽然，无与有、妙与窍，无非阴静阳动，一气判为二气，二气仍归一气而已矣。以其静久而动，无中生有，名曰阳生活子时；以其动极复静，有又还无，名曰复命归根。要皆一太极所判之阴阳也。两者虽有异名，而实同出一源，太上谓之玄。玄者，深远之谓也。学者欲得玄道，必静之又静，定而又定，其中浑无物事，是为无欲观妙，此一玄也。及气机一动，虽有知，却不生一知见，虽有动，却不存一动想，有一心，无两念，是为有欲观窍，此又一玄也。至玄之又玄，实为归根之所，非众妙之门而何？所惜者，凡人有此妙窍，不知直养，是以旋开旋闭，不至耗尽而不已。至人于玄关窍开时，一眼觑定，一手拿定，操存涵养，不使须臾或失，所以直造无上根源，而成大觉金仙。

When a practitioner begins the alchemical work, if not by starting from "without desire observing marvels, with desire observing the opening," where else could a foundation be laid? Even so, "non-being" and "being," "marvels" and "opening," are nothing but yin stillness and yang movement: one primordial Breath divides into two breaths, and those two breaths return once more to the single Breath.

When prolonged stillness stirs into motion and being emerges from non-being, alchemists name it "yang arising at the Living Zi Hour"; when motion reaches its peak and returns to stillness, and being reverts to non-being, they name it "returning to the root and restoring life." At bottom, both are simply the yin and yang divided out of a single Supreme Ultimate. Though they bear different names, they spring from one source, which Lord Lao calls "mystery" (xuan)—meaning deep, subtle, and far-reaching.

To attain this mysterious Way, the student must grow still and stiller, settled and more settled, until not a single thing stirs within—that is "observing its marvels without desire," the first mystery. Then, when the vital impulse moves, though there is clear knowing, no conceptual judgment arises; though there is movement, no meddlesome thought of manipulating it is formed—one undivided heart, free of any second thought. That is "observing its opening with desire," the second mystery.

Uniting these two into "mystery upon mystery" brings one to the true home where life returns to its root—what else could this be but the "gate of all marvels"? How regrettable that ordinary people likewise possess this wondrous opening yet do not know how to nourish it directly: no sooner does it open than wandering desires pull it shut again, over and over until primordial Breath is spent. By contrast, at the very instant the Mysterious Pass opens, the realized person fixes one steady eye upon it and takes firm hold with one sure hand, guarding and nurturing it without letting it slip for a single moment—and thus reaches straight back to the supreme source and becomes a Great Awakened Golden Immortal.

§6
Original (Chinese)

下手工夫，在玄关一窍。太上首章，即将无名有名、观妙观窍指出，足见修道之要，除此一个玄关窍，余无可进步也。故开首四句，说大道根源，实属无形无状，不可思议穷究。惟天地未开之初，混混沌沌，无可端倪，即人致养于静时也。天地忽辟之际，静极而动，一觉而醒，即人侦气于动，为炼丹之始基。第此倏忽之间，非有智珠慧剑，不能得也。要之，念头起处为玄牝，实为开天辟地、生人育物之端，自古神仙无不由此一觉而动之机造成。又曰：无欲观妙、有欲观窍，两者一静一动，互为其根，故同出而异名。凡有形象者，可得而思量忖度，若此妙窍，无而有，有而无，实不可方所名状，纵舌如悬河，亦不能道其一字，所以谓之玄玄。学者亦不得视为杳冥，毫不穷一个实际下落。果于此寻出的的确确处，在人视为恍惚，在我实有把凭，久之着手成春，头头是道矣。

The entire starting work of internal alchemy rests upon the single opening of the Mysterious Pass. That Lord Lao opens his very first chapter by pointing straight to "Nameless and Named" and "observing marvels and observing the opening" proves beyond doubt that in cultivating the Way, apart from this Mysterious Pass there is no other road forward.

Accordingly, the opening four lines describe the root of the Great Way as utterly formless and featureless, beyond all conceptual thought or probing. The undivided chaos before heaven and earth split, offering no visible clue, corresponds to the practitioner's nurturing of Original Spirit in utmost stillness; the sudden opening of heaven and earth, when stillness peaks and stirs into a single lucid awakening, corresponds to perceiving the rising vital impulse in nascent movement—the very starting foundation of refining the Elixir. Yet in that fleeting flash, without the pearl of clear wisdom and the sword of sharp discernment, one cannot lay hold of it.

In sum, the exact point where a thought first begins to stir—before falling into discursive clutter—is the Mysterious Female (see Mysterious Pass). It is the very origin that splits heaven and earth and engenders humanity and all creatures; not a single immortal from antiquity onward was formed outside this pivot of "a single awakening stirring into motion." Thus the classic adds that observing marvels without desire and observing the opening with desire—one still, one moving—serve as each other's root, and therefore issue from the same source under different names.

Anything with fixed shape can be measured and calculated by the intellect; this wondrous opening alone moves from non-being into being and from being back into non-being, impossible to pin to a bodily inch or shape. Even with eloquence rushing like a waterfall, one could not speak a single word of its true savor—hence it is called "mystery upon mystery." Yet students must never treat it as a vague abstraction without seeking its concrete footing in practice. Once you genuinely recognize that unmistakable place in direct experience, what sounds dim and hazy to others becomes a solid certainty in your own hands; over time, whatever you touch blooms with spring, and every gesture accords with the Way.

Key Terms

  • Mysterious Pass (Wondrous Opening of the Mysterious Pass, Mysterious Female): Not a fixed anatomical organ, but the pivotal moment of prior-heaven vitality appearing where utmost stillness stirs into movement, breath opens and closes, and the very first impulse of awareness arises.
  • Cave of Emptiness: One of Huang Yuanji's favorite metaphors, referring to the open, vacuous central region below the heart and above the kidneys where one gently lowers the curtains to let Spirit and Breath meet.
  • Dim and obscure: The undivided, lucidly vacuous state reached once Spirit and Breath merge—free of wandering thoughts yet never sinking into sleepy dullness.
  • Living Zi Hour: Unbound by clock time, the spontaneous instant when a single yang impulse stirs out of utmost stillness, bringing being forth from non-being.
  • Original Spirit and True Intention: In this chapter, the spontaneous spark of true awareness rising in utmost stillness without external prompting—Original Spirit in stillness, True Intention as it subtly stirs.
  • Extinguish the moving mind, not the illuminating mind: Letting go of restless, scheming thought (moving mind) while preserving quiet, wakeful clarity (illuminating mind), preventing descent into lifeless torpor.

Putting It into Practice

  1. First learn "stillness without sleep." In quiet sitting or daily rest, true relaxation is never drowsy fog; it is setting down mental calculations (extinguishing the moving mind) while keeping a crisp, gentle wakefulness (not extinguishing the illuminating mind).
  2. "Gazing inward" is not staring rigidly. The region below the heart and above the kidneys is simply an open space where attention can rest; let breath and mind keep easy company there without clenching or forcing focus onto the belly, which only breeds stiffness and agitation.
  3. Notice the clarity of the "first impulse." "Where thought first stirs is the Mysterious Female." Before speaking or acting in daily life, catching yourself in that split second before emotional reactivity takes over dissolves countless needless troubles on the spot.
  4. Do not spin in circles over bookish phrasing. "The Way that can be spoken is not the constant Way": understanding the text is only a map. Setting aside even a little quiet time each day to sense breath and thought directly is when the journey actually begins.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Ever without desire, to observe its marvels; ever with desire, to observe its opening": Historically this passage of the Daodejing has two main punctuations: chang wu, yu yi guan qi miao ("ever non-being, wishing to observe its marvels," favored by Wang Anshi and Su Zhe) and chang wuyu, yi guan qi miao ("ever without desire, to observe its marvels," followed by Heshang Gong, Wang Bi, and most Ming–Qing commentators). Huang Yuanji builds his entire commentary around the latter reading, which our text and translation follow.
  2. "Broad yet hidden" (fei er qie yin): From Chapter 12 of the Doctrine of the Mean ("The Way of the noble person is broad yet hidden"), meaning the Way's functions are vast and omnipresent while its substance remains subtle and invisible.
  3. "The mandate of Heaven—how deep and unceasing!": From the Classic of Poetry ("Hymns of Zhou," Wei tian zhi ming); 於 is read wū, and wūmù describes the profound, solemn, unceasing operation of Heaven.
  4. "Land of Nothing-at-All" (wuheyou zhi xiang): From Zhuangzi, "Free and Easy Wandering," symbolizing a vast, unobstructed state beyond worldly calculation.
  5. "Possess wisdom yet appear simple, hold intelligence yet refrain from using it": In the original printed text 愚 ("simple") was misprinted or misread as 感; emended here from standard alchemical usage and the immediate context ("neither dull" / "not extinguishing the illuminating mind").
  6. Textual note: Several woodblock/OCR errors in the base edition have been corrected (including 叉玄 → 又玄, 自言 → 有言, 人于 → 入于, 请之玄 → 谓之玄, 须更 → 须臾, 香冥 → 杳冥, and a duplicate character in 思量忖度). Chapter titles were added in modern collated editions following Diandao zhi shu and are retained here for ease of reference.
