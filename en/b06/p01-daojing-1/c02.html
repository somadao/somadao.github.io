Chapter 2 · Accomplishing Without Claiming

Beauty and ugliness, good and bad are all posterior dualities; only by recognizing the Mysterious Pass where utmost stillness stirs and returning through yin and yang to the Boundless can one act without contrivance and accomplish without claiming credit

About This Chapter

Chapter 2 of the Daodejing is often read as a lesson in dialectical relativity and effortless conduct, yet Huang Yuanji probes straight to the roots of internal alchemy, unpacking the chapter into four practical stages—dissolving dualities, establishing the Mysterious Pass, harmonizing yin and yang, and entering effortless transformation:

  1. Transcending posterior dualities (§2, §5): The moment "beauty" and "goodness" are fixed as labels, "ugliness" and "badness" immediately shadow them—all belonging to Posterior Heaven discrimination. The true Way resides solely in that undivided One Breath of Prior Heaven at the dawn of life, prior to good and evil, thought and calculation.
  2. Lowering the curtains and regulating breath to establish the Mysterious Female (§3): In quiet sitting, lay down all entanglements, lower the curtains of the eyes toward the empty Cinnabar Field, and blend concentration of Spirit with regulating the breath until they fuse into one—entering the dim and obscure state (non-action), until out of utmost stillness a single awakening stirs (action), establishing the gate of the Mysterious Pass in the Center.
  3. Returning to the Boundless through yin and yang (§4, §6): The classic's six pairs—being and non-being, hard and easy, long and short, high and low, tone and voice, front and back—are, to Huang Yuanji, concrete markers of alchemical practice: stillness and movement, strong and gentle firing, circulation along the Conception and Governing vessels, conjunction of Kan and Li, and union of Qian and Kun—all pathways for "using posterior yin and yang to return to the prior-heaven Boundless."
  4. The genuine realm of accomplishing without claiming (§4, §6): The sage teaches without words and claims no credit for what is achieved—not out of feigned modesty to ward off envy, but as the natural overflow of calm, wondrous transformation: never speaking of merit, yet his merit rivals sun and moon; never claiming fame, yet his name endures across the ages.

Safety note: This chapter discusses seated concentration, breath regulation, and alchemical metaphors such as "Conception and Governing circulation" and "ascent and descent of Kan and Li." Beginners should simply sit comfortably at ease and breathe naturally, never using mental force to push blood or breath up and down the torso, which can cause dizziness, chest tightness, or energetic disruption. If any discomfort arises, stop immediately and take a relaxed walk.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：天下皆知美之为美，斯恶已；皆知善之为善，斯不善已。故有无相生，难易相成，长短相形，高下相倾，音声相和，前后相随。是以圣人处无为之事，行不言之教，万物作焉而不离，生而不有，为而不恃，功成而弗居；夫惟弗居，是以不去。

Lord Lao says: When everyone in the world clings to a fixed standard of "beauty" as beauty, "ugliness" is born right alongside it; when everyone clings to a fixed standard of "goodness" as good, "what is not good" is set up along with it. Thus being and non-being give rise to one another, hard and easy complete one another, long and short define one another, high and low incline toward one another, musical tone and human voice harmonize with one another, and front and back follow one another.

Therefore, the realized sage handles affairs through natural "non-action" (wuwei) and teaches through silent example; as the myriad things flourish and unfold, the sage remains seamlessly in resonance with them without ever parting; he gives them life yet does not possess them, acts on their behalf yet does not presume upon his power, brings great work to completion yet never claims the credit. Precisely because he never lays claim to it, that merit never leaves him.

§2
Original (Chinese)

古云：「劝君穷取生身处，返本还原是药王。」又曰：「穷取生身受命初，莫怪天机都泄尽。」由此观之，足见受命之初，浑然天理，无有瑕疵，彼说美说恶，说善说丑，皆为道之害也。夫道究何状哉？在儒家曰「隐微」，其中有不睹不闻之要；释家曰「那个」，其中有无善无恶之真；道家曰「玄关」，其中有无思无虑之密。大道根源，端本于此。一经想像，便落窠臼；一经拟议，便堕筌蹄。虽古来神仙，赞叹道妙，曰美曰善，要皆恍惚其象，非实有端倪。盖以为美也，就有恶对；以为善也，就有丑对。又况美在是，恶亦在是；善在是，丑亦在是。此殆后天阴阳，有对待，有胜负、参差，而非先天一元之气也。故太上曰：「天下皆知美之为美，斯恶已；皆知善之为善，斯不善已。」是知，人不求虚无一气，而第言美之为美、善之为善，是亦舍本而逐末也。

An ancient alchemical verse puts it with piercing clarity: "I urge you to trace to the very end the place where your body was born—returning to the root and restoring the origin is the King of Medicines." Another says: "Probe back to the very first instant you received life and Heaven's mandate; do not blame me for leaking all of Heaven's secret." From this we see clearly that at the dawn of receiving life, all is pure, undivided heavenly principle without the least blemish; all later talk of beauty and ugliness, good and bad, becomes a hindrance to realizing the Way.

What, then, does the Way truly look like? Confucians call it "the hidden and subtle," holding the unseen, unheard pivot; Chan Buddhists call it "That," holding the original reality beyond good and evil; Daoists call it the Mysterious Pass, holding the secret pivot free of thought and calculation. The very root of the Great Way stands squarely here. The instant you try to imagine it, you drop into old ruts; the instant you open your mouth to discuss and define it, you fall into the trap of words and concepts.

Although immortals of old sometimes praised the marvels of the Way with words like "beautiful" or "good," those were merely tentative, dim figures of speech, not fixed attributes to grasp. For the moment you declare something "beautiful," an "ugly" counterpart stands opposite it; the moment you declare something "good," an "evil" counterpart rises to match it. Worse still, beauty and ugliness sit entangled in the very same spot, and good and bad dwell side by side. All such distinctions belong to Posterior Heaven yin and yang—filled with opposition, winning and losing, and unevenness—never to the undivided One Breath of Prior Heaven. That is why Lord Lao says: "When everyone knows beauty as beauty, ugliness is already there; when everyone knows good as good, not-good is already there." Clearly, failing to seek that vacuous primordial Breath while chattering solely about beauty and goodness is abandoning the root to chase the branches.

§3
Original (Chinese)

太上特示下手之工，为大众告曰：凡人打坐之始，务将万缘放下，了无一事介于胸中，惟是垂帘塞兑，观照虚无丹田，即凝起神，又要调息，调起息，仍要凝神，如此久之，神气并成一团，顷刻间自入于杳冥之地，此无为也；及无之至极，忽然一觉而动，此有为焉。我于此一念从规中起，混混续续，兀兀腾腾，神依气立，气依神行，无知有知，有觉无觉，即玄牝之门立矣。由是恪守规中，凝神象外，一呼一吸，一往一来，务令气气归玄窍，息息任天然，即天地人物之根，圣贤仙佛之本，此最吾道家秘密天机，不容轻泄者也。

Lord Lao therefore specially reveals the practical starting method for all to see: whenever a beginner starts seated practice, lay down ten thousand worldly ties so not a single care lingers in the chest; simply lower the curtains of the eyes, close the mouth ("seal the opening"), and gaze gently inward at the hollow, vacuous Cinnabar Field.

How does the work proceed? While gathering Spirit, simultaneously attend to regulating the breath; while smoothing the breath, continue calmly holding Spirit together. Keeping them in quiet companionship like this, Spirit and Breath naturally blend into a single sphere, and before you know it you enter the clear, selfless realm of the dim and obscure—this is "non-action." Then, when this non-being reaches its utmost stillness, a single spark of clear awareness suddenly stirs within—this is "action."

At that very moment, a genuine impulse rises from the hollow "Center" (guizhong) of the body, seamlessly continuous and warmly buoyant; Spirit rests steadily upon Breath, and Breath moves along with Spirit. Call it unknowing, yet it is crystal clear; call it aware, yet not a single wandering thought stirs—right here the Gate of the Mysterious Female is established. From then on, faithfully guard this Center and hold Spirit quietly beyond fixed shapes; with every exhalation and inhalation, every going and coming, let each breath return to the Mysterious Opening and follow its own natural rhythm. This is the root of heaven, earth, humanity, and all things, the foundation of sages, worthies, immortals, and Buddhas—truly the deepest heavenly secret of our Daoist lineage, not to be lightly divulged.

§4
Original (Chinese)

修士行持，与其求之无极，不可捉摸，何若求之阴阳，更有实据。经曰：有无相生，不过动而静，静而动，出玄入牝，燮理阴阳者也。难易相成，不过刚而柔，柔而刚，鼎炉琴剑，一烹一温者也。长短相形，即出入呼吸，任督往来，前行短、后行长之谓也。高下相倾，即火在上而使之降，水在下而使之升，上下颠倒，坎离之妙用也。音声相和，即神融气畅，百脉流通，不啻鸣鹤呼群，同声相应，不召自来也。前后相随，即子驰于后，午降于前，乾坤交媾，和合一团，依依而不舍也。此数者，皆由后天之阴阳，而返乎先天之无极也。圣人知道之本原，冲漠无朕，浩荡无痕。其处事也，则以无为为尚，而共仰恭己垂裳之风；其行教也，则以不言为宗，而自寓过化存神之妙。圣人作而万物睹，又何离之有耶？自此耕田凿井，被生成而竟忘其行；开源节流，勤化导而并化其迹。即使功满乾坤，名闻天下，而圣人若耻为虚名，未尝有实绩也。夫岂若《书》云：「汝惟不矜不伐，天下莫与争能、争功」者，尚有弭人争竞之想哉？此殆归于神化之域，淡定之天，一惟自适其乐，而不忘自得之真。古言：视富贵如浮云，弃功名若敝履者，其斯之谓欤？虽然，道成德自立，实至名自归。圣人纵不居功，而天下后世，咸称道不衰。是不言功而功同日月，不言名而名重古今。夫惟弗居，是以不去也。

In actual cultivation, rather than straining from the outset after an ungraspable "Boundless," how much more concrete it is to begin with yin and yang! Look at the six pairs in the classic—every single phrase is a direct pointer to practice:

  • "Being and non-being give rise to one another": Simply motion turning into stillness and stillness stirring into motion, going out through the Mysterious and entering the Female, harmonizing yin and yang within.
  • "Hard and easy complete one another": Simply firmness alternating with gentleness—like alternating brisk martial fire to refine and gentle civil fire to warm in the tripod and furnace, taut and relaxed in right measure.
  • "Long and short define one another": The ebb and flow of breathing and vital movement circulating along the Conception and Governing vessels—the front Conception path shorter, the back Governing path longer, answering one another in a continuous loop.
  • "High and low incline toward one another": Allowing heart-fire above to descend and kidney-water below to ascend, inverting high and low so they mingle—the wondrous function of the conjunction of Kan and Li.
  • "Tone and voice harmonize with one another": Spirit melting softly and Breath flowing freely through all hundred channels, like a calling crane answered by its flock—resonant tones echoing without being summoned.
  • "Front and back follow one another": Vital movement rising along the back Governing path at the yang Zi phase and descending along the front Conception path at the yin Wu phase, Qian and Kun uniting into one harmonious sphere, lingering together without parting.

All six are ways of "moving through Posterior Heaven yin and yang to return to the Prior Heaven Boundless."

The sage knows well that the source of the Way is vast, tranquil, and without trace or seam. Thus in ordering the world he prizes non-action, embodying the serene dignity of the sage-kings of old who sat composed with robes hanging loose while all under heaven rested in peace; in teaching others he makes wordless influence his mainstay, naturally transforming wherever he passes and settling hearts in unseen quiet. When the sage stands forth, all things look to him in natural response—how could there ever be estrangement or separation? People dig their wells and till their fields, nurtured in peace without ever feeling governed; the sage opens sources of livelihood, curbs waste, and guides diligently, yet dissolves every trace of his own deeds afterward.

Even when his accomplishments fill heaven and earth and his reputation spans the realm, the sage feels almost shy of hollow renown, as if he had done nothing remarkable at all. How different this is from the counsel in the Book of Documents—"Only because you do not boast or brag does no one under heaven contend with your ability or merit"—which still retains a calculation about avoiding rivalry! The sage has entered the realm of wondrous transformation and tranquil poise, simply delighting in natural ease and never parting from genuine self-contentment. When the ancients spoke of "viewing wealth and rank as floating clouds" and "casting aside fame and merit like worn-out sandals," was it not this very state they meant? Even so, once the Way is perfected, virtue stands firm of itself; where solid reality exists, renown follows naturally. Though the sage never claims credit, generations across the world praise him without end: he never speaks of merit, yet his merit equals sun and moon; he never seeks fame, yet his name weighs heavy across antiquity and today. Precisely because he never lays claim to it, it never departs.

§5
Original (Chinese)

学者须从虚极静笃中，养出无美无善之真出来，才算修炼有本。其道维何？玄关窍也。舍此则无生矣。修道者，舍此玄关一窍，别无所谓道矣。如以美善为道，亦属后天尘垢。太上以此言警之，望人因流而溯源也。不然，美善之称，亦三代下之君子，又乌可厚非哉？

The student must nurture, out of utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness, that prior-heaven authenticity where neither "beauty" nor "goodness" is even posited—only then does cultivation have a true root. What is this path? The single opening of the Mysterious Pass. Apart from it there is no generation of life; apart from this Mysterious Pass, the cultivator has no other Way to speak of. Treating worldly notions of "beauty" and "goodness" as the Way itself still belongs to posterior dust and stains. Lord Lao speaks thus to awaken people and lead them back up the stream to its source. Otherwise, on the plane of ordinary human conduct, someone worthy of being called "fine and good" is already a rare noble person since the Three Dynasties—how could one harshly fault that?

§6
Original (Chinese)

《易》曰：「一阴一阳之谓道。」是阳非道也，阴亦非道，道其在阴阳之间乎！又况道者理也，阴阳者气也。理无气不立，气无理不行。单言道，实无端倪可状，惟即阴阳发见者观之，庶确有实据。此章言无美无善之真，直抉大道根源，望人端本立极，以为修身治世之基。「有无难易」数句，是教人由对待之阴阳，返乎真一之气。其中又教人，从有无相入处，寻出玄关一窍，为炼丹本根。至于守中养丹，阳生活子，运转河车，亦无不层层抉破。惟圣人直探其源，故恭己无为，不言而信，虽有生有为，而在己毫无德色。迨至功成告退，视富贵为不足重轻，非圣人，其孰能与于斯？学者玩索而有得，非但下手有基，即通天亦有路矣。他注云：天下皆知美善之所以为美善，则自不为恶与不善矣。此讲亦是。但太上之经，多在原头上说，不落二乘。

The Book of Changes says, "One yin and one yang—that is called the Way." This means yang alone is not the Way, nor is yin alone the Way; the Way lies right in the living interplay between yin and yang! Moreover, the Way is formless principle (li), while yin and yang are moving Breath (qi). Without Breath, principle has nowhere to stand; without principle, Breath has no way to move. Speaking of the Way in isolation leaves nothing visible to grasp; only by observing it where yin and yang manifest do we gain solid footing.

This chapter first unveils the prior-heaven truth beyond beauty and goodness, laying bare the root of the Great Way so that people may establish a sound foundation for both self-cultivation and ordering the world. Next, the lines on "being and non-being, hard and easy" teach us to move from relative yin and yang back to the undivided True One Breath—and show us how, right where being and non-being interpenetrate, to recognize the Mysterious Pass as the root of refining the Elixir. As for guarding the center to nurture the Elixir, yang stirring at the Living Zi Hour, and turning the River Chariot (Celestial Circuit), all are unlocked layer by layer between the lines.

Only the sage probes directly to this source; therefore he stays composed in non-action and inspires trust without speaking—giving life and acting greatly yet showing not a trace of self-congratulation. When the work is done and he steps back, wealth and rank weigh no more than a feather; who but a sage could reach such stature? If a student ponders this chapter deeply and realizes its meaning, not only will his initial practice stand on solid rock—the broad highway ascending to Heaven will lie clear before him.

Other commentators often explain the opening lines as: "If everyone understood why beauty and goodness are truly beautiful and good, they would naturally refrain from ugliness and evil." That makes good sense on the level of everyday morality, yet Lord Lao's classic always speaks from the ultimate source and refuses to settle for secondary homilies.

Key Terms

  • Mysterious Pass (Gate of the Mysterious Female, the Center): The prior-heaven pivot of vitality appearing where utmost stillness stirs and being meets non-being; called "the hidden and subtle" by Confucians, "That" by Chan Buddhists, and the "Mysterious Pass" by Daoists.
  • Lowering the curtains and sealing the opening: Gently lowering the eyelids to turn gaze inward ("lowering the curtains") while keeping the mouth closed to curb sensory leakage ("sealing the opening")—the paired opening posture for seated quieting.
  • Conjunction of Kan and Li ("high and low incline toward one another"): Li-fire resides above (heart) and Kan-water below (kidneys); through regulating breath and settling Spirit, fire descends and water rises so water and fire harmonize and Spirit and Breath unite.
  • Conception and Governing vessels and River Chariot (Celestial Circuit): The front Conception and rear Governing pathways forming a midline loop ("front shorter, back longer"; "Zi racing up the back, Wu descending the front"), referring to the natural cyclical ascent and descent of vital Breath.
  • Guarding the center and Living Zi Hour: Resting calmly in the central palace in stillness, welcoming the first subtle stirring of yang without clinging to fixed shapes or slipping into torpor.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Step back from habitual black-and-white judging. Most daily emotional friction comes from endlessly labeling people and events as "good/bad" or "win/lose." Stepping back now and then to observe plainly without rushing to judge softens the mind at once.
  2. Steady restlessness with "settling Spirit + regulating breath." When thoughts scatter, do not try to bully your mind into "thinking of nothing." Softly lower your eyelids and let attention rest lightly on your breath and midsection so mind and breath slow down together.
  3. Practice "letting go" once a task is done. After doing a kindness or finishing a project, there is no need to replay "that was all my doing" in your head. Turning your focus back to the smooth unfolding of the work itself builds far deeper trust.
  4. Seek balance between stillness and action, not an either-or split. Cultivation is not hiding in dead stillness and doing nothing; genuine skill is "firmness and gentleness completing each other"—focused when effort is called for, relaxed and open when it is time to release.

Translator's Notes

  1. "As the myriad things flourish and unfold, [the sage] does not part from them" (wanwu zuoyan er bu li): Standard editions of the Daodejing (such as Wang Bi's) read 不辞 ("does not decline / refuse them"), whereas several Qing alchemical editions and Huang Yuanji's base text read 不离 ("does not part from them"), which Huang explicitly expounds below ("When the sage stands forth, all things behold him—how could there be parting?"). Our text and translation follow Huang's reading.
  2. "I urge you to trace to the very end the place where your body was born...": A famous couplet widely quoted in internal alchemy classics such as the Pointers on Inner Nature and Life (Xingming guizhi), emphasizing that the Elixir is not sought outside oneself but in returning to the primordial Breath of conception.
  3. "Old ruts" (kejiu) / "fish-traps and rabbit-snares" (quanti): Kejiu means stale clichés or set molds; quanti alludes to Zhuangzi ("External Things"), where the trap and snare symbolize words and concepts—clinging to the tool makes one miss the reality.
  4. "Composed with robes hanging loose" (gongji chuishang) / "transforming wherever he passes" (guohua cunshen): The first blends Book of Changes ("Appended Remarks II": "The Yellow Emperor, Yao, and Shun let their robes hang loose and all under heaven was well-governed") with the Analects; the second comes from Mencius ("Jin Xin I"), describing the sage's effortless, numinous moral influence.
  5. "Only because you do not boast or brag...": From the Book of Documents ("Counsels of Great Yu"), where Shun praises Yu for never boasting of his talent or parading his achievements.
  6. Textual note: Numerous woodblock/OCR typos in the source edition have been corrected (日 → 曰 in several citations, 进尽 → 泄尽, 窠白 → 窠臼, 丁无一事 → 了无一事, 人于香冥 → 入于杳冥, 出玄人牝 → 出玄入牝, 便之降 → 使之降, 恭已/在已 → 恭己/在己, 争竟 → 争竞, 奔功名 → 弃功名); an old interlinear note misstating the standard variant of 离 as 静 (for 辞) has been corrected and moved here.
