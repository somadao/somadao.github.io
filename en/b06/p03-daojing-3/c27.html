Chapter 27 · Always Good at Saving People

No tracks, no flaws, no tally sticks, no bolts, no ropes—worldly conduct and inner refinement are one single whole; supreme fire phasing follows nature without contrived traces, and the doorway to the Way lies wholly in taking the good as teachers and the bad as mirrors

About This Chapter

Chapter 27 strings together five instances of "good at"—good at walking, good at speaking, good at reckoning, good at shutting, good at binding—to portray the sage's seamless, traceless mastery, before turning to "always good at saving people, so no one is cast aside; always good at saving things, so nothing is wasted," and the deep worldly wisdom that "the good and the not-good serve as each other's teacher and resource." Huang Yuanji analyzes the chapter on two levels, seamlessly uniting "outer kingly conduct" with "inner sage refinement":

  1. In worldly conduct: uncontrived responsiveness makes every person a teacher. The sage demands perfection only of himself, never harshly of others; responding smoothly to season and circumstance without artifice or bias, he casts aside neither person nor thing. Even finer is "taking the good as teachers and the bad as warnings"—learning from the worthy and examining oneself in the mirror of the unworthy, so no encounter in life is ever wasted.
  2. In inner refinement: all five "goods" map directly onto natural alchemy. With keen insight, Huang Yuanji reads every single one of the five phrases through internal alchemy: "good walking leaves no tracks" is the Waterwheel turning of itself rather than forced mental hauling; "good speaking leaves no flaws" is mind-to-mind transmission beyond words; "good reckoning uses no tally sticks" is natural fire phasing rather than mechanical counting; "good shutting uses no bolts" is undivided guarding of the center to form the Embryo; and "good binding uses no ropes" is Spirit and Breath naturally clinging together to condense the Pearl of Millet.
  3. The shared threshold for sage and ordinary person alike: However subtle the theory sounds, the actual starting point is astonishingly plain—simply "moving toward good when you see it, correcting faults when you have them." Anyone who grows arrogant—"neither honoring their teacher nor cherishing their resource"—remains locked outside the gate of the Way, no matter how clever they are.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：善行无辙迹，善言无瑕摘，善计不用筹策，善闭无关键而不可开，善结无绳约而不可解。是以圣人常善救人，故无弃人；常善救物，故无弃物，是谓袭明。故善人者，不善人之师；不善人者，善人之资。不贵其师，不爱其资，虽智大迷，是谓要妙。（袭者，重也。《易》曰「重明以丽乎天下」是也）

Laozi says: One truly good at traveling leaves no wheel ruts or footprints; one truly good at speaking leaves no flaw open to blame; one truly good at reckoning uses no counting rods or tally sticks; one truly good at shutting uses neither bolt nor latch, yet nothing can pry it open; one truly good at binding uses neither cord nor knot, yet nothing can untie it.

Therefore the sage is always good at rescuing and fulfilling people, so no person is ever cast aside; always good at cherishing and using things, so nothing is ever wasted—this is called "inheriting and doubling brightness" (ximing). Thus the good person is the teacher of the not-good, and the not-good is the resource of the good. If one neither honors one's teacher nor cherishes one's resource, though clever one is deeply deluded—this is called the subtle essential.

(Original note: Xi means "doubled" or "layered." As the Book of Changes says, "doubled brightness illuminating all under heaven"—precisely this meaning.)

§2
Original (Chinese)

圣人之心，只求诸己，不求诸人；其施之于事物也，无为不通，随在皆当，内无歉于己，外无恶于人，《易》所谓「时止则止，时行则行，动静不失其时，其道光明」，殆斯人欤？其于行也，时而可行，行之而已，前不见其所来，后不见其所往，抑何辙迹之俱无哉？其行之善有如此。其于言也，时当可言，言之而已，内不见辱于己，外不贻羞于人，抑何瑕摘之悉化哉？其言之善有如此。至于物之当计，事之宜筹，揆之以理，度之以情，顺理而施，如情而止，宜多则多，当少则少，何须筹策之劳？即此因应无心，物我俱化，非善计而何？更有宜闭、宜结之事，其在他人，不闭则乱，不结则散，而圣人外缘胥绝，内念不生，完完全全，非所谓善闭、善结者乎？虽无绳约之束、关键之防，而无隙可乘，俨若弥缝甚固，其不可开不可解也，不诚天理浑全、无懈可击耶？之数者，殆顺乎自然之天，不参以人为之伪，故其效如此。要皆内修而无外慕，自正而无他求，所以立己立人，人无遗类，成己成物，物无弃材，其济人利物之善，为何如者？是皆自明明德，又推之以理民及物，不谓之重袭其明哉？然而善人初不自知也。善人浑忘物我，故不善者感之而尊为师；善人亦不自满也，见不善人，善人即以之为资，见善则从，不善则改，善人所由益进于善，而至于美大化神之域焉。若凡人，自恃其才，自逞其能，见善者，置之不问，不知奉以为模；不善者，弃之如遗，反鄙之而不屑，不知见贤思齐，不贤内省，善恶虽殊，而为己之师资则一也。似此不贵其师，不爱其资，殆愚而好自用，贱而好自专者，不诚昏昧人哉？夫善者师之，恶者戒之，随在皆有益于己，无人不有益于身，是诚修己之要术，治身之妙道也。人其勉之！

The sage's heart demands perfection only of himself, never of others. When he engages affairs and things, without contrived effort nothing is blocked and every response fits its moment—inwardly leaving no regret, outwardly giving no offense. When the Book of Changes says, "Resting when it is time to rest, moving when it is time to move; neither stillness nor movement misses its season, so his Way shines bright," is it not describing just such a person?

When it is time to act, he simply acts: ahead one sees no scheming arrival, behind one sees no ostentatious wake—how completely free of wheel ruts and footprints! Such is his excellence in action. When it is time to speak, he simply speaks: inwardly he never demeans himself, outwardly he never shames another—how completely free of flaws open to criticism! Such is his excellence in speech.

As for things needing measurement or affairs needing planning, he weighs them by reason and gauges them by human feeling, acting in accord with principle and stopping where feeling calls for rest—giving much when much is fitting, little when little is right. What need is there to toil over counting rods? This effortless, uncontrived responsiveness in which self and things blend as one—what is it if not excellence in reckoning?

And when something calls for shutting or binding: with ordinary people, leave it unlocked and chaos breaks out; leave it untied and it falls apart. Yet the sage severs outward entanglements and raises no stray inward thoughts—utterly whole and intact. Is he not the very image of "good shutting" and "good binding"? Though there are no ropes tying it down and no bolts barring the gate, not the tiniest crack offers an opening; it is sealed as seamlessly as heavenly craftsmanship, impossible to pry open or unravel—is this not the unbroken wholeness of Heavenly Principle, completely unassailable?

All five of these spring from following natural Heaven without mixing in human artifice; hence their wondrous effect. At bottom, all arise from cultivating oneself inwardly without craving outward praise, rectifying oneself without demanding of others. Thus establishing himself he establishes others, leaving no human being forsaken; fulfilling himself he fulfills things, leaving no material wasted. How vast is such goodness in aiding humanity and benefiting all creatures! First brightening one's own luminous virtue, then extending it to nurture people and things—is this not truly "inheriting and doubling brightness"?

Yet the truly good person is not even self-conscious of being good. Because he forgets the boundary between self and others, the not-good are deeply moved and honor him as teacher. At the same time, the good person never grows complacent: meeting someone who falls short, he immediately treats them as a mirror and resource—following whatever is good and correcting whatever is bad. That is how the good person advances ever deeper into goodness until entering the lofty realms of beauty, greatness, sagehood, and spirit.

By contrast, ordinary folk rely on petty cleverness and love flaunting their abilities: meeting a truly good person, they brush past without inquiry, failing to take them as a model; meeting an unworthy person, they cast them aside in disdain, failing to remember the maxim "when you see the worthy, think of equaling them; when you see the unworthy, examine yourself within." Though good and bad differ in conduct, their value as your own teacher and resource is one and the same! Thus neither honoring one's teacher nor cherishing one's resource—foolish yet fond of self-will, shallow yet fond of dictating—is that not utter blindness? Take the good as your teacher and the bad as your warning: everywhere you turn brings benefit, and every person you meet aids your cultivation. This is truly the essential craft of self-cultivation and the wondrous Way of governing oneself—strive for it!

§3
Original (Chinese)

此见圣人之语，无所不通。事物之理，即性命之道，体用原是兼赅，本末由来不离。如云「善行无辙迹」，推之气机流行，河车自运，亦是如此；若有迹象，即属搬运存想，非自在河车，上合天道之流行。曰「善言无瑕摘」，即「无法可说，是名说法」。又曰：「祖师西来意。」孔子曰：「天何言哉？四时行焉，百物生焉。」有瑕可摘，即有言可见，非圣人心领神会之宗旨。释氏曰「道本无言，却被人说坏了」，是其意矣。曰「善计无筹策」，周天之数，不过喻名三百之数，实非有爻策可计，有则非自然火候。曰「善闭无关键」，本是鸿濛未破，元神默默，元气冥冥，返还于元始之初，以结胎而成圣；若有闭则有开，非内炼之道也。曰「善结无绳约」，言神恋气而凝，命依性而住，神气吻合，复还太极，以结成黍米之珠、阳神之体。若有则勉强撮合，非自然之凝聚，而不可以复命归真。顾其功效如此，而修养之要，不过见善则迁，有过则改，取法乎善与不善之类，返观内省，以为功也。倘矜才恃智，傲法凌人，不贵其师，不爱其资，纵有才智，亦愚昧之夫，终不足以入道矣。于此见修道之要妙，圣凡原同一辙焉。

From this chapter we see even more clearly that the sage's words reach everywhere without obstruction. The principles of everyday affairs are identical with the Way of inner nature and life; substance and function encompass each other, and root and branch can never be severed.

Look at "good walking leaves no tracks": extended to inner alchemy, when vital breath circulates and the Waterwheel turns of its own accord, it is just the same—if deliberate tracks can be felt, that has fallen into mentally hauling and imagining energy, never the effortless Waterwheel harmonizing above with Heaven's course.

Look at "good speaking leaves no flaws": it matches Buddhism's "there is no Dharma to be spoken—this is called speaking the Dharma," and Chan's question about "the Patriarch's meaning in coming from the West." Confucius likewise exclaimed, "Does Heaven ever speak? Yet the four seasons turn and the hundred things grow!" Wherever flaws can be picked, one has fallen into the cage of words and phrases, far from the sage's silent heart-to-heart purport. As Buddhists say, "The Way is originally wordless—it is only people who talk it to pieces!"

Look at "good reckoning uses no tally sticks": the three hundred and sixty degrees and hexagram lines spoken of in the Celestial Circuit are merely metaphors for rounded rhythm, not literal stalks to be counted off mechanically—once you count rigidly, it is no longer natural fire phasing.

Look at "good shutting uses no bolts": originally it is guarding unbroken primeval wholeness, where Original Spirit rests silent and Original Breath abides deep and still, returning to the primordial beginning to form the Embryo and achieve sagehood. If you rely on forcible shutting, whatever is forcefully shut can also be forced open—that is never the Way of inner refinement.

Look at "good binding uses no ropes": it means Spirit naturally cherishes Breath and condenses, Life naturally leans on Nature and settles, and Spirit and Breath fit seamlessly together, returning to the Supreme Ultimate to crystallize the "Pearl of Millet" (Holy Embryo) and the body of Pure Yang Spirit. If ropes are needed, that is forced mental gluing rather than natural cohesion, incapable of restoring Life and returning to Truth.

Yet marvelous as these inner attainments are, the practical essence of cultivation comes down to two plain rules: "move toward good when you see it, correct faults when you have them"—drawing lessons alike from the good and the not-good, turning inward in constant self-examination as one's daily work. If instead one prides oneself on talent and intellect, scorns the Way and looks down on others, neither honoring one's teacher nor cherishing one's resource, then however brilliant one may be, one remains a blind fool who can never enter the gate of the Way. Thus we see that the deepest essential of cultivating the Way runs along the exact same track for sage and ordinary person alike!

Key Terms

  • Inheriting and doubling brightness (ximing): xi means "doubled" or "passed onward"—first brightening one's own virtue, then extending it to aid people and cherish things so light passes on layer upon layer without dying out.
  • Spontaneous Waterwheel (heche ziyun): the state in internal alchemy where abundant true breath circulates naturally along the vessels, contrasted with deliberately "hauling and imagining" energy by mental force.
  • fire phasing (huohou): the subtle modulation of awareness, breath tempo, and timing in inner refinement; here Huang Yuanji stresses that Celestial Circuit degrees and hexagram tallies are only metaphors, while authentic mastery is natural fire phasing "using no tally sticks."
  • Pearl of Millet (shumi zhi zhu): an alchemical metaphor for the pure spiritual elixir condensed in the Cinnabar Field when Spirit and Breath unite seamlessly, suspended bright as a single grain of millet.
  • Pure Yang Spirit (yangshen): the pure-yang body of Original Spirit refined in the highest stages of internal alchemy.

Putting It into Practice

  1. The highest skill leaves no labored trace. Whether leading a team, helping a friend, or steadying yourself, truly effective action fits the grain of reason and feeling without fanfare; the harder you strain to show everyone how hard you are working, the more tracks and flaws you leave behind.
  2. Treat difficult people as a free mirror. Learn humbly from people you admire ("honoring your teacher"), and when someone irritates or offends you, pause before reacting with contempt—ask yourself, "Do I carry a trace of that very trait?" That is "cherishing your resource."
  3. Waste no experience and discard no part of yourself. Applied to personal growth, "leaving no person forsaken and no material wasted" means accepting your imperfect edges and turning setbacks into nourishment rather than bitterness.
  4. Watch out for over-counting and tightness in practice. In quiet sitting or breathwork, constantly counting scores, checking boxes, or tensing up for fear of "losing energy" falls into ropes and bolts; drop the tension, and Spirit and Breath naturally settle together.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Good speaking leaves no flaws" (wuxia zhai): Most received editions of the Daodejing read wuxia zhe (无瑕谪, "free from blame"), while Huang Yuanji's base text reads wuxia zhai (无瑕摘, "no flaw to pick at") and glosses it accordingly in §3; the original reading is preserved.
  2. "The realms of beauty, greatness, sagehood, and spirit" (mei da hua shen): From Mencius (7B.25: "Fullness is called beauty; fullness with radiance is called greatness; greatness that transforms is called sagehood; sagehood beyond knowing is called spirit").
  3. "There is no Dharma to be spoken—this is called speaking the Dharma": Adapts the Diamond Sutra; "the Patriarch's meaning in coming from the West" is a classic Chan koan pointing to direct transmission beyond words.
  4. "Does Heaven ever speak? Yet the four seasons turn and the hundred things grow!": Quoted from the Analects (17.19), showing that the Way operates silently.
  5. Textual collation: Base-edition misprints for "never shames another" (外不贻羞于人), "is this not called doubling brightness?" (不谓之重袭其明哉), a stray character (進) in "take them as a model" (不知奉以为模), "good and bad differ" (善恶虽殊), "Does Heaven ever speak?" (天何言哉), and "Pearl of Millet" (黍米之珠), along with repeated ji (己) / yi (已) and yue (曰) / ri (日) confusions, have been corrected in the Chinese source.
