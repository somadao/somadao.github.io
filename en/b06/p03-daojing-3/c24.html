III · Classic of the Way (Ch. 21–30) · 道经

Chapter 24 · Standing on Tiptoe

True non-action and naturalness are neither dull torpor nor laxity, but mirror-like purity and steadying the mind by nurturing breath—every excess strain of tiptoeing or self-display ends up harming both body and Way

About This Chapter

Chapter 24 carries forward the closing theme of "few words and naturalness" from the previous chapter. In rapid succession, Laozi names six forms of overexertion—standing on tiptoe, striding too wide, putting oneself on display, insisting one is right, boasting of merit, and puffing up with pride—and brands them all "leftover food and tumorous growths," excess baggage that even external creatures find repulsive. Huang Yuanji unpacks this across two dimensions: cultivating the mind and nurturing breath in inner refinement:

  1. "Naturalness" is not lazy "stubborn emptiness," but balanced centeredness: Standing on tiptoe lets you peer farther, yet you cannot stay up for long; spreading your legs wide fits a horse's saddle, yet you cannot walk far that way. The "non-action and naturalness" of the Way is neither dull stupor nor careless indulgence, but moving in accord with Heavenly Principle and human reality—neither overdoing nor falling short.
  2. The two words "pure and still" (qingjing) hold lively radiant light: True "purity" (qing) means not a single stray thought arises; true "stillness/cleanliness" (jing) means not a speck of dust stains the mind. Yet it is never the groggy idleness of an ordinary person doing nothing—it is "light within purity, scenery within stillness," like a clear deep pool reflecting the bright moon, luminous through and through.
  3. Nurturing the mind must rest on "nurturing Breath": Quoting Mencius, Huang Yuanji puts his finger on the crux: merely talking about "nurturing the mind" often leaves people restless for a whole lifetime. One must first settle the breath—when breath is steady, Spirit grows luminous; when Spirit is luminous, the mind settles naturally into peace. Once the heart is truly pure and true yang arises of itself, every ascent and descent of vital breath must be allowed to unfold naturally, never pushed or forced like standing on tiptoe or over-striding.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：跂者不立，跨者不行，自见者不明，自是者不彰，自伐者无功，自矜者不长。其于道也，曰余食赘行，物或恶之，故有道者不处也。（跂，音器）

Laozi says: One who stands on tiptoe cannot stand steady; one who strides too wide cannot walk far; one who puts himself on display does not see clearly; one who insists he is always right does not shine; one who boasts of his merits achieves no true merit; one who prides himself on superiority cannot long endure as a leader.

Measured against the Way, these are called "excess food and superfluous growths"—even creatures recoil from them. Therefore one who holds the Way never abides in such states.

(Original note: Qi is pronounced like qi ["vessel"], meaning to stand up on tiptoe.)

§2
Original (Chinese)

前云希言自然，非若世之蚩蚩蠢蠢，顽空以为无为，放旷以为自然者比，其殆本大中至正之道，准天理人情，循圣功王道，操存省察，返本还原，以上合乎天命，故无为而无不为，自然而无不然也。《易》曰：「穷理尽性，以至于命」殆其人欤？过则病，不及亦病，《书》曰「无偏无党，王道荡荡」是也。即如人之立也，原有常不易。跂者，两足支也。《诗》曰：「跂予望之。」以之望人，则可高瞻而远瞩，若欲久立，其可得乎？跨者，两足张也。以之跨马，则可居于鞍背，若欲步行，又焉能乎？明者不自是，自是则不明。彰者不自见，自见则不彰。自伐者在在无功，有功者物莫能掩，何用伐为？自矜者往往无长，有长者人自敬服，奚用矜为？若不信无为自然之道，不知「莫之为而为，莫之致而致」，致为皆听诸天，何等自在！「行乎不得不行，止乎不得不止」，行止浑于无心，何等安然？倘不知「虚而无朕」，即是「大而能容」，或加一意，参一见，若食者之过饱，行者之过劳，非徒无益，而又害之。学者须顺天德之无违，循物理之自得，不惟人不可参杂作伪于其间，即物亦当听其安闲，调其饮食。苟稍不得其宜，越乎常度，或多食之，或苦行之，如犬之过饱则伤，牛之过劳则困，是亦不安于内而有恶于己焉，故曰「物或恶之」。彼矫揉造作，以期能立、能行，昭明表彰，功堪动人，长可迈众者，断断乎其难之也。有道之君子，深为鄙之，不屑处己。

When the previous chapter spoke of "few words and naturalness," do not mistake it for the dull, clueless ways of the world—taking lifeless "stubborn emptiness" for non-action or careless laxity for naturalness! What it truly means is rooted in the Way of Great Equilibrium and Supreme Uprightness: taking Heavenly Principle and human reality as its measure, following the upright path of sagehood and true governance, maintaining steady mindfulness and self-examination, and returning to the origin so as to unite above with Heaven's Mandate. That is why it "does nothing yet leaves nothing undone, stays natural yet leaves nothing unaccomplished." When the Book of Changes says, "fathoming principle, fulfilling inner nature, and thereby arriving at destiny," is it not speaking of just such a person?

Going too far is a sickness, and falling short is also a sickness—as the Book of Documents says, "Without bias, without faction, broad and level is the Royal Way." Take the simple act of standing: setting both feet flat on the earth is the constant, unchanging norm. "Qi" means propping oneself up on tiptoe—as the Classic of Poetry says, "On tiptoe I gaze out for him." Raising your heels to gaze into the distance indeed lets you see high and far, yet if you try to stand like that for long, how could you? "Kua" means spreading both legs wide apart—to mount a horse, that lets you sit securely astride the saddle, yet if you try to travel on foot that way, how could you walk?

One who truly sees clearly does not insist he is right; the moment you insist you are right, clarity dims. One whose virtue truly shines does not put himself on display; the moment you display yourself, luster fades. Those who boast of merit end up with no real merit everywhere—when real merit exists, nothing in creation can hide it, so what need is there to brag? Those who puff themselves up rarely keep their preeminence—when true stature exists, people naturally respect and defer to it, so why put on airs?

If people distrust the Way of non-action and naturalness, failing to realize how things come to pass "done without anyone forcing them, arriving without anyone contriving them"—leaving action and outcome to Heaven's order—how could they taste that freedom? Failing to live by "moving when one cannot but move, stopping when one cannot but stop"—moving and resting completely free of forced contrivance—how could they know that deep ease? Failing to see that "emptiness without trace" is precisely "vastness able to contain all," they insist on adding a private intention here or mixing in a fixed opinion there—just like eating past fullness or marching past exhaustion, not only bringing zero benefit, but actively inflicting harm.

A student must harmonize with Heavenly Virtue without opposition and follow the innate ease of all things. Not only must human beings avoid injecting contrivance and artifice, even with animals and creatures around us we should allow them quiet ease and measured nourishment. Once anything exceeds its proper measure—whether overfed or overworked, like a dog sickened by overeating or an ox broken down by over-toil—it feels uneasy within and recoils against the excess itself. Hence Laozi says, "even creatures recoil from them." As for those who rely on strained artifice to force themselves to stand taller, stride faster, shine brighter, win acclaim, and outstrip the crowd—they will never succeed. A noble person of the Way looks down on such posturing from the heart and disdains to put himself in such a state.

§3
Original (Chinese)

此希言自然，不外一个清净。何谓清？一念不起时也。何谓净？纤尘不染候也。总要此心如明镜无尘，如止水无波，只一片空洞了灵之神，即清净矣。倘若世之庸夫俗子，昏昏罔罔，终日无一事为，即非清净。惟清中有光，净中有景，不啻澄潭明月，一片光华，乃得清净之实。若有一毫自见自是、自伐自矜之意，便是障碍。所以学道人，务使心怀浩荡，无一事一物，搅我心头，据我灵府，久久涵养，一点灵光普照，恍如日月之在天，无微不入焉。只怕一念之明，复一念之肆，则明者不常明矣。昔孟子之所长在于养气，气不动则神自灵，神灵则心自泰，故不曰「养心」，而曰「养气」，诚以志壹则动气，气壹则动志也。苟不求养气，而徒曰养心，无惑乎终身不得其心之宁者多矣。心果清净，真阳自生，一切升降运行，顺其自然为要。如跂者必使之立，跨者必使之行，余食过饱，赘行过劳，皆未得其当，物犹恶之，而况人乎？是以有道之君子，不忍处此也。

Brought down to actual practice, "few words and naturalness" comes to nothing more than two words: "purity and stillness" (qingjing). What is "purity" (qing)? The moment when not a single stray thought arises. What is "stillness/cleanliness" (jing)? The stage when not a speck of worldly dust stains the heart. In short, the mind must become like a bright mirror free of dust, like still water free of ripples—leaving only an open, lucid, radiant expanse of Spirit: that is true purity and stillness.

If one merely drifts like a dull, muddled commoner—groggy and blank, doing nothing worthwhile all day—that is never purity and stillness. Only when there is radiant light within purity and living clarity within stillness, like a crystal-clear deep pool mirroring a full moon in one seamless sheen of light, does one touch the reality of purity and stillness. Even a hair's breadth of self-display, self-righteousness, boasting, or pride becomes an immediate obstruction.

Therefore, a student of the Way must cultivate a vast, open heart, allowing no single affair or object to stir up the mind or occupy the spiritual chamber. Nurtured steadily over time, a single spark of luminous light shines forth everywhere, like sun and moon suspended in the sky, penetrating the finest crevices. The only danger is letting one moment of clarity be followed by a moment of lax indulgence—then what was bright fails to stay bright.

Long ago, Mencius's great strength lay in "nurturing Breath": when breath stays settled and unruffled, Spirit naturally turns luminous; when Spirit is luminous, the heart naturally rests in peace. That is why Mencius did not stop at saying "nurture the mind," but specifically emphasized "nurturing breath"—truly because "when the will is unified it moves the breath, and when the breath is unified it moves the will." If one makes no effort to nurture and steady the breath, and only talks loosely about "nurturing the mind," no wonder so many spend a whole lifetime without ever finding peace of heart!

Once the mind truly becomes pure and still, true yang arises of its own accord; from that point on, the supreme essential for every rise, fall, and circulation of breath is simply to follow its natural course. Forcing it—like trying to stand permanently on tiptoe or walk in an exaggerated straddle, like gorging on excess food or pushing oneself into needless toil—misses the mark completely. Even dumb creatures recoil from such excess; how much more human beings? That is why a noble person of the Way cannot bear to place himself in such a state.

Key Terms

  • Stubborn emptiness (wankong): mistaking Daoist "emptiness" and "purity and stillness" for dead wood, cold ashes, or dull, unfeeling stupor devoid of inner brightness and vitality.
  • Purity and stillness (qingjing): defined by Huang Yuanji as "not a single stray thought arising (qing), not a speck of dust staining the heart (jing)"—never dull blankness, but lucid clarity with "light within purity, scenery within stillness."
  • Spiritual chamber (lingfu): an elegant Daoist name for the original source of the mind (from Zhuangzi, "The Sign of Virtue Complete"), the seat where true Spirit abides.
  • Nurturing Breath (yangqi): citing Mencius's "nurturing my flood-like breath" and "when the will is unified it moves the breath; when the breath is unified it moves the will," pointing to steadying inner breath so that Spirit grows luminous and the heart comes to rest.
  • Excess food and superfluous growths (yushi zhuixing): literally leftover/excessive eating and extra growths or redundant actions; in cultivation, a metaphor for overdoing, forcing, and adding contrived effort against the natural fire phasing.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Tell the difference between true stillness and groggy spacing out. If sitting quietly leaves your head heavy and foggy, that is drowsiness or zoning out, not qingjing. True stillness feels physically relaxed yet bright and clear, like a clear pool reflecting the moon.
  2. When anxious, steady the breath before trying to reason with the mind. Just as Huang Yuanji points out through Mencius, commanding your brain to "stop worrying and calm down" usually backfires. Slow and settle your breathing first, and the mind naturally follows into ease.
  3. Watch out for the "tiptoe" mindset in practice and work. Tiptoeing gives you a brief view from above, yet you cannot hold it for three minutes. Any peak achieved by burning through sleep, forcing results, or frantically proving yourself is "standing on tiptoe"—far inferior to walking steadily with both feet on the ground.
  4. Drop the reflex to justify yourself and show off. When you accomplish something, skip the urge to brag; when you hold an insight, skip the urge to insist you are right. Pulling back the energy spent proving yourself to others frees up room to build real inner stability.

Translator's Notes

  1. "The Book of Changes says: 'Fathoming principle, fulfilling inner nature, and thereby arriving at destiny'": From the Discussion of the Trigrams (Shuogua), used by both Confucians and Daoists to summarize the integrated path of inner nature and life.
  2. "The Book of Documents says: 'Without bias, without faction, broad and level is the Royal Way'": From the "Great Plan" (Hongfan), evoking the impartial, centered grandeur of the Great Way.
  3. "Done without anyone forcing it, arriving without anyone contriving it": Adapts Mencius (5A.6: "What is done without anyone doing it is Heaven; what arrives without anyone bringing it is Destiny"). The following line, "moving when one cannot but move, stopping when one cannot but stop," echoes Su Shi's famous self-assessment of natural writing.
  4. "When the will is unified it moves the breath, and when the breath is unified it moves the will": From Mencius (2A.2), invoked by Huang Yuanji to underline the practical alchemical path of steadying breath to pacify spirit.
  5. Textual collation: Base-edition misprints for "standing on tiptoe" (跂者不立), "stubborn emptiness" (顽空), "boasters" (自伐者), "naturally respect" (人自敬服), "recoil against oneself" (有恶于己), and "if one does not seek to nurture breath" (苟不求养气), along with multiple confusions of yue (曰) and ri (日), have been corrected in the Chinese source.
