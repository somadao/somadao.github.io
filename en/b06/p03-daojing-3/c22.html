III · Classic of the Way (Ch. 21–30) · 道经

Chapter 22 · Preserved Whole and Returned

Yielding to remain whole is not slick compromise, but guarding the subtle first stirring of true vitality—only through humility, embracing the One, and refraining from display or contention can we return our heaven-given nature and life intact to their source

About This Chapter

When people read "yield and remain whole; bend and become straight; stay hollow and be filled; wear old and be renewed," they often treat it as worldly advice on tactical concession or keeping a low profile. Huang Yuanji draws the entire chapter back to the concrete reality of dual cultivation of inner nature and life, uniting Laozi's "yield and remain whole" (qu ze quan) with the Confucian Doctrine of the Mean's "the next best reaches the subtle bends (qu), and subtlety can achieve sincerity":

  1. "Qu" is not crooked compromise, but the "subtle hidden pivot": In the utter stillness of having nothing at all, a faint spark of vitality suddenly stirs—exceedingly delicate and brief. Daoism calls it the Mysterious Opening, and Confucianism calls it the "first sprout" (duanni). Tiny as the single thread of yang at the winter solstice or the crescent moon on the third night of the month, it already holds the wholeness of the entire Supreme Ultimate; guard this one point, and from the subtle grows the manifest, from the bend grows the whole.
  2. "Have little and gain; have much and be confused" is cultivation's sharpest warning: Many students of the Way grow greedy for quantity—now practicing guarding the center, now harvesting the medicine, now fussing over advancing fire and withdrawing tallies. The more techniques they collect, the more their minds turn into a tangled knot, leaving them homeless for life. What truly works is simply guarding the single "old treasure" already innate within one's own body—warming the old to know the new, and guarding the center and embracing the One.
  3. Once achievement comes, the greatest danger is self-display, self-righteousness, boasting, and pride: Even if one cultivates a measure of wisdom and skill, it is merely something every human being already possesses—you have only realized it a step sooner, so what is there to flaunt? The instant pride stirs, heart-fire flares up, clouding clear awareness and provoking strife. Only with a level heart, peaceful breath, and humble self-effacement can one receive one's heaven-bestowed nature and life whole and return them whole.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：曲则全，枉则直，洼则盈，弊则新，少则得，多则惑。是以圣人抱一，为天下式。不自见故明，不自是故彰，不自伐故有功，不自矜故长。夫惟不争，故天下莫与之争。古之所谓「曲则全」者，岂虚言哉？诚全而归之。（洼，音羽。言其卑也。）

Laozi says: What stays tucked in subtlety remains whole; what bends under slight becomes straight; what lies low and hollow is filled to the brim; what holds to the old root is made new; what seeks little and stays single-minded truly gains; what grasps at much grows confused.

Therefore the sage embraces the One and serves as the standard for all under heaven. He does not put himself on display, and so he sees clearly; he does not insist he is right, and so his truth shines forth; he does not boast of his merits, and so his achievements endure; he does not pride himself on his stature, and so he remains lasting and preeminent. Precisely because he never contends, no one in the world can contend with him.

The ancient saying "yield and remain whole"—could it be empty words? In truth, only thus can one preserve one's heaven-given nature intact and return it whole to its source!

(Original note: Wa is pronounced like yu, meaning low and humble.)

§2
Original (Chinese)

大道之要，必至无而含至有，却至有而实至无，始为性命双修之道。盖以性本无也，无生于有；命本有也，有生于无。若著于虚无，便成顽空，著于实有，又拘名象，纵不流于妄诞不经，亦是一边之学，究难与大道等。修行人，必先万缘放下，纤尘不染，于一无所有之中，寻出一点生机出来，以为丹本，古人谓之真阳，又曰真铅，又曰真一之气是也。太上云：「曲则全」，言人身隐微之间，独知独觉之地，有一个浑沦完全、活泼流通之机，由此存之养之，采取烹炼，即可至于丹成仙就。昔人喻冬至，一线微阳，至于生生不已；又喻初三一弯新月，渐至十五月圆，无非由曲而全之意也。

The supreme essential of the Great Way is always this: utter non-being harbors utter being, while utter being is in truth utter non-being. Only this constitutes the authentic Way of dual cultivation of inner nature and life. Because our inner nature is originally formless ("non-being"), yet that formless nature must be revealed through embodied life ("non-being arises from being"); and our bodily life is originally tangible ("being"), yet this tangible life springs forth from formless emptiness ("being arises from non-being"). If one clings stubbornly to emptiness, it turns into dead "stubborn emptiness"; if one clings stubbornly to tangible existence, one becomes trapped in names and appearances. Even if such a path does not degenerate into absurd fantasies, it remains a one-sided teaching that can never equal the Great Way.

A practitioner must therefore first lay down all worldly entanglements until not a speck of dust stains the mind, and right within that state of "having nothing at all," discover a single spark of spontaneously stirring vitality to serve as the root of the Elixir. The ancients called it True Yang, or True Lead (see Lead and Mercury), or the One Breath of Prior Heaven.

When Laozi says "yield and remain whole" (qu ze quan), he means that within the most hidden, subtle recess of the human body—known and felt in solitude alone—there lies an undivided, complete, lively, and freely flowing pivot of vitality. Beginning here, preserving it, nurturing it, harvesting and refining it, one can step by step reach the completion of the Elixir and the Way. The ancients likened it to the single faint thread of yang at the winter solstice that grows ceaselessly into full summer, or to the slender crescent moon on the third night of the lunar month that gradually rounds out into the full moon of the fifteenth—all expressing the single truth of moving from the subtle and tucked-away to complete wholeness.

§3
Original (Chinese)

夫曲，隐也。隐微之处，其机甚微，其成则大，即《中庸》云「曲能有诚」是。要之，一曲之内，莫非理气之元；全体之间，亦是太极之粹，即曲即全，故曰「曲则全」。圣人寻得此曲，兢兢致慎，回环抱伏，如鸡温卵，如龙养珠，一心内守，不许外露，久则浩浩如潮，逆而上伸，一股清刚之气，挺然直上，出乎日月之表，包乎天地之外。坤卦谓「坤至柔而动也刚」，皆由致曲之余，潜伏土釜，积而至于滔天，勃不可遏，有如是耳。且夫枉而为阴、为柔是此气，直而为阳、为刚亦此气，虽曰由枉而直，其实即枉即直。

"Qu" means hidden and subtle. In that hidden, subtle place, the first stirring is tiny indeed, yet its eventual fruition is vast—precisely what the Doctrine of the Mean means by "reaching the subtle bends can achieve sincerity" (carrying the smallest sprout of goodness to its fullness brings utmost authenticity). At bottom, within that single subtle point lies the very origin of cosmic Principle and Breath; the entire cosmos in its wholeness is nothing other than the pure unfolding of this Supreme Ultimate. The subtle bend already contains the whole—hence "yield and remain whole."

Once the sage discovers this single subtle spark of vitality, he guards it with reverent caution, cradling and brooding over it again and again—like a hen warming her eggs or a dragon cherishing its pearl—keeping his whole heart turned inward and never permitting it to leak or display itself outside. After long accumulation, this vitality swells like a vast tide and rises in reversal, becoming a pure, stalwart current of upright breath that shoots straight aloft, transcending sun and moon and encompassing heaven and earth. The Book of Changes says of the Kun hexagram, "Kun is supremely yielding, yet when it moves it is firm and strong"—precisely because, after tending the subtle spark and hiding it deep in the earthen cauldron of the Cinnabar Field, it accumulates until it surges sky-high with unstoppable vigor.

In truth, the breath that rests bent, yin, and yielding is this very breath, and the breath that later extends straight, yang, and stalwart is the exact same breath. Though we speak of moving "from bent to straight," at its core the bent is already the straight.

§4
Original (Chinese)

自隐曲中，洞彻本源之后，其见则易，为守则难，惟优焉游焉，直养其端倪，更卑以下人，谦以自待，庶无躁暴急迫之性，不生邪见，不动凡火，方能养成金丹。由是以神驭气，以气合神，隐显无端，变化莫测，所谓「至诚无息」、「体物不遗」，无在而无不在也，何其盈乎！然要必谦乃受益，洼乃为盈也，不然乌能包涵万有哉？况乎一曲之微，皆吾人本来之物，所谓弊也，弊即故也，《论语》「温故而知新」是。学人欲得新闻生新意，非从此故有之物以温之，何能得新？是亦即弊即新也。虽然，弊亦无几耳，惟从其少而养之浩然之气，大可以塞天地、贯斗牛。若谓道浩瀚弥纶，无在不是，取其多而用之，吾恐理欲杂乘，善恶莫辨，时而守中，时而采药，时而进火退符，著缘执名，多多益善，究属无本之学，未得止归，终是一个迷团，无怪乎毕生怀疑莫悟也。

Once one penetrates through this hidden subtlety to behold the source clearly, "seeing" it is actually easy; the hard part is "guarding" it over the long haul. Only by moving at an unhurried, spacious pace—quietly and directly nurturing this first sprout, while becoming ever more humble toward others and modest in one's own bearing—can one remain free of impatience and rashness, avoid crooked views, and keep Posterior Heaven ordinary fire from flaring up, so that the Golden Elixir matures steadily. From this, one comes to guide Breath with Spirit and merge Breath with Spirit, appearing and vanishing without fixed trace, unfathomable in transformation—just as the Doctrine of the Mean says, "utmost sincerity never ceases" and "it embodies all things without leaving any out," dwelling nowhere yet present everywhere. What overflowing fullness! Yet the prerequisite is always that humility receives benefit and the low hollow fills to the brim; otherwise, how could one possibly contain the myriad things?

Furthermore, this subtle spark of vitality is nothing other than our own innate, original possession—what Laozi calls "old" (bi). Bi here means "old" or "originally held," just as the Analects says, "warming the old to understand the new." If a practitioner wishes to gain fresh insight and awaken new life, without continually warming this single "old treasure" already within oneself, how could anything new arise? Thus the old itself contains the new: the old is the new.

Yet this innate root is exceedingly small—just that single point. Concentrate on nurturing that tiny bit, and the flood-like upright breath you cultivate grows vast enough to fill heaven and earth and pierce the constellations. If instead you tell yourself that the Way is boundless and everywhere, and greedily try to seize and apply a multitude of methods, I fear Heavenly Principle and human desire will tangle together until good and bad can no longer be told apart: one moment practicing guarding the center, the next trying to harvest the Medicine, the next fussing over advancing fire and withdrawing tallies (see fire phasing), clinging to external techniques and labels under the delusion that "more is better." In the end, all of that is rootless learning without a resting place—one big knot of confusion. No wonder so many spend a lifetime in doubt and never awaken!

§5
Original (Chinese)

圣人抱一以自修，又将施之天下，为天下楷模，使不知一曲之道，实为一贯之道，而偶有所离，偶离则无式，无式则无成，道何赖乎？夫道本天人一理，物我同原，为公共之物，何今之学者每固执己见，谓人莫己若？即此矜骄之念，已觉障蔽灵明，而不知酌古准今，取法乎上。《中庸》云：「君子之道，闇然日章；小人之道，的然日亡。」诚修士所宜凛凛矣。纵使几于化神，亦属分所宜然，职所当尽，何必炫耀于世，夸大其功？若便自伐，不但为人所厌，即功亦伪而不真。古人功成告退，并不居功之名，宜其功盖天下，为万世师也。至于自修自炼，犹衣之得暖，食之得饱，皆自得之而自乐之，且为人所各有而各足之，何必骄傲满假，自矜其长？虽云智慧日生，聪明日扩，亦是人性所同然，不过我先得之耳，何长之有？若使自矜其长，则长者短矣！人虽至愚，谁甘居后？争端有不从此起耶？君子无所争，故天下莫能与争。古所谓「曲则全」者，诚非虚言也，谓非「全受而全归之」者欤？

The sage cultivates himself by guarding the center and embracing the One, and extends that same standard to the world as a model for all. If one fails to see that this subtle single "bend" (qu) is in fact the single unifying thread running through all things, and drifts from it even momentarily, the moment one drifts one loses the standard; without a standard, nothing comes to completion—and on what could the Way then rest?

The Way is by nature one single principle uniting heaven and humanity, a single source shared by self and all things—a public treasure belonging to all. Why, then, do today's students so often cling stubbornly to private opinions, imagining that no one matches them? That single thought of conceit already clouds their luminous awareness, leaving them blind to weighing ancient wisdom against the present and learning from those higher than themselves. As the Doctrine of the Mean warns: "The way of the noble person appears dim and understated, yet grows brighter day by day; the way of the petty person flashes brightly on display, yet fades away day by day." Every cultivator should take this warning to heart with solemn awe!

Even if your cultivation approaches wondrous spiritual transformation, that is still no more than fulfilling your proper human portion and duty—why parade it before the world and exaggerate your achievements? The moment you boast of merit, not only do others find it distasteful, the merit itself turns hollow and false. The ancients withdrew quietly once their work was done, never claiming credit; no wonder their deeds covered the world and made them teachers for ten thousand generations. As for self-cultivation and inner refinement, it is just like putting on clothes to keep warm or eating food to be full—something you enjoy quietly for yourself, and moreover something every person innately possesses in full. What reason could there be for arrogance or bragging about your superiority? Even if wisdom grows day by day and discernment widens, those are capacities shared by all human nature; you have merely realized them a step earlier—what superiority is there in that? The instant you boast of your strength, that strength shrinks into a flaw! Even the dullest person hates being pushed to the back: do not quarrels and rivalry spring directly from this? The noble person has no urge to compete, so no one under heaven can outdo him. The ancient saying "yield and remain whole" is truly no empty phrase—is it not precisely receiving our heaven-given nature and life whole, and returning them whole?

§6
Original (Chinese)

此即《中庸》「其次致曲，曲能有诚」之道。曲即隐曲，道曰「玄窍」，佛曰「那个」，儒曰「端倪」是。又非虚而无物也。天地开辟，人物始生，尽从此一点发端，随时皆有动静可见。其静而发端也，不由感触，忽然而觉，觉即曲也；其动而显象也，偶然感孚，突焉而动，动即曲也。要皆从无知无觉时，气机自动，动而忽觉，此乃真动真觉。但其机甚微，为时最速，稍转一念，易一息，即属后天，不可为人物生生之本，亦不可为炼丹之根。吾人受气成形，为人为物，都从此一念分胎。修道之邪正真伪，孰不自此一息发源耶？《周书》曰：「罔念作狂，克念作圣。」圣狂一念之分，如此其速，即此一曲之谓也。古人喻如电光石火，又如乘千里骥绝尘而奔，此时须有智珠朗照，方能认得清楚。既识得此个端倪，犹要存养之、扩充之，如孟子所谓「火始然，泉始达」，浩浩炎炎，自然充塞天地。然扩充之道，又岂有他哉？非枉屈自持，则不能正气常伸；非卑洼自下，则不能天德常圆。惟守吾身故物，不参以贰，不杂以三，温其故，抱其一，不求之于新颖之端，不驰之于名象之繁，斯乃不至愈学愈迷，而有「日新又新」之乐矣。古圣人知一曲为成仙证圣之阶，遂将神抱气，气依神，神气合一而不离，以为自修之要，以为天下之式。倘自见自是，即昧其明而不彰，况自伐则劳而无功，自矜则短而不长，智起情生，往往为道之害。惟不自见自是、自伐自矜，斯心平气和，自然「在彼无恶，在此无斁」，又谁与争哉？道之潜移默契如此，非抱一者，乌能全受全归，以返其太始之初乎？

This chapter expounds precisely the teaching of the Doctrine of the Mean: "The next best reaches the subtle bends (qu), and subtlety can achieve sincerity." "Qu" is the hidden, subtle recess—what Daoism calls the Mysterious Opening, Buddhism calls "That One," and Confucianism calls the "first sprout" (duanni). Nor is it an empty void devoid of reality. The opening of heaven and earth and the birth of humanity and all things begin from this single point, whose stillness and movement can be glimpsed at any time: when it originates in stillness, without any external stimulus, a sudden lucid awareness dawns—that single awareness is qu; when it manifests in movement, through a spontaneous inner resonance, the breath-pivot suddenly stirs—that single stirring is also qu. In both cases, out of a state without conceptual knowing, the vital pivot moves of itself, and upon moving one suddenly knows it: this is true movement and true awareness.

Yet this pivot is unimaginably fine and passes in a flash: shift a single thought or change a single breath, and it has already fallen into Posterior Heaven, no longer fit to serve as the creative root of living beings or the seed of the Elixir. How we receive breath to take form, and the whole course of our lives, diverges at the fork of this single thought; whether cultivation is upright or crooked, genuine or counterfeit, everything originates in this single breath! As the Book of Documents ("Books of Zhou") says: "Without mindfulness a sage becomes a madman; with mindful restraint a madman becomes a sage." That razor-thin instant separating sage from madman is precisely this single qu. The ancients likened it to a flash of lightning or spark off flint, or to riding a thousand-mile steed vanishing past the dust: at that instant one needs a bright pearl of wisdom shining clearly within to recognize it unmistakably.

Once you recognize this first sprout, you must still preserve, nurture, and expand it—like Mencius's image of "a fire just kindled, a spring just breaking through"—so that, left unextinguished and unblocked, it blazes bright and flows wide until it naturally fills heaven and earth. And what secret method expands it? None other than this: unless you restrain and humble yourself in yielding, upright breath cannot stretch out steadily; unless you place yourself low like a hollow basin, heavenly virtue cannot stay full and round. Simply guard the "old treasure" already within your own body, admitting no second mind and mixing in no third thought; warm the old and embrace the One, neither chasing novel gimmicks nor getting lost in tangled terminology. Only thus will you avoid growing more confused the more you study, and instead taste the joy of "renewing daily, and again renewing."

The ancient sages knew that this single subtle point is the stairway to sagehood and immortality, so they had Spirit embrace Breath and Breath lean upon Spirit, uniting Spirit and Breath without a moment's separation—making this both the essence of self-cultivation and the standard for the world. If you put yourself on display or insist you are right, you dim your own brightness and fail to shine; worse still, boasting makes your labor fruitless, and pride turns your strengths into weakness. Flaunting cleverness and feeding vanity are always the ruin of the Way. Only when free of self-display, self-righteousness, boasting, and pride—with heart level and breath peaceful—does one naturally fulfill the Classic of Poetry's words: "Unresented there, unwearied of here." Who then would ever contend with you? So quietly and imperceptibly does the Way bring silent accord: unless one truly embraces the One, how could one receive life whole and return it whole, back to the primordial beginning?

Key Terms

  • Yield and remain whole (qu ze quan) / reaching the subtle bends (zhiqu): Huang Yuanji unites Laozi's "yield and remain whole" with the Doctrine of the Mean's "reaching the subtle bends can achieve sincerity," meaning to recognize and warmly nurture the subtle first stirring of true yang in utter stillness so that it grows from faint sprout to full wholeness.
  • Mysterious Opening (xuanqiao): also called "That One" (Chan Buddhism) and the "first sprout" (duanni, Confucianism); the hidden locus where Prior Heaven vitality suddenly stirs and awakens out of non-conceptual stillness.
  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: also referred to in this chapter as True Yang, True Lead, and True-One Breath—the root of alchemical refinement that emerges from emptiness once all worldly entanglements are laid aside.
  • guarding the center and embracing the One: Spirit embracing Breath and Breath leaning on Spirit, faithfully holding to the single source of vitality without greed for multiple techniques or mental clutter.
  • Earthen cauldron (tufu): an alchemical metaphor for the lower Cinnabar Field (the central Earth furnace), where nascent subtle yang is concealed and warmly nurtured.
  • Receiving whole and returning whole (quan shou er quan gui): originally from the Book of Rites ("Meaning of Sacrifices"—"one's parents gave birth to one whole, and a child returns oneself whole"), adapted by Huang Yuanji to mean cultivating one's heaven-given Prior Heaven nature and life to full perfection and returning them intact to the primordial origin.

Putting It into Practice

  1. The chief pitfall in cultivation is biting off too many methods at once. The more books you pile up and the more techniques you juggle—"now guarding the center, now harvesting medicine"—the more tangled your mind becomes. Choose one plain, grounded practice (such as resting with the breath and staying present) and keep "warming the old" rather than chasing new gimmicks every week.
  2. "Understanding" takes a second; "guarding" is where real skill lives. A flash of insight comes easily, but keeping clarity steady amid daily moods, pressures, and temptations requires unhurried, long-term patience and humble restraint.
  3. Treat "not showing off" as a way of protecting your energy. Rushing to lecture others or boast as soon as you feel a small breakthrough stirs up agitated "ordinary fire." Treat inner gains as matter-of-factly as eating when hungry or wearing a coat for warmth, and your foundation will grow far more solid.
  4. Notice the split second a thought first rises. Whether irritation, pride, or quiet clarity, every impulse begins like a "spark off flint." Paying gentle attention to the first moment an emotion surfaces puts your hand directly on the switch that separates composure from folly.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Wa, pronounced like yu": Received editions of the Daodejing read wa ze ying (洼则盈, with variants 窪/漥). Modern Mandarin reads wa as wā, while older phonology also had a yū/yǔ reading denoting low-lying hollows; hence the original note glosses its sound as yu and its meaning as "low and humble."
  2. "Kun is supremely yielding, yet when it moves it is firm and strong": Quoted from the Wenyan commentary on Hexagram Kun in the Book of Changes, describing how Kun's outward gentleness conceals immense strength when deeply accumulated.
  3. "Utmost sincerity never ceases," "embodies all things without leaving any out," and "appears dim yet grows brighter day by day": All drawn from the Doctrine of the Mean. In the base edition, the character di (的, "brightly on display") was dropped from the second half of the couplet and has been restored from the classic.
  4. "Without mindfulness a sage becomes a madman; with mindful restraint a madman becomes a sage": From the Book of Documents ("Many Regions"), stressing how the boundary between folly and wisdom turns on mindful self-restraint.
  5. "A fire just kindled, a spring just breaking through": From Mencius (2A.6), Mencius's famous metaphor for expanding the four innate sprouts of human goodness.
  6. "Unresented there, unwearied of here": From the Classic of Poetry ("Odes of Zhou," Zhenlu, also cited in the Doctrine of the Mean), where yi (斁) means "weariness" or "dislike."
  7. Textual collation: Numerous misprints in the base edition—including errors for "stubborn emptiness" (顽空), "absurd" (妄诞), "called" (谓之), "now qu" (夫曲), "exposed outside" (外露), "yielding" (为柔), "more humble" (更卑), "moreover" (况乎), "the Way" (夫道), "cling to one's own views" (固执己见), "self-refinement" (自炼), "arrogance" (骄傲), "crooked and upright" (邪正), "other?" (他哉), and "dims its brightness" (昧其明), plus a stray interpolated character (耍)—have been corrected in the Chinese source.
