Chapter 30 · One Skilled in Arms Stops at Resolution

Applying fire in internal alchemy is like waging war on a battlefield—exceedingly perilous; a depleted body resorts to forceful methods only out of necessity, stops the moment results arrive, and avoids pride or flexing strength, for only humble suppleness escapes "what grows rigid soon ages"

About This Chapter

On the surface, Chapter 30 is a stirring anti-war classic: "Where armies camp, thorns and brambles grow; in the wake of a great host, lean years surely follow," and "achieve resolution without boasting, without claiming credit, without pride." Huang Yuanji first depicts with searing poignancy the devastation of warfare and the sage-king's gift for winning hearts through virtue, then pivots sharply to one of the most sobering internal-alchemy analogies in the entire book:

  1. In worldly affairs: weapons are ominous and Heaven's Way rebounds. War not only strews corpses across abandoned fields, it injures the cosmic harmony of heaven and earth. Even when forced to dispatch troops to quell chaos, one must "resolve the crisis and stop immediately," never boasting of victory. Highest of all is the sage-emperor Shun's example—"cultivating virtue rather than arms," winning surrender without striking a blow.
  2. In internal alchemy: applying purposeful fire is like leading troops into battle—a truly ominous affair. An unspoiled, intact youthful body may walk a gentle, smooth path; only depleted, aging bodies resort out of necessity to forceful purposeful methods to revive waning vitality. Yet if attention scatters during fire-refinement, it becomes "where armies camp, thorns and brambles grow"; and when strong yang fire drives out deep-seated old ailments—manifesting as heavy sweating, cloudy urine, or even erupting sores—it resembles "in the wake of a great host, lean years surely follow," where panic can trigger damaging Posterior Heaven ordinary fire.
  3. The hinge of success or ruin: never flex strength or boast, lest "what grows rigid soon ages." Even if cultivation brings robust health and resistance to cold and heat, the tiniest stirring of conceit, showing off, or pride instantly ignites ordinary fire and overturns the cauldron. Plants stay alive while tender and supple; once stiff and hard, vitality has already fled. Shedding the rigid, combative mind—living plainly, steadily, following principle and trusting Heaven—is the true path of longevity.

Safety note: This chapter references forceful traditional alchemical methods such as "knocking the bamboo to summon the tortoise and strumming the zither to call the phoenix," and Huang Yuanji himself issues two grave warnings at the start and close of his commentary that "this is truly a perilous affair" and "great danger lurks here—students must take utmost care." Moreover, the text mentions intense reactions such as heavy sweating, cloudy urine, or "external sores and eruptions." Modern readers must take careful note: if skin rashes, abnormal discharge, persistent pain, or any clear physical symptoms appear during practice, seek proper medical diagnosis and treatment promptly—never dismiss real medical conditions as "expelling yin energy" or delay care!

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：以道佐人主者，不以兵强天下，其事好还：师之所处，荆棘生焉，大军之后，必有凶年。故善者果而已矣，不敢以取强。果而勿矜，果而勿伐，果而勿骄，果而不得已，果而勿强。物壮则老，是谓不道，不道早已。

Laozi says: One who assists a ruler through the Way never uses armed force to dominate the world, for such deeds rebound upon their doer: wherever armies camp, brambles and thorns spring up; in the wake of a great military campaign, years of famine inevitably follow.

Therefore one skilled in arms seeks only decisive resolution to rescue the people and stops at once, never daring to seize power by force. Resolve the crisis, yet do not boast; resolve it, yet do not claim credit; resolve it, yet do not grow proud; resolve it solely out of necessity; resolve it without flexing strength. Whatever forces itself to peak rigidity soon grows old—this is called departing from the Way, and what departs from the Way soon comes to an end.

§2
Original (Chinese)

上古之世，各君其国，各子其民，熙熙皞皞，共安无事之天，人己浑忘，畛域胥化，又焉有战争之事哉？迨共工作乱而征伐起，蚩尤犯上而兵革兴，于是文则有玉帛，武则有兵戎，治则用礼乐，乱则用干戈，朝廷所以文武并重也。然有道之君子，达而在上，辅佐熙朝，赞襄郅治，惟以道事人主，不以兵强天下。此是何故？盖杀人之父兄，人亦杀其父兄，人心思返，天道好还，冤仇报复，靡有休息。又况兵过之乡，人民罹害；师行之处，鸡犬亦空，以故杀戮重而死亡多，尸填巨港，血满长城，无贵无贱，同为枯骨，生之数不啻杀之数，死之人多于生之人，由是井里萧条，田野荒废，而荆棘生焉。且肃杀之气，大伤太和，乖戾之风，上干天怒，因而阴阳不燮，雨旸不时，旱干水溢，频来凶荒，饥馑荐臻，民不聊生，朝不及夕。古云：「大军之后，必有凶年」，势所必至也。然而饥寒交迫，盗心日生，年岁凶荒，乱民迭作，亦有不得不为兵戎之请者。古云：「兵贵神速，不贵迟疑。」故善用兵者，亦果而已矣。行仁义之师，望若时雨；解倒悬之苦，迎以壶浆。如武王壹戎衣而天下定，无非我武维扬，歼厥渠魁已耳，何敢逞杀戮于片时，取强威于一己？其果而胜也，切勿自矜，矜则有好兵之念；切勿自伐，伐则有黩武之心。就令除残暴于反掌，登人民于春台，亦安邦定国之常、救世扶危之道，为将帅者分所应尔，何足骄于人哉？夫骄人者，好杀人者也。纵使果敢弭乱，出斯民于水火，然有此三心，虽无杀之事，而杀之机已伏于中，非道也。须知，行兵之事，圣人不得已而用之，即未损一兵，未折一将，不伤一民，不戕一物，亦未足语承平之雅化，何况非圣王所期许者，果而勿强焉可也。诗云：「劝君莫觅封侯事，一将功成万骨枯。」以此思之，兵危事，战凶机，非天下生生之道也。况乎主宾相敌，旷日持久，师老财殚，臣离民怨，可已而不已，其何以为国乎？更有坚壁相持，连年转饷，一旦偶疏，而敌或扼其险要，绝其粮饷，士闻风而预走，军望气以先逃，昔日雄师，今成灰烬，亦何怪其然耶？夫亦曰「物壮则老」，其势有必然者。且夫用兵之事，以有道诛无道者也。如此喜兵好战，欲安民，反致害民；欲弭乱，反将生乱，不道极矣。夫诛无道而自行不道，何如屯田防寇，休兵睦邻，早已之之为愈也。否则，如舜伐三苗，苗民负固，舜不修戎而修德，舞干羽于两阶，七旬而有苗格。此不威之威、不武之武，甚于威武者多矣。为上者，知之否？

In high antiquity, rulers governed their own realms and cherished their people like children; everyone lived in radiant harmony under a tranquil sky, forgetting boundaries between self and other—how could war ever arise? Only when Gonggong stirred rebellion did punitive campaigns begin, and when Chiyou defied order did armor and weapons appear. From then on, diplomacy used jade and silk while defense used arms; peace was ordered by rites and music while turmoil was quelled by shields and spears—that is why courts honored both civil and martial arts.

Yet when a noble person of the Way rises to high office to assist a bright reign and foster deep peace, he serves his sovereign by the Way alone, never seeking to dominate the world by force of arms. Why? Because kill another's father or elder brother, and they will kill yours in return: human hearts seek vengeance and Heaven's Way rebounds without fail, spinning endless cycles of bloodshed. Worse still, wherever troops pass, villagers suffer ruin; wherever armies march, not even chickens or dogs survive. Slaughter mounts and death piles high: corpses choke deep channels, blood fills the border walls, noble and lowly alike turn to bleached bones; the slain rival or outnumber the living, villages turn desolate, fields go untended, and thorns and brambles overrun the earth.

Furthermore, that grim aura of slaughter gravely wounds cosmic harmony, and bitter resentment rises to disturb Heaven, throwing yin and yang out of balance: rain and sunshine miss their seasons, droughts and floods strike in turn, famine follows famine, and the common people cannot look from morning to evening. As the old saying goes, "In the wake of a great host, lean years surely follow"—it is an inevitable consequence!

Admittedly, when hunger and cold close in, banditry breeds and uprisings flare in famine years, leaving moments when taking up arms truly cannot be avoided. As the proverb says, "In war, swift resolve is prized, hesitation is fatal." Therefore one skilled in arms resolves the crisis cleanly and stops ("resolution and no more"). Marching with an army of humaneness and justice, longed for like timely rain, lifting people out of desperate misery so they rush out with food and drink to welcome them—just as King Wu donned armor once and settled the realm, displaying upright resolve to remove only the ringleaders—how could he dare indulge in wanton slaughter or parade personal might?

When victory is decisively won, never boast ("do not boast"), for boasting breeds fondness for arms; never claim credit ("do not claim credit"), for claiming credit fuels reckless militarism. Even if you topple tyranny with the turn of a hand and lift the people into springtime ease, that is simply the ordinary duty of stabilizing the realm and rescuing the endangered—a commander's plain responsibility, nothing to lord over others ("do not grow proud")! Anyone proud before others harbors a taste for killing. Even after bravely ending chaos and pulling people from fire and flood, if those three attitudes—boasting, claiming credit, pride—lurk inside, though no killing happens outwardly, the seed of slaughter is already planted within; that is never the Way.

Remember: warfare is a last resort the sage employs only out of sheer necessity. Even if not a single soldier is lost, not a single general falls, not one civilian is harmed, and not one creature injured, it still falls far short of the gracious culture of true peace—still less is it what a sage-king desires. Therefore resolve the crisis and never flex strength! As the Tang poem laments so piercingly: "I urge you, do not chase a marquis's title—one general's glory dries ten thousand bones." Ponder that line: arms are perilous tools, war is an ominous engine, never the life-giving Way of the world.

Worse still, when opposing sides lock into protracted conflict, armies grow exhausted, treasuries empty, ministers grow estranged, and citizens resentful—refusing to stop when stopping is possible, how can a state survive? Or armies dig in behind high ramparts, hauling grain for years on end, until one momentary slip lets the enemy seize the mountain pass and sever supply lines: soldiers bolt at a rumor, ranks crumble at the sight of the foe, and yesterday's mighty host turns to ashes—what is surprising in that? Simply "what grows rigid soon ages," an inescapable law of momentum!

Originally, taking up arms meant using the Way to check the wayless. If instead one delights in war—aiming to pacify the people yet harming them, aiming to quell chaos yet breeding worse chaos—that departs utterly from the Way! Rather than preaching justice while acting unjustly, how much wiser to farm border garrisons for defense, rest the troops, keep peace with neighbors, and lay down arms early! Better yet, follow the example of Emperor Shun facing the Three Miao tribes: when the Miao stood defiant behind strongholds, Shun polished virtue instead of weapons, having dancers perform peaceful shield-and-feather dances between the two staircases of his court—and within seventy days the Miao came willingly to submit. Such authority without menace, such martial power without bloodshed, surpasses armed might a thousandfold! Do those who stand in high places understand this?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言用火、行符、采取、烹炼之道，是有为有作，比之用兵克敌，大是一场凶事，不可大意作去。如曾子之战兢自惕，子思之戒慎时严，方可变化气质之躯，复还先天面目。若童真之体，未经凿破，未曾损坏者，固可相时而动，遵道而行，无偏无党，无险无危，直臻神化之域。如破漏之人，与年老之体，后天铅汞将尽，性命何依？不得不用敲竹唤龟、鼓琴招凤二法，而后有玉芝灵苗、刀圭上药，可采可炼，化凡躯于乌有，结圣胎于灵关。第火候至密，非得真师口授，万不能洞彻精微，即得秘密天机，然内德外功，一有不满，犹为神天所不佑。惟虚心访道，积德累仁，事事无愧，在在怀仁，以谦以柔，以忍以下，神依于气，气恋夫神，绵绵不绝，造到固蒂深根，决不时而忘之，纷纷驰逐，时而忆之，切切不已，故曰：「以道佐人主者，不以兵强天下。」即使尽善，而火煅之后，凡气已除，真气未曾积累，势必似无似有，微而难测。且有不炼而气散，愈炼而气愈散者，皆由心有出入，似蔓草之难除。故曰：「师之所处，荆棘生焉。」况夫神火一煅，而多年之残疾、自幼之沉疴，阳火一逼，阴气难留，其轻者，或从汗液浊溺而出，其重者，或外生疮毒而化，种种不一，修士不可惊为病也。只要心安，即能化气。可见炼己之道，必化凡体为玉体，变浊躯为金躯，切不可惊，惊则又动后天凡火，而大伤元气也。故曰：「大军之后，必有凶年。」

Turning this chapter inward to internal alchemy, it speaks of applying fire, timing tallies, harvesting, and cooking—forms of "purposeful action" that, just like deploying troops against an enemy, are truly a perilous business never to be undertaken carelessly! Only with Master Zeng's trembling vigilance and Zisi's unceasing watchfulness in solitude can one transform this conditioned physical frame and restore one's Prior Heaven original face.

An unspoiled youthful body, unbroken and unspent, may naturally move in harmony with the seasons and follow the Way without bias or hazard, arriving smoothly at spiritual transformation. Yet for depleted adults or aging bodies whose Posterior Heaven Lead and Mercury are nearly exhausted, what can inner nature and life rely on? Only out of necessity do they resort to traditional purposeful methods called "knocking the bamboo to summon the tortoise" and "strumming the zither to call the phoenix," awakening the Jade Fungus sprout and supreme Medicine for harvesting and refinement—transmuting the coarse mortal frame and forming the Holy Embryo within the Mysterious Pass.

Yet the fire phasing here is unimaginably delicate: without oral instruction directly from an authentic master, one can never fathom its fine nuances; and even if one learns the hidden key, should inner virtue or outer merit fall short by even a fraction, Heaven and the spirits still will not lend protection. Only by humbly seeking true guidance, accumulating virtue and kindness, acting blamelessly in every affair, keeping compassion in every setting, and holding oneself with humility, gentleness, patience, and lowliness—letting Spirit lean upon Breath and Breath cherish Spirit in an unbroken thread until roots grow deep and stems grow firm, never forgetting it completely to chase distractions and then frantically pushing whenever one remembers—does one fulfill "assisting the ruler by the Way, never dominating by force of arms."

Even when every precaution is taken, right after intense inner calcination—when coarse ordinary breath has been cleared away while true breath has not yet accumulated thickly—one enters a faint, elusive interval that feels barely there. Worse, many find their energy scatters when they do not practice and scatters even faster the harder they force practice—all because the mind darts in and out, stray thoughts tangling like stubborn weeds: hence "where armies camp, thorns and brambles grow"!

Moreover, once inner spirit-fire begins its calcination, long-buried chronic weaknesses and old ailments stored since youth are driven outward by yang fire so cold yin stagnation can no longer hide: in milder cases, traditional accounts describe heavy sweating or cloudy urine; in heavier cases, even external sores or eruptions breaking out to clear stagnation. According to traditional alchemical experience, the danger here is panicking—if the mind stays calm, vitality transforms smoothly; if one panics, agitated Posterior Heaven ordinary fire flares up at once and gravely injures Original Breath. Hence Laozi's warning: "In the wake of a great host, lean years surely follow."

§4
Original (Chinese)

善用兵者，贵果敢；善用火者，贵神速。故曰：「果而已矣。」在修士，当此体化纯乾之时，切不可恃，恃其才以为不饥不渴，可以行步如飞，冬不炉，夏不扇，无端妙用，迥异常人，而自以为强也。自谓为强，又动后天凡火，不遭外人诽谤，必至内药倾危。况生一自强之心，即令十月怀胎，三年乳哺，件件功成告毕，不差时刻，而自矜自伐，骄傲凌人，殊非载道之器。纵果于成功，亦必果于偾事。倾倒之患，安可胜言哉？又况自恃其强，不知谦下存心始可修德凝道，是犹草木之柔脆者有生机，坚强者无生气，日复一日，年复一年，光阴逾迈，岁月云遥，而年华不待，身体难康，精衰气弱，故曰：「物壮则老。」以此言之，自高者适以自下，自豪者适以自危，不道甚矣！不如去其刚强之心，平平常常，安安稳稳，认理行将去，随天摆布来，庶几不强而自强，不道而有道耶？此下手用火之工，大有危险存焉，学者其慎之。

One skilled in arms values crisp decisiveness; one skilled in fire phasing values stopping swiftly the instant the goal is reached without lingering in battle—hence "resolution and no more."

For a cultivator, even if practice brings the body to radiant vitality and pure yang vigor, never presume upon it! Never boast of your powers—imagining you feel neither hunger nor thirst, walk as though flying, need no brazier in winter and no fan in summer, brimming with unusual feats beyond ordinary mortals—and fancy yourself mighty! The instant you tell yourself "I am strong," restless Posterior Heaven ordinary fire flares right back up: outwardly it invites suspicion and slander, inwardly it rocks the foundation and imperils the Inner Medicine.

Worse still, once that heart of flexing strength sprouts, even if you completed "ten months of gestation and three years of nursing" step by step without missing a single beat, the moment you turn boastful, self-congratulatory, and arrogant toward others, you are no longer a vessel fit to carry the Way! Decisive as you were in achieving success, you will be just as decisive in ruining everything—the disaster of overturning the cauldron is beyond telling!

Besides, anyone who leans on brute strength fails to see that only a humble, lowly heart can cultivate virtue and condense the Way. It is just like plants and trees: tender, supple shoots hold living vigor, while stiff, brittle branches have already lost their sap. Day after day, year after year, time slips past and waits for no one; still straining to prove one's toughness, youth fades, health falters, essence dwindles, and breath weakens—precisely what Laozi means by "what grows rigid soon ages"!

Seen in this light, exalting oneself pulls oneself down, and puffing up with pride pushes oneself straight into danger—how utterly contrary to the Way! Far better to shed that rigid, combative heart early: live plainly and steadily, walk forward recognizing principle, and meet whatever comes as Heaven arranges it. Then, without flexing strength, you become truly strong; without claiming the Way, you embody the Way at every turn. Great peril lies in this work of applying fire—let every student walk with utmost care!

Key Terms

  • Knocking the bamboo to summon the tortoise; strumming the zither to call the phoenix (qiaozhu huangui, guqin zhaofeng): Song–Yuan alchemical code phrases referring broadly to purposeful breath-and-intention techniques used by depleted or aging bodies to rouse dormant vitality and awaken the Kan–Li medicine. Huang Yuanji stresses that such forceful methods are "like waging war," never to be used lightly.
  • Jade Fungus sprout and knife-tip supreme Medicine (yuzhi lingmiao, daogui shangyao): poetic alchemical names for nascent Prior Heaven essence and the Golden Elixir; daogui was an ancient tiny spoon for measuring medicinal powder, symbolizing exact dosing in alchemy.
  • Ten months of gestation, three years of nursing (shiyue huaitai, sannian rubu): alchemical metaphors drawn from pregnancy and infancy—"ten months of gestation" denotes quietly warming the Holy Embryo once formed, while "three years of nursing" denotes the prolonged hidden tending after maturation without flaunting attainments.
  • Ordinary fire (fanhuo): contrasted with calm, gentle Prior Heaven true fire; restless Posterior Heaven heart-fire stirred by panic, haste, arrogance, or showing off, which rapidly scatters Original Breath.
  • What grows rigid soon ages (wu zhuang ze lao): whenever living things strain into rigid toughness, they swiftly tip from peak into decline; tenderness and humility are the lasting fountain of vitality.

Putting It into Practice

  1. All "strong medicine" and high-intensity sprints are strictly for emergencies. Whether extreme fasting, punishing overtime, or heated confrontation, force works like an army—solve the urgent problem and stand down immediately ("resolution and no more"), never turning emergency measures into your lifestyle.
  2. Living plainly and steadily is the highest art of health and self-cultivation. Huang Yuanji's closing line—"walk forward recognizing principle, meet whatever comes as Heaven arranges"—carries quiet power: drop competitiveness and the itch for exotic feats, eat well, sleep deeply, keep your heart level, and you will travel farthest.
  3. At the first taste of success, cool the three sparks of boasting, claiming credit, and pride. Reaching a goal or feeling great energy is precisely when smugness sneaks in; once pride takes over, attention shifts from doing the work to showing off, marking the exact turning point into decline.
  4. Never treat ancient accounts of "expelling illness" as an excuse to skip medical care. If real symptoms—rashes, pain, urinary changes, or persistent discomfort—appear during any mind-body practice, see a qualified doctor and get checked properly. Sound medical care and inner calm go hand in hand.

Translator's Notes

  1. "King Wu donned armor once and settled the realm": Echoes the Doctrine of the Mean ("donning armor once, he possessed the realm") and the Book of Documents ("removing only the ringleaders"), praising King Wu's restrained overthrow of tyranny without wanton killing.
  2. "I urge you, do not chase a marquis's title—one general's glory dries ten thousand bones": From late Tang poet Cao Song's famous anti-war poem Year of Jihai (reading ping jun mo hua in standard editions).
  3. "Shun against the Three Miao... dancing with shields and feathers between the two staircases; in seventy days the Miao submitted": From the Book of Documents ("Counsels of Great Yu"), where shields (gan) and pheasant plumes (yu) were ceremonial dance props symbolizing civil harmony overcoming hostility.
  4. "Assist a bright reign" (fuzuo xichao), "rain and sunshine miss their seasons" (yuyang bushi), and "famine follows famine" (jijin jianzhen): In the base edition, a character was dropped after fuzuo xi (restored as chao by four-character parallel idiom), yang (旸) was misprinted as pan (盼, corrected from the Book of Documents), and jianzhen (荐臻, from the Classic of Poetry) was misprinted as jianquan (渐全).
  5. Textual collation: Base-edition misprints for "radiant harmony" (熙熙皞皞), "vengeance" (冤仇), "bleached bones" (枯骨), "droughts and floods" (旱干水溢), "commander" (为将帅者), "moreover" (况乎), "call the phoenix" (鼓琴招凤), "roots deep and stems firm" (固蒂深根), "thorns and brambles" (荆棘), a stray comma inside "coarse frame" (浊躯), "once a heart of flexing strength sprouts" (况生一自强之心), and repeated confusions among ji (己)/yi (已), er (而)/mian (面), wu (无)/you (尢), and yue (曰)/ri (日) have been corrected in the Chinese source.
