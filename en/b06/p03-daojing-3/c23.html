Chapter 23 · Few Words and Naturalness

Whirlwinds and cloudbursts can never last, for the Great Way rests in non-action and naturalness—moving from "having the Way" and "having Virtue" to "forgetting even what was gained" depends entirely on unwavering trust and an ordinary heart

About This Chapter

Using the vivid image that "a whirlwind does not last a whole morning, nor a sudden downpour a whole day," Chapter 23 lays bare the relationship between "naturalness" and "endurance." Following Laozi's thread, Huang Yuanji offers a remarkably illuminating internal-alchemy reading of "those of the Way become one with the Way; those of Virtue become one with Virtue; those of Loss become one with Loss":

  1. Forced contrivance, however fierce, never lasts: A gale can uproot great trees and a cloudburst can flood the plains, yet what rushes in violently vanishes just as fast—if even heaven and earth cannot sustain a breach of natural rhythm for a single day, how could an ordinary mortal, tiny as a grain of millet in a vast granary? In cultivation, pushing aggressively for rapid results or chasing flashy marvels always starts like a tiger and ends like a snake.
  2. "One with Loss" is not failure, but the highest stage of maturity: Most commentators read "those of Loss become one with Loss" as people who lose the Way inviting ruin upon themselves. Huang Yuanji opens a striking new perspective, interpreting the three lines as three ascending stages of practice: first walking according to the Way's rules ("one with the Way"), next realizing tangible inner attainment ("one with Virtue"), and finally reaching deep, effortless mastery where every deliberate trace of "something gained" melts away into "a carefree sense of having nothing to hold onto" (shuangran ruo shi)—merging seamlessly with the Great Way itself!
  3. Every crooked side-path springs from a single root: "lack of trust": Why do some people begin studying the Way in youth and reach white-haired old age with nothing to show for it—falling either into dry, isolated quietism or into rigid mechanical holding? At bottom, they refuse to trust the plain, upright road of "non-action and naturalness." With subjective opinions wedged in their minds, they keep hunting for shortcuts and exotic tricks, wandering further astray with every step.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：希言自然。故飘风不终朝，骤雨不终日。孰为此者？天地。天地尚不能久，而况于人乎？故从事于道者，道者同于道，德者同于德，失者同于失。同于道者，道亦乐得之；同于德者，德亦乐得之；同于失者，失亦乐得之。信不足焉，有不信焉。

Laozi says: Speaking seldom—refraining from forced commands—is what accords with naturalness. Thus a whirlwind does not last out the morning, and a sudden downpour does not last out the day. Who makes these happen? Heaven and earth. If even heaven and earth cannot sustain them for long, how much less can human beings?

Therefore, among those who devote themselves to the Way: those who practice the Way become one with the Way; those who embody Virtue become one with Virtue; and those who let go even the mark of "attainment" become one with that state of ungrasping "loss." Those who are one with the Way, the Way delights to welcome; those who are one with Virtue, Virtue delights to welcome; those who are one with ungrasping loss, that unattached state delights to welcome. When trust in the Way falls short, doubt and disbelief naturally arise.

§2
Original (Chinese)

道本无声无臭，故曰希言。道本无为无作，故曰自然。夫物之能恒、事之能久者，无非顺天而动，率性以行，一听气机之自运而已。若矫揉造作，不能顺其气机，以合乾坤之运转，日月之升恒，适有如飘荡之风、狂暴之雨，拔大木，涌平川，来之速者去亦速，其势岂能终日终朝哉？虽然，孰是为之？问之天地，而天地不知也。夫天地为万物之主宰，不顺其常，尚不能以耐久，况人在天地，如太仓一粟，又岂不行常道而能悠久者乎？

The Way is by nature without sound or scent, hence "few words" (silent and unforced); the Way is by nature free of contrived action, hence "naturalness."

Whatever endures among things and lasts among affairs does so solely by moving in accord with Heaven's timing, acting in accord with inner nature, and entrusting itself wholly to the spontaneous rotation of vital breath. If instead one forces and contrives, refusing to follow the natural rhythm of breath so as to harmonize with the turning of Qian and Kun and the steady rising of sun and moon, one becomes just like a raging gale or violent downpour—capable of uprooting huge trees and flooding level plains for a moment, yet whatever arrives with furious speed departs just as fast. How could such violence possibly last through a morning or a full day?

And yet, who produces these storms? Ask heaven and earth, and heaven and earth themselves do not know (for they act with no deliberate intent). Even heaven and earth, sovereigns of the myriad things, cannot endure when they depart from their constant course; how much less can a human being—no larger in the cosmos than a single grain of millet in a great granary—hope to endure without walking the constant, upright Way?

§3
Original (Chinese)

故太上论道之原，以无为为宗，自然为用。倘不从事于此，别夸捷径，另诩神奇，误矣。试观学道之士，虽东西南北之遥，声教各异，然既有志于道，不入邪途，无不吻合无间。行道而有得于心，谓之德。既知修道，自然抱德。凡自明其德，绝无纷驰者，无不默契为一，故曰「道者同于道，德者同于德」。又何怪诞之有耶？下手之初，其修也有道有德，有轨有则，脱然洒然，无累无系；到深造自得之候，居安资深，左右逢原，从前所得者，至此爽然若失，工夫纯粹，打成一片，恰似闭门造车，出而合辙，尤不一也，故曰「失者同于失」。此三者，工力不同，进境各别。至于用力之久，苦恼之场，亦化为恬淡之境，洋洋乎别饶佳趣，诩诩然自畅天机。苦已尽矣，乐何极乎？故曰：「同于道者，道亦乐得之；同于德者，德亦乐得之；同于失者，失亦乐得之。」

Therefore, when Laozi speaks of the source of the Way, he takes "non-action" as its fundamental principle and "naturalness" as its function. Anyone who neglects this and instead boasts of shortcuts or flaunts miraculous tricks has gone badly astray.

Look at genuine seekers of the Way across the world: though separated east, west, north, and south, with differing tongues and lineages, as long as their hearts are truly set on the Way and never stray onto crooked paths, what they realize fits together seamlessly without the slightest gap. Practicing the Way until one gains real realization in the heart is called "Virtue" (de); once one knows how to cultivate the Way, one naturally embraces Virtue. All who brighten their own Virtue without letting the mind scatter outward silently accord as one. Hence "those of the Way become one with the Way; those of Virtue become one with Virtue"—what is strange or bizarre about that?

When first setting a hand to practice, one follows the Way and holds to Virtue with clear tracks and guidelines, already feeling light, free, and unencumbered. When one reaches deep mastery and genuine self-attainment—dwelling securely, drawing from deep reserves, finding the source effortlessly on every side—all the deliberate marks of "what I gained before" suddenly melt away into a carefree sense of having nothing to cling to (shuangran ruo shi). One's practice grows utterly pure and seamless, just like building a cart behind closed doors and finding, when you roll it out, that it fits every track under heaven without the slightest mismatch—this is "those of Loss become one with Loss."

These three—"one with the Way," "one with Virtue," and "one with Loss"—reflect differing depths of effort and ascending stages of attainment. After long, steady work, the very field of affliction and vexation transforms into a landscape of serene simplicity, overflowing with quiet delight and effortless harmony with heaven's rhythm. When suffering has run its course, what limit is there to joy? Hence: "Those who are one with the Way, the Way delights to welcome; those who are one with Virtue, Virtue delights to welcome; those who are one with Loss, Loss delights to welcome."

§4
Original (Chinese)

可见无为之体，人所同修；自然之工，人所共用。虽千里万里之圣，千年万年之神，时移地易，亦自然若合符节，有同归于一辙者焉。倘谓自然者，不必尽然，则有臆见横于其中，有异术行乎其内，或执于空而孤修寂炼，或著于实而固执死守，如此等类，不一而足，皆由不信无为之旨、自然之道，而各执己见以为是，无惑乎少年学道，晚景无成，志有余而学不足，终身未得真谛，误入旁门也。可悲也夫，可慨也夫！

From this we see that the substance of "non-action" is what all true practitioners cultivate together, and the skill of "naturalness" is what all use in common. Even sages separated by thousands of miles, or perfected beings separated by thousands of years, across changing eras and distant lands, tally naturally like two halves of a bamboo seal, converging along one and the same track.

Yet if someone insists, "Naturalness can't really be the whole story," then subjective conjecture gets lodged inside and heterodox tricks creep in: some cling to "emptiness" and shut themselves in dry, lifeless quietism; others cling to "solidity" and rigidly lock onto some spot in the body or mechanical technique. Deviations of this sort are too numerous to count. Every single one stems from refusing to trust the purport of non-action and the Way of naturalness, each person clinging to private opinion as supreme. No wonder some set their hearts on the Way in youth yet arrive at old age with nothing accomplished—plenty of ambition, not enough true learning, missing the real truth their whole lives and stumbling into crooked side-paths. How tragic! How lamentable!

§5
Original (Chinese)

此言无为自然之道，即天地日月、幽冥人鬼，莫不同此无为自然，以生以遂，为用为行而已矣。凡人自有生后，聪明机巧，昼夜用尽，本来天理，存者几何？惟有道高人，一顺天理之常，虽下手之初，不无勉强作为，及其成功，一归无为自然之境，有若「不思而得，不勉而中，从容中道」者焉。故以圣人观大道，则无为自然之理，昭昭在人耳目，有不约而同者；若以后人观大道，则无为自然之诣，似乎惟仙惟圣方敢言此，凡人未可语此也。《中庸》云：生学困勉，成功则一，不将为欺人之语哉？非也。缘其始有不信之心，由不道之门，其后愈离愈远，所以无为自然之道，不能尽同，而分门别户，从此起矣。学者明此，方不为旁门左道所惑也。

This chapter teaches that the Way of non-action and naturalness governs everything—from heaven, earth, sun, and moon above to humans and unseen spirits below: nothing is born, matures, functions, or moves except through this same non-action and naturalness.

From the moment ordinary people are born, they burn through cleverness and scheming day and night—how much of their original Heavenly Principle remains? Only a person truly grounded in the Way follows the constancy of Heavenly Principle without deviation. Admittedly, at the very outset of practice, some deliberate effort and conscious restraint cannot be avoided; yet once the work matures, everything returns to the realm of non-action and naturalness—just as the Doctrine of the Mean describes: "attaining without strained thought, hitting the mark without forced effort, moving calmly and naturally in accord with the Way."

Thus, viewed through the eyes of a sage, the principle of non-action and naturalness shines plainly before every eye and ear, so all who awaken to it agree without prior arrangement. Viewed through the eyes of later ordinary people, however, "non-action and naturalness" looks hopelessly lofty, as if only immortals and sages dared speak of it while common folk had no business approaching it. If that were true, wouldn't the Doctrine of the Mean's promise—"whether born knowing, learning through study, or struggling through hardship, whether practicing with ease or with strenuous effort, once achieved the result is identical"—be nothing but empty deception?

Of course it is not deception! The trouble begins when people harbor a seed of "disbelief" right from the start and walk through a door that departs from the Way. As they go on, they drift farther and farther off course, unable to agree on the Way of non-action and naturalness—and that is precisely how rival sects and competing factions spring up. Only when a student sees through this clearly will they never be led astray by crooked side-paths.

Key Terms

  • Few words and naturalness (xiyan ziran): "few words" refers to the Way being soundless, scentless, and free of forced commands; "naturalness" refers to moving in harmony with the innate rhythm of vital breath without contrivance.
  • One with the Way, one with Virtue, one with Loss (tong yu dao, tong yu de, tong yu shi): Huang Yuanji interprets these as three ascending stages of cultivation—practicing along the Way's clear tracks, embodying genuine inner attainment without distraction, and reaching pure maturity where even the self-conscious sense of "having gained something" dissolves completely.
  • Dry quietism and rigid holding (guxiu jilian, guzhi sishou): the two opposite deviations Huang Yuanji warns against—clinging to lifeless emptiness on one side, or rigidly forcing tangible sensations and mechanical techniques on the other.
  • Prior Heaven / Posterior Heaven: though the two terms do not appear verbatim in this chapter, the contrast between "original Heavenly Principle" and "cleverness and scheming," and the movement from "initial deliberate effort" back to "non-action and naturalness," follows the exact arc of using Posterior Heaven effort to return to Prior Heaven naturalness.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Choose slow and steady over fierce and brief. Just as a whirlwind never lasts a whole morning, pushing too hard—binge all-nighters, extreme crash diets, or forcing marathon meditation sessions—quickly burns out. Ten calm, steady minutes every day beat three frantic hours on a sudden whim.
  2. Accept the awkwardness at the beginning. Huang Yuanji is refreshingly realistic: "At the very outset of practice, some deliberate effort cannot be avoided; once mature, all returns to non-action and naturalness." Feeling stiff or self-conscious when first regulating breath, quieting the mind, or building a new habit is completely normal—stay steady, and natural ease follows.
  3. Stay wary of "shortcuts" and "miraculous claims." Any instant method promising to bypass everyday self-cultivation for sudden wonders goes against nature's rhythm. The test of the real Way is simple and grounded: it leaves you quieter, calmer, and clearer—not more grandiose or hyped up.
  4. True maturity forgets to keep score of its own progress. Moving from "following the rules," to "feeling real benefit," to finally letting go of even the pride of attainment and living in simple ease—that is the unhurried freedom of becoming "one with Loss."

Translator's Notes

  1. "The steady rising of sun and moon" (riyue zhi shengheng): Alludes to the Classic of Poetry ("Minor Odes," Tianbao: "like the waxing moon, like the rising sun"), depicting the unceasing regular courses of sun and moon. The base edition's misprint kai (开) for sheng (升) has been corrected.
  2. "A single grain of millet in a great granary" (taicang yisu): From Zhuangzi ("Autumn Floods": "like a tiny grain of tare in a vast granary"), expressing human smallness within the cosmos.
  3. "Dwelling securely, drawing from deep reserves, finding the source on every side": From Mencius (4B.14): "A noble person delves deeply into the Way desiring to attain it for himself; attaining it, he dwells in it securely; dwelling securely, he draws on it deeply; drawing deeply, he meets its source on left and right."
  4. "Attaining without strained thought..." and "born knowing, studying, struggling—the achievement is one": Both from the Doctrine of the Mean, summarizing its teaching that differing starting points and degrees of effort lead to the same ultimate realization.
  5. Textual collation: Base-edition misprints for "whirling" (飘荡), "straying onto crooked paths" (不入邪途), "free and easy" (洒然), "clinging to emptiness" (执于空), "rigidly holding" (固执死守), "private opinion" (己见), "unaccomplished in old age" (晚景无成), and "eyes and ears" (耳目) have been corrected in the Chinese source.
