II · Classic of the Way (Ch. 11–20) · 道经

Chapter 17 · Work Done, Affairs Accomplished

Governing oneself and governing the world spring from one single "truth"; descending from effortless transformation to clever deceit, utmost sincerity and true trust remain the root of both alchemy and statecraft

About This Chapter

On the surface, this chapter ranks four tiers of governance across history: the highest ruler leaves the people scarcely aware of being ruled, assuming everything happens naturally; the next earns affection and praise through benevolence; the next commands fearful obedience through stern laws; and the lowest relies on craft and deceit until trust collapses into mutual contempt.

Yet Huang Yuanji immediately uncovers the deeper structure of the Daodejing—radiating in every direction, where governing oneself is governing the world—and pinpoints the single overlooked eye of the entire chapter: the word "trust" (xin):

  1. Self-cultivation and statecraft alike hinge on a single "true" heart. Whether governing a realm or refining oneself, unbroken sincerity resonates effortlessly outward; the moment sincerity falters within ("when trust is insufficient"), suspicion and deceit erupt on every side ("there are those who do not trust").
  2. The four tiers mirror both historical decline and stages of practice. Huang Yuanji maps "unaware of it → love and praise → fear → contempt" both to the historical descent from Sovereign → Emperor → King → Hegemon → scheming strategist, and to four levels of cultivation: pure non-action (six roots settled) → transformation through warmth and respect → stern restraint → clever manipulation.
  3. "Trust belongs to Earth"—true trust is itself the Mysterious Pass and root of the Elixir. In Five-Phase internal alchemy, Trust belongs to central Earth, which is True Intention (Intention-earth). From start to finish, cultivation rests not on flashy tricks, but on unceasing sincerity. The line between sage and fool, truth and sham, life and death lies in that single instant of sincerity.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：太上，不知有之（诸家皆作「下知有之」，然与经意不合，此传写之误也）；其次，亲之誉之；其次，畏之；其次，侮之。信不足焉，有不信焉。犹兮其贵言。功成事遂，百姓皆谓「我自然」。（「犹兮」句，言优游感孚，慎重其语言。）

Laozi says: With the very highest rulers, the people are unaware that anyone rules over them (Huang Yuanji's note: most editions read "those below merely know they exist," but that contradicts the sense of the classic and is a scribal error). With the next tier, people grow close to them and praise them. With the next, people fear them. With the lowest, people despise and defy them. When trust is lacking within, there will surely be those who do not trust. How measured and cautious they are—sparing of their words! (Huang Yuanji's note: this line describes unhurried, heartfelt influence and extreme care in issuing words.) When the work is completed and affairs succeed, the common people all say, "We are simply like this by ourselves!"

§2
Original (Chinese)

太上治身之道，即治世之道，总不外一真而已。真以持己则己修，真以应物则物遂，虽有内外之分，人己之别，而此心之真，则无或异焉。人能至诚无息，则人之感之者亦无息；人或至诚有间，则物之应之者亦有间。盖人同此心，心同此理，修其身而天下自平，丧其真而天下必乱也。自三皇五帝，以逮于今，从未有或异者。太上欲人，以诚信之道自修，即以诚信之道洽人。不见而章，不动而变，无为而成，在己不知有治之道，在人观感薰陶，亦不觉其自化而不知其所之。此上古之淳风，吾久不得而见矣。故太上曰：「太上，不知有之。」以君民熙熙皞皞，共嬉游于光天化日之下，倘非诚信存存，乌有如斯之神化乎？

Laozi's Way of governing the body is one and the same as the Way of governing the world—coming down, in the end, to a single word: truth (zhen). Holding oneself with truth cultivates the self; meeting others with truth brings everything to fruition. Though we distinguish inner from outer and self from others, the truthfulness of the heart never varies. When a person maintains unbroken sincerity, others' response to them is likewise unbroken; when sincerity falters and develops gaps, the world's response develops gaps as well. For all human beings share this same heart, and every heart shares this same principle: cultivate yourself and the realm naturally finds peace; lose your truth and the realm inevitably falls into chaos. From the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors down to our own day, this law has never changed.

Laozi wishes people to cultivate themselves through sincerity and trustworthiness, and by that very same sincerity harmonize with others: shining clearly without self-display, transforming without forced agitation, accomplishing without meddling. On the ruler's side, there is no self-conscious pride in "governing"; on the people's side, bathed day and night in quiet example, they grow better without even noticing who guided them. How long it has been since I have seen that wholesome spirit of high antiquity! Hence Laozi says: "With the very highest, people are unaware of it." Ruler and people live together in bright ease and quiet harmony beneath open skies—without unceasing sincerity dwelling within, how could such wondrous transformation ever occur?

§3
Original (Chinese)

至皇古之休风已邈，太上之郅治无闻，则世风愈降，大道愈乖，有不堪语言见闻者。若去古未远，斯道尚存，天性未漓，真诚尚在，但非太古之笃实，亦为今世之光华。同一治也，一则无心而自化，一则有意以施仁，保民如保赤子，爱民如爱家人。斯时之尊上而敬长者，亦若如响斯应，即感孚不一，德化难齐，亦惟亲之爱之、奖之誉之，绝不加以词色，俾之怀德畏威，是虽不及乎太上，然亦遵道遵路之可嘉，所谓「大道废，有仁义」者是。是皇降为帝，帝降为王，皆本天德以行王道者也。

Once the blessed atmosphere of high antiquity grew remote and that supreme effortless governance was heard of no more, public morals slid lower and drifted farther from the Great Way, reaching states painful to describe or hear. Yet in ages not too distant from antiquity, traces of the Way still lingered: innate nature was not yet watered down, and sincerity remained alive. Though falling short of primal simplicity, it still shone as a bright glory of its time. Both were forms of good governance—one transformed effortlessly without conscious intent, while the other deliberately practiced benevolence, cherishing the people like newborn infants and loving them like one's own kin.

In such times, people honored superiors and respected elders as naturally as an echo answers a voice. Even when moral influence did not reach everyone uniformly, rulers still drew them closer with affection and encouraged them with praise, never flashing harsh words or angry looks to instill fear. Though inferior to the very highest era, this still walked the upright path and deserved praise—the exact condition Laozi calls in the next chapter "When the Great Way declines, there arise humaneness and righteousness." Thus Sovereigns descended to Emperors and Emperors to Kings; though stepped down a rank, they still practiced the Kingly Way grounded in Heavenly Virtue.

§4
Original (Chinese)

以后古风愈远，大道愈偷，王降为霸，假以行真，心各一心，见各一见，与帝王之一德感孚者远矣。故礼教犹是，政刑犹是，法制禁令亦犹是，而此心之真伪，则杳不相若焉。惟借才华以经世，凭法度以导民，处置得宜，措施合法，使民望而畏之，不敢犯法违条，即是精明之主，太平之世。等而下之，不堪言矣。恃智巧以驱民，逞奸谋而取众，以神头鬼面之心，为神出鬼没之治；当其悻悻自雄，嚣嚣自得，未有不以为智过三王、才高五霸，而斯世之百姓，卒惕惕乎中夜各警，其侮民也实甚，斯民虽不敢言，而此心睽违，终无一息之浃洽，所以不旋踵而祸乱随之矣。孔子曰：「上好信则民用情。」倘信不足于己，安能见信于民？此上与下所以相欺而相诈也。夫制度文诰、条教号令之颁，虽圣人亦所不废，然情伪分焉，感应殊焉。惟帝王以身作则，以信孚民，法立而政行，言出而民信，卒至光被四表，功成事遂，如尧之於变时雍，舜之恭己无为，而百姓皆谓「我自然」。噫！此真信之所及，以视信不足于内者，相判何啻天渊哉！

Later still, as ancient ways receded further and the Great Way grew threadbare, the Kingly Way sank into Hegemony. Borrowing the banner of righteousness to advance private ambition, ruler and ruled each harbored separate hearts and conflicting views—worlds apart from the earlier sage-kings whose single, undivided virtue moved every heart. Outwardly, rites and teachings looked identical, laws and punishments looked identical, regulations and edicts looked identical—yet the sincerity versus hypocrisy underneath differed like night and day. At that stage, rulers could only manage affairs by clever talent and steer the populace by strict statutes; when measures were well administered and legally sound so that the people looked on in awe and dared not break the law, that already counted as a shrewd ruler and a peaceful era.

Lower still, the sight becomes unbearable: relying on crafty manipulation to herd the populace and wielding sly intrigues to capture followers, carrying a treacherous mind to run an unpredictable, duplicitous regime. Swaggering with arrogant self-satisfaction, every such ruler imagines himself wiser than the Three Kings and shrewder than the Five Hegemons—while ordinary citizens lie awake at night in anxious dread, guarding against one another. This is despising and insulting the people to the extreme! Though citizens dare not speak aloud, their hearts have long since turned away without a single moment of genuine harmony; inevitably, upheaval follows hard on its heels.

Confucius said: "When those above prize trustworthiness, the people respond with genuine feeling." If you lack trustworthiness within yourself, how can you expect the people to trust you? That is the root of mutual deception between high and low. Institutions, proclamations, and laws are not abolished even by sages—yet sincerity and falsity divide them cleanly, and the resonance they evoke is worlds apart. Only a ruler who leads by personal example and wins hearts through trust sees laws take hold smoothly and words immediately believed, until radiance reaches all four quarters, work is done, and affairs succeed—like Emperor Yao, under whom the people naturally grew harmonious, or Emperor Shun, sitting in reverent composure and practicing non-action, while all the people remarked, "We are simply like this by ourselves!" Alas! Compare what true trustworthiness achieves with what ensues when trust is lacking within—the chasm between them is far wider than heaven and the abyss!

§5
Original (Chinese)

《道德》一经，原是四通八达，修身在此，治世在此，推之天下万事万物，亦无有出此范围者。即如此章「太上」二字，言上等之人，抱上等之质，故曰太上。上德清净无为，六根皆定。其次爱敬化民，有感即通。其次以威严驭世。其次以智巧导民，所谓术也。而其极妙者，莫如信。信属土，修炼始终，纯以意土为妙用。故太上云：「其精甚真，其中有信」，是丹本也。信非他，一诚而已。人能至诚无息，则丹之为丹，即在是矣。但信与伪，相去无几。克念作圣，罔念作狂，人禽界，生死关，所争只一间耳。吾愿后学，寻得真信，以为真常之道可也。信在何处？即是玄关一窍。人其知之否？

The Daodejing opens in every direction at once: the principles of self-cultivation are here, the principles of governing the world are here, and extending outward to all affairs and creatures under heaven, nothing falls outside its compass. Take the opening phrase "the very highest" (taishang): it refers to people of the highest caliber bearing the finest innate quality.

Highest virtue is pure and effortless, with all six sense-roots completely settled. Next comes transforming others through warmth and respect, where every sincere impulse finds immediate resonance. Next comes steering affairs through stern authority. Lowest comes herding people through clever schemes—mere manipulative technique. Yet the most marvelous pivot of the entire chapter is the single word "trust" (xin). In the Five Phases, Trust belongs to Earth; from beginning to end, alchemical cultivation relies solely on central Intention-earth (True Intention) for its subtle operation. That is why Laozi says later in Chapter 21, "Its Essence is utterly true; within it lies Trust"—for this is the very root of the Elixir!

What is Trust? Nothing other than pure sincerity (cheng). If a person maintains unceasing utmost sincerity, the very essence of the Golden Elixir is right there. Yet the distance between genuine trust and hollow pretense is razor-thin. As the Book of Documents warns, "Restrain stray thoughts and you become a sage; give them free rein and you become a fool." The boundary between human and beast, the threshold between life and death, often hangs on a hair's breadth. My wish for future students is that you discover this genuine Trust and make it your constant Way. And where exactly does Trust dwell? Right inside the single aperture of the Mysterious Pass. How many people truly realize this?

Key Terms

  • True Intention (zhenyi): linked here to "Intention-earth" and "Trust belongs to Earth," the central governing factor throughout internal alchemy whose essence is unbroken sincerity.
  • Mysterious Pass (xuanguan): at the chapter's close, Huang Yuanji states directly, "Where does Trust dwell? Right inside the single aperture of the Mysterious Pass"—showing that the Mysterious Pass is not an exotic hidden spot, but the point of unfeigned sincerity and undivided mind.
  • Essence (jing): citing Chapter 21 ("Its Essence is utterly true; within it lies Trust"), noting that Prior Heaven true essence always carries tangible, verifiable sincerity and living response.
  • Measured and sparing of words (you xi qi gui yan): Laozi's portrait of the highest sage who refrains from issuing constant commands; Huang Yuanji explains it as unhurried, heartfelt influence and extreme restraint in speech, leading through personal example.

Putting It into Practice

  1. In leadership and family life, the highest skill leaves others feeling "we did this ourselves." Issue fewer hollow directives and stop demanding center stage ("sparing of words"). Anchor order and trust in personal example so colleagues and family succeed with ease and ownership.
  2. Check your own "sufficiency of trust" before blaming others. When collaborators drag their feet, family members stay guarded, or teammates pass the buck, look in the mirror first: did I slip into calculation, break small promises, or hold back sincerity? "When trust is lacking, there are those who do not trust" is the most honest law of cause and effect.
  3. Watch against sliding from integrity into manipulation. Clever tactics, slick rhetoric, and manufactured urgency may extract short-term compliance, but they drain long-term trust until silent resentment and defiance ("despising them") take over. Better a plain, slightly clumsy honesty than polished trickery.
  4. Treat utmost sincerity as your steadying anchor. Cultivation need not feel esoteric: refusing to deceive or brush past your own intentions, curbing one stray impulse and holding fast to one honest thought, is the most solid "Intention-earth" practice there is.

Translator's Notes

  1. "With the very highest, people are unaware of it": Standard Wang Bi editions read "those below merely know they exist" (xia zhi you zhi), whereas Heshang Gong and several early versions read "unaware of it" (bu zhi you zhi). Huang Yuanji explicitly argues that the former conflicts with the classic's spirit and defends the latter reading of effortless, imperceptible transformation.
  2. "Shining clearly without self-display, transforming without agitation, accomplishing without meddling": Quoted from the Doctrine of the Mean (Zhongyong), characterizing the quiet potency of utmost sincerity.
  3. "When those above prize trustworthiness, the people respond with genuine feeling": From the Analects ("Zilu"), pointing to the direct reciprocity of trust between leaders and citizens.
  4. Yao's harmonious transformation and Shun's reverent non-action: The former alludes to the Book of Documents ("Canon of Yao"), where the people spontaneously grow harmonious; the latter to the Analects ("Wei Linggong"), praising Shun for governing effortlessly by keeping his own person reverently composed facing south.
  5. "Its Essence is utterly true; within it lies Trust": Quoted from Chapter 21 of the Daodejing, invoked here to confirm Trust-Earth as the root of the Elixir.
  6. "Restrain stray thoughts and you become a sage; give them free rein and you become a fool": From the Book of Documents ("Duofang"), showing that the shift between sagehood and folly turns on a single moment of mindfulness.
  7. Textual collation: Several base-text misprints were corrected in the Chinese source, including 分 → 兮 in 犹兮 and its gloss, 日 → 曰, 等实 → 笃实, 散长者 → 敬长者, 难产 → 难齐, 玉 → 王 in three instances, 香不相若 → 杳不相若, 淡洽 → 浃洽, 光之 → 尧之, 躬己 → 恭己, and an extraneous comma removed from 而百姓皆谓.
