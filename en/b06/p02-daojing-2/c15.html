II · Classic of the Way (Ch. 11–20) · 道经

Chapter 15 · Subtle, Wondrous, Profound, and Penetrating

Seven everyday images sketch the caution, simplicity, and spaciousness of one who embodies the Way; settling turbidity in stillness and letting vitality stir gently from lasting peace, never seeking fullness so as to stay ever renewed

About This Chapter

Having depicted the formless substance of the Way in the previous chapter, Laozi turns in this chapter to the living person who embodies the Way: what does an accomplished sage actually look like?

Laozi strings together seven vivid everyday similes: cautious as crossing an icy river in winter, watchful as fearing neighbors on every side, composed and respectful as a guest, melting and unencumbered as ice about to thaw, solid and honest as uncarved wood, open and vast as an empty valley, and outwardly unassuming—almost like muddy water. Tracing these seven qualities, Huang Yuanji brings out three core insights:

  1. The Way holds nothing bizarre; its whole essence lies in reverence (jing). Citing King Wen's "reverent attentiveness" and Confucius's measured, mindful steps, Huang Yuanji shows that a genuine master puts on no mystical theatrics—only steady inward respect, careless in neither a single thought nor a single task.
  2. Balanced cultivation of stillness and movement: "stillness condenses spirit; movement generates breath." For Huang Yuanji, the classic lines "Who can settle turbidity in stillness until it slowly clears?" and "Who can abide long in peace until movement gently stirs life?" point directly to guarding the center and embracing the One and the Living Zi Hour: when the mind is clouded by worldly dust, deep stillness lets it clear of its own accord; when inner nature rests long in peace, vitality stirs gently at the depth of stillness—ready to be harvested as Medicine and refined into the Elixir.
  3. "Those who preserve this Way do not desire fullness." Whatever rushes into showy "newness" wears out fastest. Remaining humble, empty, and unburdened by achievement leaves only the outward shell appearing worn and plain, while the inner spirit stays eternally fresh.

Safety note: Expressions in this chapter such as "stillness condenses spirit, movement generates breath" and "harvesting as Medicine and refining into the Elixir" describe natural bodily rhythms arising out of quiet stillness. Do not rush for quick results or deliberately try to provoke physical sensations. Maintaining a calm, modest, unhurried attitude directly accords with the chapter's teaching of "not desiring fullness" and "not seeking flashy newness."

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：古之善为士者，微妙玄通，深不可识。夫惟不可识，故强为之容：豫兮若冬涉川，犹兮若畏四邻，俨兮其若客，涣兮若冰之将释，敦兮其若朴，旷兮其若谷，浑兮其若浊。孰能浊以澄，静之徐清？孰能安以久，动之徐生？保此道者，不欲盈。夫惟不盈，故能敝不新成。

Laozi says: The skilled masters of antiquity were subtle and wondrous, profound and penetrating—so deep they could not be fathomed. Precisely because they cannot be fathomed, one can only make a rough sketch of their outward bearing: hesitant and cautious, as if wading across a river in winter; watchful and alert, as if wary of neighbors on all four sides; composed and dignified, as if a guest in another's house; yielding and unbound, like ice about to melt; honest and solid, like uncarved timber; spacious and open, like an empty valley; blended and unassuming, like muddy water. Who can let what is turbid settle through stillness until it gradually becomes clear? Who can abide long in stillness until vitality gently stirs to life? Those who keep this Way never wish to be full to the brim. Precisely because they never seek fullness, they can remain worn and unpretentious without chasing shallow, hasty "newness."

§2
Original (Chinese)

太上前章言道体，此章言体道之人。人与道，是二而是一也。道无可见，因人可见。人何能仙？以道而仙。道者何？真一之气也。真一之气，即《中庸》之德也。欲修大道，岂有他哉？文王小心翼翼昭事上帝，孔子足蹜蹜如有循。道之为道，不过一敬焉耳。人能以敬居心，一念不苟，一事不轻，大道不即此而在乎？虽然，道无奇怪，尤赖有体道者，存乎其间，斯道乃不虚悬于天壤。故太上云：古之善为士者，「其为物不二，则其生物不测」，何其至微而至妙乎？「寂然不动，感而遂通」，何其至玄而至通乎？顾其心之浩浩、气之洋洋，不啻江海之深，令人无从测识。

In the previous chapter Laozi spoke of the substance of the Way; here he speaks of the person who embodies the Way. Person and Way appear to be two, yet are in reality one: the Way itself is invisible, becoming visible only through the person who lives it; and by what means can a human being become an immortal? Only through union with the Way. What is the Way? It is the Breath of True Unity (One Breath of Prior Heaven), which is the very same sincere Virtue described in the Doctrine of the Mean. To cultivate the Great Way, is there really any other secret? Look at King Wen, serving High Heaven brightly with cautious, reverent care; look at Confucius, walking with short, steady, mindful steps as though tracing a guiding line. What makes the Way the Way comes down to nothing other than a single word: reverence (jing). If a person keeps reverence in the heart—never sloppy in a single thought, never careless in a single deed—is the Great Way not right there before them?

Even so, while the Way contains nothing weird or miraculous, it depends upon living people who embody it in this world so that it does not remain an empty abstraction suspended between heaven and earth. Thus Laozi praises the accomplished adepts of old: just as the Doctrine of the Mean says, "Being undivided in its nature, its generation of things is unfathomable"—how supremely subtle and wondrous! And as the Book of Changes says, "Silent and unmoving, yet upon stimulus it penetrates everything"—how supremely profound and penetrating! With hearts vast and boundless and breath flowing full and free, deep as great rivers and seas, they leave ordinary people with no way to take their measure.

§3
Original (Chinese)

故太上曰：「夫惟不识，故强为之容。」以明其内之真不可得而测，其外之容有可强而形焉。其心心慎独，在在存诚，如豫之渡河，必俟冰凝而后渡；若犹之夜行，必待风静而后行，最小心也。其整齐严肃，亦如显客之遥临，不敢稍慢。其脱然无所累，夷然无可系，又似冰释为水，杳无形迹可寻。其忠厚存心，仁慈待物，浑如太朴完全，雕琢不事，而浑然无间。其休休有容，谦谦自抑，何异深山穷谷，虚而无物，大而能容耶？其形如此，其性可知。要皆浑天载于无声，顺帝则而不识，宛若舜居深山，了无异于深山野人者，其浑噩之风，岂昏浊者所得而拟乎？

Therefore Laozi says: "Precisely because they cannot be fathomed, I roughly sketch their outward bearing"—showing that while their inner truth can never be gauged, the demeanor radiating outward can still be loosely pictured:

Thought by thought they practice vigilance in solitude and harbor sincerity everywhere—like the wary beast yu crossing a winter river, carefully testing until the ice is solid before stepping across; or the alert you moving by night, waiting until the wind stills before venturing out, cautious in the extreme. Their bearing is orderly and poised, like an honored guest visiting from afar who would not dream of the least irreverence. Yet when releasing attachments and moving at ease, they are utterly unencumbered and untethered—like winter ice melting into flowing water, leaving not a single stiff trace behind. Loyal and generous at heart, kind and gentle toward all creatures, they resemble whole, uncarved timber, untouched by artificial chiseling and completely seamless. Broad-minded, accommodating, and quietly humble, how do they differ from a deep, secluded mountain valley—empty within, yet vast enough to hold everything?

From such outward conduct, one can glimpse their inner nature. In short, they merge Heaven's workings into silence and follow Heaven's pattern without self-conscious pride, just like Emperor Shun dwelling in the deep mountains, outwardly indistinguishable from the rustic woodsmen around him. How could genuinely muddled, worldly people ever be compared to such wholesome, unassuming simplicity?

§4
Original (Chinese)

但浑与浊相肖，圣与凡一理。凡人之浊，浊真浊也；圣人之浊，浑若浊也，实则至浊至清而已。然圣不自圣，所以为圣；凡不自凡，竟自终凡。孰能于心之染污者，而澄之使静，俟其静久而清光现焉？孰能于性之本安者，而涵泳之，扩充之，迨其养之久久，而生之徐徐，采以为药，炼以为丹？保生之道，不诚在是乎？此静以凝神，动以生气，即守中，即阳生活子时也。由此一升一降，收归鼎炉，渐采渐炼，渐炼渐凝，无非一心不二，万缘皆空，保守此阳而已。有而不有，虚而愈虚，有至虚之心，无持盈之念，是以能返真一之气，得真常之道焉。又曰「能敝不新成」者何？盖以凡事之新成者，其敝必速，兹则敝之无可敝也，敝者其迹，不敝者其神，一真内含，万灵外著，其微妙玄通，固有如是焉耳。

Yet "unassuming wholeness" and "turbidity" look much alike from the outside, just as sage and ordinary person share one and the same basic nature. The difference is this: an ordinary person's muddiness is genuine muddiness, whereas the sage's "muddiness" is merely an unassuming fullness that looks turbid—beneath the plainest exterior hides the purest clarity. More decisively still, the sage never deems himself a sage, and thereby becomes a sage; the ordinary person refuses to recognize his ordinariness, and thereby remains ordinary to the end.

Who, then, when the mind is clouded with worldly grime, can let it settle quietly until—after deep, sustained stillness—clear radiance emerges of itself? And who can steep and expand their innately peaceful nature until, after long nurturing, fresh vitality stirs gently forth, ready to be gathered as Medicine and refined into the Elixir? Does the Way of preserving life not truly lie right here?

This rhythm—"using stillness to condense spirit (concentrating Spirit and entering the Cavity of Breath) and subtle movement to generate breath"—is precisely what Daoists call "guarding the center and embracing the One" and the "Living Zi Hour" when a single spark of yang stirs to life. Through natural ascent and descent, gathered back into the tripod and furnace of the lower center, gradually harvested, gradually refined, and gradually condensed, the entire art comes down to one undivided heart, empty of worldly entanglements, simply safeguarding this spark of Prior Heaven yang. Even when one has attained something, one acts as though having nothing; the emptier one becomes, the more open and receptive one remains, harboring a supremely vacant heart free of any urge to cling to fullness—and thereby returning to the One Breath of Prior Heaven and realizing the true, constant Way.

And why does Laozi conclude with "able to remain worn without seeking new completion"? Because whatever bursts hastily into shiny newness is sure to wear out quickly. Here, by contrast, one rests in an unpretentious plainness that cannot be worn down further: what appears weathered is only the outer shell, while what never decays is the inner Spirit. One pure truth held within, ten thousand responsive wonders shining without—such is the real meaning of the ancient sages' "subtle, wondrous, profound, and penetrating" mastery!

§5
Original (Chinese)

此言体道者之谨慎小心。虽曰道本虚无，而有道高人，自能无形而形，无象而象，若内外一致者然。章内「若」字七句，皆借物以形容道妙，正见微妙通玄，渊深不可测度处。「孰能」以下数句，是言未能成德而求以入道者，浊不多澄，静存则心体自洁；安贵于久，动察则神智不穷。满招损，故不欲盈也；速易敝，故不新成也。吾愿学人，虚而有容，朴而无琢，浑浑灏灏，随在昭诚悫之风，斯人心未有不化为道心，凡气未有不易为真气者。切勿以深莫能测，遂逡巡而不前也！

This chapter speaks of the attentive caution of those who embody the Way. Although the Way itself is empty and formless, accomplished masters naturally give visible dignity to the formless and palpable bearing to the imageless, harmonizing inner and outer as one. The seven similes beginning with "as if" all borrow natural images to portray the Way's subtlety, revealing precisely that unfathomable, abyssal depth.

The lines beginning with "Who can..." address students who have not yet perfected virtue and are seeking entry into the Way: when the mind is turbid, do not thrash around trying to scrub it from outside—simply rest quietly, and the mind's substance clarifies by itself. When peace is found, value endurance—quietly sensing subtle movement brings inexhaustible clarity and wisdom. Fullness invites loss, hence "not desiring fullness"; haste brings quick decay, hence "not rushing into newness." My heartfelt wish for students is that you become open and accommodating, simple and uncarved, spacious and unassuming, radiating quiet sincerity wherever you go. Do that, and the ordinary human mind cannot fail to transform into the mind of the Way, nor ordinary breath fail to transmute into true breath. Never hesitate and hang back simply because the Way seems too deep to fathom!

Key Terms

  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: referred to here as "Breath of True Unity," which Huang Yuanji equates with the sincere Virtue of the Doctrine of the Mean, identifying return to this breath as the root of attaining the Way.
  • guarding the center and embracing the One: the practice of abiding in centered stillness with an undivided mind, linked here to "stillness condenses spirit" and "emptying all entanglements."
  • Living Zi Hour: the spontaneous stirring of Prior Heaven yang at the extreme depth of stillness, corresponding here to "abiding long in peace until movement gently stirs life" ("movement generates breath").
  • Medicine (yao): an internal alchemy metaphor for the Prior Heaven yang vitality emerging gently out of deep stillness—"harvested as Medicine and refined into the Elixir."
  • concentrating Spirit and entering the Cavity of Breath: the foundational method of gathering spirit through stillness into the inner tripod and furnace, summarized here as "stillness condenses spirit, movement generates breath."
  • Worn without seeking new completion (bi bu xin cheng): Laozi's image of the sage content with plainness rather than showy novelty; Huang Yuanji explains it as "what wears is the outer trace, what never wears is the inner Spirit"—outward simplicity preserving inward durability.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Bring "reverence" into every single thought and small task. Real cultivation does not hinge on exotic experiences, but on being "careless in neither a single thought nor a single deed." Approaching ordinary tasks, spoken words, and solitary thoughts with the quiet attentiveness of "crossing winter ice" naturally gathers scattered spirit.
  2. When your mind is agitated, stop fighting it—let it "settle in stillness until it slowly clears." Struggling aggressively against turbulent thoughts or emotions only stirs up more mud. Like a glass of murky water, stop shaking it and sit quietly for a few minutes; sediment sinks and clarity returns on its own.
  3. True vitality grows out of sustained calm. Fresh insight and energy rarely come from frantic pressure; they follow "abiding long in peace until movement gently stirs life." Settle yourself steadily first, and genuine momentum wells up naturally.
  4. Guard against complacency and rushing to show off. Growing self-satisfied after modest progress or hurrying to parade flashy "new results" is usually where decline begins. Keep some empty margin and a plain, unhurried posture if you want to go far.

Translator's Notes

  1. Cautious as yu crossing a winter river, watchful as you fearing neighbors: Traditional commentaries often interpret yu (an elephant-like beast) and you (a cautious ape or monkey) as two wary animals slow to act without checking carefully. Huang Yuanji follows this reading to illustrate vigilant solitude and sincerity.
  2. King Wen serving High Heaven with cautious care: From the Classic of Poetry ("Major Odes," Daming), describing King Wen's attentive, reverent devotion to Heaven.
  3. Confucius walking with short, mindful steps as if following a line: From the Analects ("Xiangdang"), describing Confucius's composed, reverent bearing while carrying a ritual jade tablet.
  4. "Being undivided in its nature, its generation of things is unfathomable": From the Doctrine of the Mean (Zhongyong), characterizing the unified creative power of Heaven and Earth.
  5. "Silent and unmoving, yet upon stimulus it penetrates everything": From the Appended Remarks (Xici shang) of the Book of Changes.
  6. Shun dwelling in the deep mountains, indistinguishable from mountain rustics: From Mencius ("Jinxin I"), portraying the sage's unassuming, plain exterior.
  7. Textual collation: Several base-text misprints were corrected in the Chinese source, including 日 → 曰, 小心翼冀 → 小心翼翼, 香无形迹 → 杳无形迹, 昏独 → 昏浊 (matching 浑与浊相肖 below), 万缘皆室 → 万缘皆空, and 徽妙通玄 → 微妙通玄.
