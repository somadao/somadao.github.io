Chapter 12 · Leaving That and Taking This

Sensory indulgence and rare goods drain the spirit; cultivation values emptying the mind to nourish inner nature and filling the belly to establish life, prioritizing what is within over what is without

About This Chapter

This chapter addresses the sensory temptations everyone faces every day: dazzling sights, seductive music, rich delicacies, exhilarating chases, and rare possessions. Laozi delivers five rapid warnings before landing on a single conclusion: "Therefore the sage acts for the belly and not for the eye; hence he leaves that and takes this."

Rather than turning the chapter into grim asceticism, Huang Yuanji unpacks it lucidly from two angles—finite human vitality and the true joy of the heart:

  1. The hidden ledger of sensory pleasure: gratifying the outer body, harming the inner mind. Silk strings, fine food, and refined enjoyment are not inherently evil—ancient sages themselves did not abolish music or well-seasoned meals. What truly injures us is indulgence and excess. Human energy is limited: when the eyes lock onto dazzling spectacles, spirit scatters; when the ears chase sensual melodies, inner clarity grows dull.
  2. The alchemical meaning of "for the belly, not for the eye." The "eye" represents outward-chasing sensory cravings. For Huang Yuanji, the "belly" means far more than eating one's fill: it means "emptying the mind to nourish inner nature, and filling the belly to establish life"—gathering back the spirit scattered abroad to warm and cultivate the One Breath of Prior Heaven that arrives with us at birth and departs with us at death.
  3. Things come to an end; the Way is inexhaustible. Where today are Emperor Shun's famed zither, Yi Ya's delicacies, or the Jade Disk of He? However priceless external things may be, they vanish in the end. Only cultivating one's own inner nature and life yields an indestructible treasure we truly carry with us.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：五色令人目盲，五音令人耳聋，五味令人口爽。驰骋田猎，令人心发狂；难得之货，令人行妨。是以圣人为腹不为目，故去彼取此。

Laozi says: The five colors blind the eye; the five tones deafen the ear; the five flavors dull and spoil the palate. Racing horses and hunting drive the mind to madness; goods hard to obtain obstruct right conduct and invite harm. Therefore the sage acts for the belly and not for the eye—leaving behind what lies outside ("that") and choosing what abides within ("this").

§2
Original (Chinese)

世之营营逐逐，驰心于声色货利之场，极目遐观，爽心悦口者，非以此中佳境诚足乐耶？孰知人世之乐，其乐有限。惟吾心之乐，其乐无穷。又况乐之所在，即忧之所在。有益于身者，即有损于心。如五彩之章施也，其色光华，其文灿烂，谁不见之而色喜、望之而神惊？讵知目之所注，神即眩焉。人生精力，能有几何？似此留心物色，纵性怡情，以为美观，未有不气阻神销，胸怀缭乱，而目反为之盲也。故曰：「五色令人目盲」，诚至论也。

Why do people in the world bustle and chase endlessly, letting their hearts gallop through arenas of sights, sounds, goods, and profit—gazing far and wide at splendor and seeking every delight of mind and mouth—if not because they think such pleasures are truly fulfilling? Yet who realizes that worldly pleasures have sharp limits, whereas the joy of one's own heart is boundless? Worse still, the very place where external pleasure dwells is also where anxiety hides; whatever flatters the outer body secretly drains the inner mind.

Consider the display of the five brilliant colors: gleaming hues and resplendent patterns—who does not brighten with delight at the sight and marvel in amazement? Yet the very instant the eye fixes upon them, the spirit is already dazzled. How much vital energy does a human being have in one lifetime? If one continually pins one's attention to outward appearances, indulging one's inclinations in the name of aesthetic pleasure, it is impossible not to end up with stagnant breath, depleted spirit, and a tangled mind—until the eyes themselves are blinded to what truly matters. Hence Laozi's verdict, "The five colors blind the eye," is truly a penetrating maxim.

§3
Original (Chinese)

至若丝桐之韵、箫管之声，古圣亦所不废；胡昏庸之子，昵女乐，比歌童，竭一己之精神，取片时之欢乐。究之，曲调未终，铿锵犹在，而耳灵之内蕴者，尽驰于外，而耳反为之聋矣。故曰：「五音令人耳聋」，不诚然哉！他如口之于味，甘旨调和，浓淡适节，圣人亦所必需。无如饕餮者流，贪口腹，好滋味，嘉肴满座，异物充厨，虽一箸数金、一餐万费不辞，其亦知利于口者，不利于心乎？况人心中，有无限至味，不肥脂而自甘，不膏粱而自饱，彼徒资餍饫者，亦只求适口焉耳。故曰：「五味令人口爽」，良非虚矣。

As for the refined resonance of silk strings on paulownia wood and the clear notes of bamboo flutes and pipes, even the sages of antiquity did not discard them. Why, then, do muddled people cling to seductive musicians and singing boys, pouring out their entire vitality to buy a fleeting moment of amusement? Before the tune has even ended, while the clanging percussion and strings still ring in the air, the innate clarity stored within the ear has already bolted outward, leaving the ear numb and deafened. So when Laozi says, "The five tones deafen the ear," is that not utterly true?

The same holds for the mouth and flavor: balanced, wholesome dishes seasoned neither too heavily nor too blandly are something even sages require in daily life. Alas, gluttons like the mythic taotie live for their appetites and crave intense delicacies, loading tables with choice dishes and stuffing kitchens with exotic fare, unbothered if a single lift of the chopsticks costs pieces of gold or a single banquet squanders ten thousand coins. Do they ever stop to ask whether what gratifies the mouth harms the mind? Moreover, within the human heart lies an inexhaustible supreme flavor—sweet without rich fats, deeply satisfying without fine meats and grains. Those who merely stuff themselves seek nothing beyond momentary tickling of the tongue. Thus "The five flavors spoil the palate" is no idle warning.

§4
Original (Chinese)

若夫田猎一事，古帝王原为生民除残去害、乐业安耕起见；后世人民，从禽从兽，于猎于田，专以走狗为事，甚至燎原遍野，纵犬搜山，直使无辜之蛇蝎、昆虫，受害不少。更有逞残暴以伤物命，专杀害以为生涯，毫不隐痛，卒之天道好还，冥刑不贷，一转瞬间，而祸患随之矣。又况驰骋田猎之时，即暴戾性天之时，其身狂，其心亦狂，太上所以有「驰骋田猎，令人心发狂」之戒也。

As for hunting, ancient sovereigns originally undertook it to rid the people of fierce beasts threatening crops and livestock, so that farmers could till and live in peace. In later ages, however, men pursued birds and beasts purely for sport, devoting themselves to coursing hounds—even setting fire to whole plains and unleashing packs across mountainsides, bringing needless slaughter to countless innocent reptiles and insects. Worse still, some give free rein to cruelty, taking animal lives and making killing their livelihood without the slightest pang of compassion. Yet Heaven's Way loves to requite, and unseen retribution shows no leniency: in the blink of an eye, calamity follows close behind. Moreover, the very moment of galloping on the hunt is when one's innate nature turns fierce and unhinged—as the body races wildly, the mind goes wild alongside it. That is why Laozi solemnly warns: "Racing and hunting drive the mind to madness."

§5
Original (Chinese)

再者，异采珍奇，帝王不寓于目，所以风醇俗美，群相安于无事之天；后人以奇异为尚，于是百计经营，千方打算，半生精气，尽销磨于货物之中，讵知己之所羡，人亦羡之，以其羡者而独有诸己，此劫夺之风所由日炽也。古云：「匹夫无罪，怀璧其罪。」是知藏愈厚，祸弥深，洵不诬也。即使急力防闲，多方保护，而神天不佑，终亦必亡而已矣。人生性命为重，一旦魄散魂飞，货财安在？何不重内而轻外耶？太上所以有「难得之货，令人行妨」，谆谆为世告也。

Then again, consider rare curios and exotic treasures. Ancient sage-kings never lingered their gaze on strange novelties; consequently social customs remained wholesome and simple, and all lived safely in tranquil ease. Later generations prized rarity and extravagance, scheming by a hundred devices and calculating a thousand ways until half a lifetime's essence and breath were ground away in pursuit of commodities. Little do they reflect that what they covet, others covet too; hoarding for oneself what everyone envies is precisely what fans the flames of theft and robbery day by day. As the ancient proverb says, "An ordinary man commits no crime, save carrying a jade disk in his coat." Truly, the thicker the hoard, the deeper the disaster—no exaggeration at all. Even if one guards it anxiously and fortifies it on every side, without Heaven's blessing it will inevitably be lost in the end. What matters most in human life is one's own inner nature and existence; once life ends and the souls scatter, where are all those riches? Why not weigh the inner heavily and hold the outer lightly? This is why Laozi earnestly cautions the world: "Goods hard to obtain obstruct one's conduct."

§6
Original (Chinese)

是以有道高人，虚其心，以养性；实其腹，以立命。知先天一气，生则随来，死则随去，为吾身不坏之至宝，一心专注于此，而外来一切，皆视若浮云，所以虚灵不昧，或受人间禋祀，或为天上真宰，至今犹昭然耳目也。试问舜琴牙味、赵璧齐庐，今犹有存焉者乎？早已湮没无闻矣。是知物有尽，而道无尽；人有穷，而道无穷。人欲长生，须将人物之有限者置之，性命之无形者修之，庶知所轻重矣。呜呼！非见大识卓之君子，乌能去彼而取此耶？

For this reason, accomplished masters of the Way empty their minds to nourish inner nature and fill their bellies to establish life. Knowing that the One Breath of Prior Heaven—which accompanies us into birth and goes with us at death—is the one indestructible treasure of our being, they devote their whole heart to it and view every external glitter as passing clouds. Thus their luminous awareness remains clear and undimmed: some are honored with reverent offerings in the human world, others abide as perfected spirits in heaven, shining vividly before eyes and ears to this very day. Ask yourself: Emperor Shun's five-stringed zither, master chef Yi Ya's supreme flavors, the state of Zhao's Jade Disk of He, the famed hunting hounds of Qi—does a single one of them survive today? All vanished long ago without a trace! Clearly, external things have an end while the Way has none; mortal forms reach a limit while the Way is boundless. Whoever seeks longevity must set aside finite possessions and worldly affairs and devote themselves to cultivating the formless realm of nature and life—only then do they grasp what is weighty and what is trivial. Alas, who but a person of grand vision and towering discernment can truly leave "that" behind and choose "this"?

§7
Original (Chinese)

教人修身大旨，原与尘世相反。须知，世人之所好者，道家之所恶；世人之所贪者，道家之所弃。盖声色货利，百般美好，虽有利于人身，究无利于人心。又况人心一贪，人身即不利焉。惟性命一事，似无形无象，不足为人身贵者，若能去其外诱，充其本然，一心修炼，毫不外求，卒之功成德备，长生之道在是矣。天下一切宝贵，孰有过于此乎？但恐立志不坚，进道不勇，理欲杂乘，天人迭起，遂难造于其极。愿后之学者，始则闲邪存诚，继则炼铅伏汞，及至返本还原，抱朴归真，又何难上与仙人为伍耶？是以圣人修内不修外，为腹不为目，去彼存此，于以壹志凝神，尽性立命，岂不高出尘世之荣华万万倍乎？

The grand principle of self-cultivation taught here runs directly counter to worldly fashion. Understand well: what worldly people dote on is what Daoists shun; what worldly people greedily grasp at is what Daoists cast away. For sights, sounds, riches, and profit—however alluring in a hundred ways—may briefly flatter the physical senses yet bring zero benefit to the human heart; worse still, the moment greed takes root in the heart, the body itself suffers swiftly after. Only the cultivation of inner nature and life, seeming formless and intangible as if it offered no worldly prestige to the body, holds the true prize: if one clears away outward enticements, replenishes what is innately whole, and cultivates single-mindedly without seeking anything outside, then merit and virtue reach completion and the Way of longevity is right here. Among all treasures under heaven, what could possibly surpass it?

The only fear is that one's resolve is unsteady and one's advance along the Way lacks courage—heavenly principle and selfish desire jostling together, the mind of the Way and the ordinary human mind rising by turns—so that one falls short of the summit. I hope that students of later ages will begin by "barring waywardness and preserving sincerity," then apply themselves to refining Lead and Mercury (using steady, settled true breath to subdue the restless, outward-darting mind). Once they return to the root and restore the origin, embracing simplicity and returning to truth, what difficulty can there be in taking one's place among the immortals? Therefore the sage cultivates the inner rather than the outer, acts for the belly rather than the eye, discards "that" and preserves "this," unifying will and concentrating spirit to fulfill inner nature and establish life. Does this not tower ten thousand times above all worldly glory and splendor?

Key Terms

  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: described in this chapter as "the indestructible treasure of our being, which comes with us at birth and goes with us at death"—the true referent of "filling the belly to establish life."
  • inner nature (xing) / existence, life (ming): Huang Yuanji summarizes "for the belly, not for the eye" as "emptying the mind to nourish inner nature (xing) and filling the belly to establish life (ming)"—nurturing the mind is cultivating nature, consolidating true breath is establishing life.
  • Lead and Mercury: internal alchemy uses "refining Lead and subduing Mercury" as a metaphor for harmonizing inner yin and yang—using steady, condensed true breath (Lead) to calm and subdue the volatile, outward-chasing mind-spirit (Mercury) so that spirit and breath unite.
  • Spoiling the palate (koushuang): in Classical Chinese, shuang means to err, lose keenness, or spoil; koushuang does not mean "refreshing to the mouth" as in modern speech, but losing sensitivity to true flavor and ruining the appetite.
  • Barring waywardness and preserving sincerity (xianxie cuncheng): from the Wenyan commentary on the Qian hexagram in the Book of Changes; guarding against stray impulses while nurturing wholehearted sincerity, a foundational discipline frequently stressed by Huang Yuanji.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Subtract from sensory and information overload. Today, "the five colors blind the eye and the five tones deafen the ear" looks like endless scrolling, short-form videos, and high-frequency stimulation shredding your attention. Schedule a daily "low-stimulation window" with notifications turned off so your eyes and ears can rest.
  2. Aim for balance in food rather than heavy intensity. Huang Yuanji is remarkably balanced: he notes that "wholesome, well-seasoned dishes in right proportion are needed even by sages," criticizing only obsessive gluttony. Eating simply and moderately actually sharpens your taste buds and leaves your post-meal energy far clearer.
  3. Watch out for the agitation of high-adrenaline thrills. Few modern people gallop after game with hounds, yet hyper-competitive gaming, speculative gambling, and online flame wars provoke the exact same "madness of mind." When you feel adrenaline surging and composure slipping, step back early.
  4. Shift focus from "performing for others' eyes" to "nourishing yourself." "Acting for the eye" means living for outward display and comparison; "acting for the belly" means quietly tending your real vitality and peace of mind. Before buying something or making a choice, ask: is this genuine nourishment, or just chasing a spectacle?

Translator's Notes

  1. "An ordinary man commits no crime, save carrying a jade disk": From the Zuo Commentary (Duke Huan, Year 10), originally referring to Shu of Yu, whose prized jade provoked the greed and aggression of his brother the Duke of Yu; later a proverb meaning that hoarding valuables invites disaster.
  2. Reverent offerings (yinsi): Yin denotes pure, solemn ritual sacrifice—here referring to accomplished sages whose virtue lives on so that later generations honor them in temples.
  3. Shun's zither, Yi Ya's flavors, Zhao's jade disk, and Qi's hounds: Four historical allusions echoing the five tones, five flavors, rare goods, and hunting: Emperor Shun's five-stringed zither singing the Ode of the South Wind; the legendary Spring and Autumn chef Yi Ya of Qi; the priceless Jade Disk of He held by the state of Zhao; and the famed hunting hound "Lu" of Qi celebrated in the Classic of Poetry ("Odes of Qi," Lu ling).
  4. "Principle and desire jostling, heaven and human rising by turns": Standard Song–Ming Neo-Confucian phrasing referring to heavenly principle (tianli) versus selfish desire (renyu), and the mind of the Way versus the ordinary human mind alternating when practice is not yet mature.
  5. Textual collation: Several base-text misprints were corrected in the Chinese source, including 日 → 曰, 驰聘 → 驰骋, 又祝 → 又况, 筲管 → 箫管, 一已 → 一己, 一餮万费不醉 → 一餐万费不辞, 视着 → 视若, 冤无 → 究无, 平之 → 卒之, and 为月 → 为目, and an extraneous character 奥 between paragraphs was removed.
