Chapter 13 · Favor and Disgrace Alike Startle

Worldly favor is an external, later-added vanity; what is truly precious is the Prior Heaven true body—only one who guards the self without selfishness can be entrusted with the world

About This Chapter

On the surface, this chapter seems paradoxical: Laozi says, "The reason I have great affliction is that I have a body," yet in the very next breath declares, "Only one who values the world as his own body can be entrusted with the world; only one who loves the world as his own body can be given care of the world." If having a body is an affliction, why should one value and love it?

Huang Yuanji resolves this apparent knot cleanly by distinguishing between "two bodies":

  1. Favor is an inferior, externally bestowed honor—gaining or losing it alike causes alarm. True nobility dwells natively within one's own life (what Mencius calls "innate nobility," lianggui). Worldly favor and rank are conferred by others and can be revoked at any moment; hence people tremble with anxiety when they receive them and panic even more when they lose them.
  2. Clinging to the Posterior Heaven "mortal body" breeds great affliction; realizing the Prior Heaven "true body" leaves no affliction. When one pamperingly feeds the physical body with fine clothes, food, and fame while scheming for private gain, the more selfishly one clings to it, the faster disaster strikes. Yet before the Way is accomplished, one still depends on this physical frame to cultivate and nurture the One Breath of Prior Heaven. Once one reaches self-forgetting and independent Original Spirit, even while living in the world one is untouched by gain, loss, fortune, or misfortune.
  3. Valuing the self is not selfishness, but responsibility toward all under heaven. Huang Yuanji firmly rejects narrow escapism—hiding away in deep mountains indifferent to human suffering. One who truly knows how to preserve the self is scrupulous in every word and deed; having cultivated oneself first, when the hour arrives one can shoulder great responsibility like Yi Yin or Zhuge Liang. That is the deep meaning of Laozi's "entrusting the world."

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：宠辱若惊，贵大患若身。何谓宠辱若惊？宠为下，得之若惊，失之若惊，是谓宠辱若惊。何谓贵大患若身？吾所以有大患者，为吾有身，及吾无身，吾有何患？故贵以身为天下者，则可以寄于天下；爱以身为天下者，乃可以托天下。

Laozi says: Favor and disgrace alike startle the mind; people prize great affliction just as they prize their own body. What is meant by "favor and disgrace alike startle"? Worldly favor is an inferior thing—receiving it startles one with anxious fear, and losing it startles one with dismay; this is called being startled by favor and disgrace alike. What is meant by "prizing great affliction as one's own body"? The very reason I suffer great affliction is that I cling to having this body; once I am free of this selfish attachment to a body, what affliction can I have? Therefore, one who treats the world with the same reverent care he gives his own body may be entrusted with the world; one who loves the world as he loves his own body may be given stewardship of the world.

§2
Original (Chinese)

孟子曰：「守孰为大？守身为大。」《诗》曰：「既明且哲，以保其身。」古人于身，亦何重哉？夫以此身也，不但自家性命依之而存，即一家之内，无不赖之以生。推而言之，「为天地立心，为万物立命，为往圣继绝学，为万世开太平」，无非此身为之主宰。虽然，主宰宇宙者此身，而主宰此身者惟道。道不能凭空而独立，必赖人以承之。故曰：「身存则道存，身亡则道亡。」

Mencius asked: "Of all things to guard, which is greatest? Guarding one's own person is greatest." The Classic of Poetry likewise says: "Clear-sighted and wise, thereby preserving his person." How greatly did the ancients honor this body! For not only does one's own inner nature and life depend on it for existence—every member of one's household relies on it for livelihood as well. Extending this outward, "to establish a heart for heaven and earth, to establish destiny for the myriad things, to carry forward the lost learning of past sages, and to open peace for ten thousand generations"—all of these rely on this human body as their active steward. Still, while this body presides over the affairs of the cosmos, what presides over this body is the Way alone. The Way cannot stand suspended in empty air; it requires human beings to bear and embody it. Hence the saying: "While the body endures, the Way endures; when the body perishes, the Way perishes."

§3
Original (Chinese)

大修行人，当大道未成之时，身远尘世，迹遁山林，韬光养晦，乐道安贫，耳不闻人声，口不谈时世，是不履红尘，岂徒避祸以全身哉？亦欲安身以立命也。至于人世荣宠之事，耻辱之端，皆视为平常故事，毫不足介意者然。虽无端而马旌下逮，币聘来临，君相隆非常之遇，蓬荜增盖代之辉，人所喜欲狂者，己则淡弥甚也。倘不幸而闻望过隆，戮辱旋及，奸邪肆谗谤之口，身家蒙不白之冤，亦惟不诿罪于人，归咎于己而已。古圣人，居宠不灭性，受辱不亡身，良有以也。要皆明于保身之道，不以功名富贵养其身，而以仁义道德修其性，所以成万年不坏之躯，为古今所倚赖也。

Before a great cultivator has accomplished the Great Way, he withdraws from worldly clamor and conceals his tracks in the mountains and forests, sheathing his brilliance, resting content in poverty while delighting in the Way, letting his ears hear no vulgar chatter and his mouth speak nothing of current affairs. Stepping back from the red dust in this manner is hardly done merely to dodge trouble and keep his skin safe—it is to settle his body and firmly establish his life. As for worldly glory and favor, or occasions of disgrace and humiliation, he treats them all as ordinary passing tales, not worth a moment's agitation. Even if imperial chariots and banners arrive unannounced with lavish gifts to summon him, ruler and ministers extending extraordinary honors that make his humble cottage shine with unmatched luster—what would drive ordinary people wild with delight only makes his heart grow quieter and more detached. Conversely, if excessive renown unfortunately invites envy so that disgrace and punishment swiftly descend, slanderers wagging malicious tongues until he and his family suffer unredressed injustice, he still refuses to blame others, turning quietly inward to examine himself.

Ancient sages remained uncorrupted in inner nature amid favor and unbroken in spirit amid disgrace for this exact reason. In the end, they all understood the true Way of preserving the body: never pampering it with rank, wealth, and glory, but cultivating inner nature with humaneness, righteousness, the Way, and Virtue—thus forging an indestructible frame enduring ten thousand years, a pillar on which both past and present can lean.

§4
Original (Chinese)

倘一有其身，自私自重，与人争名争利，为己谋食谋衣，逐逐营营，扰扰纷纷，争竞不息，攘夺无休，不旋踵而祸患随之矣。君子所以贵藏器以待时，安身以崇德也。太上见人不能居宠思畏，弭患无形，所以有「宠辱若惊」、「贵大患若身」之慨。何谓「宠辱若惊」？盖以宠为后起之荣，非本来之贵，故曰「宠为下」。但常人之情，营营于得失，故得之若惊，失之若惊，是为「宠辱若惊」。

On the other hand, the moment someone clings possessively to "this body," growing selfish and self-important—vying with others for fame and profit, scheming endlessly for his own food and clothing, scurrying in agitation, competing without cease, and grabbing without end—before he can even turn on his heel, disaster overtakes him. That is why the noble person values "keeping one's vessel concealed while awaiting the right time, and settling one's person to elevate virtue." Seeing that people fail to stay vigilant in times of favor and dissolve calamity before it takes form, Laozi utters his lament: "Favor and disgrace alike startle; people prize great affliction as their own body."

Why does he say "favor and disgrace alike startle"? Because worldly favor is an extrinsic honor tacked on later, not one's innate dignity; hence "favor is inferior." Yet ordinary human feelings churn relentlessly over gain and loss: receiving favor startles them with fear of losing it, and losing it startles them with bitter shock—that is "favor and disgrace alike startle."

§5
Original (Chinese)

其曰「贵大患若身」者何？殆谓人因有身，所以有患，若吾无身，患从何来？凡人当道未成时，不得不留身，以为修炼之具，一到脱壳飞升，有神无身，何祸之可加哉？即留形住世，万缘顿灭，一真内含，虽云游四境，亦来去自如，又何大患之有？世之修士，欲成千万年之神，为千万人之望，造非常之业，建不朽之功，须一言一行，不稍放肆，即贵其身而身存，乃可为天下所寄命者；一动一静，毫不敢轻，即爱其身而身在，乃可为天下所托赖者。如莘野久耕，而三聘抒忱，慨然以尧舜君民自任；南阳高卧，而几经束帛，俨然以鼎足三分为能。所谓「托六尺之孤，寄百里之命」者，非斯人，其谁与归？彼自私其身而高蹈远引，不思以道济天下，使天下共游于大道之中者，相去亦远矣。

And why does he say "prize great affliction as one's own body"? It means that people suffer affliction precisely because they cling to having a body; were I free of body-clinging, where could affliction find a foothold? While the Way is not yet perfected, mortal cultivators have no choice but to preserve this physical frame as their instrument of practice. Once one sheds the shell and ascends—possessing pure Spirit without attachment to a body—what calamity could possibly touch one? Even if one chooses to remain in the world in physical form, all worldly entanglements are stilled at once while the single truth abides within; roaming freely across the four quarters, coming and going at ease, what great affliction could remain?

Cultivators in the world who aspire to perfect an undying spirit, earn the trust of millions, forge extraordinary undertakings, and build imperishable merit must never allow the slightest carelessness in any word or deed: that is truly "valuing one's body" so that it endures intact, making one worthy to have the world's destiny entrusted to them. They dare not show the slightest rashness in movement or stillness: that is truly "loving one's body" so that it remains sound, making one worthy to bear the world's reliance. Look at Yi Yin, who plowed quietly in the fields of Shen until King Tang summoned him three times with gifts, whereupon he poured out his loyalty and took it upon himself to make his ruler a Yao or Shun and uplift the people; or Zhuge Liang, resting calmly in Nanyang until repeated respectful invitations drew him forth to craft the tripartite balance of the realm. As Confucius said, "One to whom an orphaned child of six spans may be entrusted, and the fate of a hundred-li state committed"—if not to such a cultivated person, to whom else could it belong? Compare that with those who selfishly guard their own skins and flee far from the world, never thinking of aiding humanity with the Way so that all may roam together within the Great Way—the gulf between them is vast indeed!

§6
Original (Chinese)

此言人身自有良贵，不待外求，有非势位之荣可比者。人能从此修持，努力不懈，古云「辛苦二三载，快乐千万年」，洵不诬矣。有何宠辱之惊，贵患之慨耶？学者大道未得时，必赖此身以为修炼。若区区以衣服饮食、富贵荣华，为养身之要，则凡身既重，而先天真身未有不因之而损者。先天真身既损，而后天凡身，亦断难久存焉。此凡夫之所以爱其身，而竟丧其身也。惟至人，知一切事物，皆属幻化之端，有生灭相，不可认以为真。惟我先天元气，才是我生生之本，可以一世，可以千万年。若无此个真修，则凡身从何而有？此为人身内之身，存之则生，失之则死，散之为物，凝之为仙，不可一息偶离者也。太上教人，兢兢致慎，不敢一事怠忽，不敢一念游移，更不敢与人争强角胜，惟恬淡自适，清净无尘，以自适其天而已。虽未出身加民，而芸芸赤子，早已庆安全于方寸。斯人不出，如苍生何？民之仰望者，深且切矣。所谓「不以一己之乐为乐，而以天下之乐为乐；不以一己之忧为忧，而以天下之忧为忧」，其寄托为何如哉！

This chapter declares that every human being already possesses an innate nobility within, needing no search outside and incomparable to the fleeting glory of power and rank. If one cultivates steadily from this foundation without slacking, the ancient saying—"Two or three years of earnest toil, ten thousand years of joy"—is truly no falsehood. What room then remains for alarm over favor and disgrace, or sighs over affliction and the body?

Before attaining the Great Way, a student must rely on this physical body to practice. Yet if one treats fine clothes, lavish food, wealth, and status as the essence of nourishing the body, then the heavier one indulges the mortal frame, the more surely the Prior Heaven true body is damaged; and once the Prior Heaven true body is injured, the Posterior Heaven mortal frame can never last long either. That is how ordinary people claim to love their bodies yet end up destroying them. Only the perfected sage understands that all external things are shifting illusions marked by arising and passing away, never to be mistaken for reality. Only my own Prior Heaven Original Breath (One Breath of Prior Heaven) is the ceaseless root of life, capable of sustaining one lifetime and extending across myriad years. Without this true root, whence would even the mortal body arise? This is "the true body within the body": preserve it and you live, lose it and you die; scattered, it sinks into ordinary matter; condensed, it becomes an immortal—not for a single breath may one stray from it.

Therefore Laozi teaches people to live with vigilant care—never neglecting a single task, never letting a single thought drift recklessly, and still less vying with others for dominance—resting instead in quiet simplicity and stainless purity, naturally at ease with Heaven within. Even before such a person steps out into public office, the countless common people already find peace and shelter within his Square Inch heart. As people in the Eastern Jin said of Xie An, "If such a man will not step forth, what will become of the common people?"—so deep and earnest is humanity's longing for him. As Fan Zhongyan wrote, "Not taking one's private joy as joy, but taking the world's joy as joy; not taking one's private sorrow as sorrow, but taking the world's sorrow as sorrow"—how weighty and noble is the trust placed in such a one!

Key Terms

  • inner nature (xing) / existence, life (ming): this chapter emphasizes "settling the body to establish life" and "cultivating inner nature through humaneness, righteousness, the Way, and Virtue"—employing the physical body as a vessel for cultivation rather than being enslaved by its cravings.
  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: called here "Prior Heaven Original Breath" and "the body within the body," identified as the ceaseless root of life—preserving it brings life, losing it brings death.
  • Square Inch (fangcun): the inner spiritual seat of the mind-heart; used here in "the countless people already find safety within his Square Inch" to describe the sage's vast compassion for the world.
  • Innate nobility (lianggui): from Mencius ("Gaozi I"), referring to the inherent moral dignity (humaneness, righteousness, loyalty, trustworthiness) present within every person, contrasted with external ranks and favors that others can give or take away.
  • Mortal body and true body (fanshen and zhenshen): Huang Yuanji's core distinction in interpreting "prizing great affliction as one's body"—the selfish physical ego obsessed with comforts and prestige is the "mortal body," while the life-source condensed from Prior Heaven Original Breath and Original Spirit is the "true body."

Putting It into Practice

  1. Anchor your self-worth in what you cultivate, not in others' applause. Floating on air when praised and collapsing when overlooked is textbook "being startled by favor and disgrace alike." Bring your attention back to daily character and solid work, and outside opinions lose their power to shake you.
  2. True self-care means moderation, not pampering. Overeating, staying up late, and chasing status masquerade as "enjoying life," yet are precisely what Huang Yuanji calls "loving one's body only to ruin it." Regular rhythms and an even temper are the best nourishment you can give yourself.
  3. Refuse pointless ego battles. "Never vying with others for dominance" is not weakness; it conserves finite vitality and mental bandwidth for endeavors that genuinely matter.
  4. Master your own words and deeds before taking charge of others. Whether in a family or an organization, the person everyone naturally trusts is the one who stays emotionally steady, exercises quiet self-restraint, and does not put private advantage first.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Of all things to guard, which is greatest? Guarding one's own person is greatest": Quoted from Mencius ("Lilou I"), indicating that preserving personal integrity is the foundation of all moral duties.
  2. "Clear-sighted and wise, thereby preserving his person": From the Classic of Poetry ("Major Odes," Zhengmin), praising Zhong Shanfu's lucid wisdom and self-preservation.
  3. "To establish a heart for heaven and earth... open peace for ten thousand generations": Famous four-line motto of the Northern Song Neo-Confucian Zhang Zai (the standard wording has "establish destiny for the living people," which Huang Yuanji paraphrases as "for the myriad things").
  4. Plowing in Shen and resting in Nanyang: The first alludes to Yi Yin, famed minister of the Shang dynasty, who farmed in the fields of Shen until King Tang invited him three times; the second alludes to Zhuge Liang of the Three Kingdoms period, who lived quietly at Longzhong in Nanyang until Liu Bei visited his thatched cottage three times.
  5. "Entrusted with a six-span orphan and the fate of a hundred-li state": From the Analects ("Taibo"), where Zengzi praises the steadfast noble person to whom a young heir and a state's survival may safely be committed.
  6. "If such a man will not step forth, what will become of the common people?": Adapting a famous remark in Shishuo xinyu and the Book of Jin ("Biography of Xie An") praising the Eastern Jin statesman Xie An during his retirement in the Eastern Mountains.
  7. Textual collation: Numerous base-text misprints were corrected in the Chinese source, including 日/白 → 曰, 惠 → 患 across five occurrences, 稻光 → 韬光, 蓬毕 → 蓬荜, 不自之冤 → 不白之冤, 已 → 己, 争竟 → 争竞, 后起之菜 → 后起之荣, 脱亮 → 脱壳, 干万年 → 千万年, 亮舜 → 尧舜, 良责 → 良贵, 洵不还矣 → 洵不诬矣, 几身 → 凡身, 虽来 → 虽未, and 为何如裁 → 为何如哉, plus a closing period added to the classic text.
