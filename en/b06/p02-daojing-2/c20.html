Chapter 20 · Alone and Different from Others

Ending contrived learning to live without anxiety is no barren escapism, but drawing back the spirit's light into primordial chaos to nurture the Mysterious Pass; while the crowd chases outward, the sage alone returns to Prior Heaven and feeds from the Mother

About This Chapter

This is the most deeply personal, lyrical chapter in the Daodejing. Setting aside the cool voice of the detached philosopher, Laozi speaks in the intimate first person "I": everyone else rejoices as if feasting at a grand banquet or climbing a spring terrace, while I alone stay still and unformed like an infant who has not yet learned to laugh; everyone else is bright, sharp, and calculating, while I alone seem dim, dull, stubborn, and rustic—"I alone am different from others, prizing sustenance from the Mother."

Huang Yuanji illuminates this profound solitude and quiet sufficiency as the highest bearing of internal alchemy:

  1. "Ending contrived learning, free of anxiety" is not ignorance, but a single-thought turn from ordinary to sage. Hearing "ending learning," many assume it lies impossibly far out of reach and shrink back. Huang Yuanji points out that between respectful wei and careless e, between good and evil, between sage and fool, lies only one thought of reverence versus slackness. Draw back the spirit's light and return to undivided chaos, and one naturally "delights in Heaven, knows destiny, and has no anxiety."
  2. "Dim and dull" is never dead, barren emptiness, but "hiding sharp clarity inside warm wholeness." The sage appears plain and blurred outwardly precisely because he is nurturing the undivided Supreme Ultimate breath within, like an unhatched egg—not fleeing into a cave indifferent to the world. On the contrary, because an unshakable master abides within, his influence in the world brings the people the very joy of "feasting at a grand banquet and climbing a spring terrace."
  3. "Prizing sustenance from the Mother" unveils the master key of reversing Posterior Heaven back to Prior Heaven. Posterior Heaven thoughts and feelings are the "child breath"; Prior Heaven innate nature is the "Mother breath." Ordinary people chase outward and scatter their root; the sage, like a small child clinging lovingly to its mother, gathers scattered feelings back into the Prior Heaven Mother breath—the core method of nurturing the Mysterious Pass and recovering the lost mind.

Safety note: Alchemical descriptions in this chapter—such as "drawing back the spirit's light," "a single sphere of Original Breath rising and descending," and "completing the nine-cycle Elixir"—depict natural states arising in deep relaxation and effortless stillness. Never force your breathing or strain to focus rigidly on any bodily spot. In everyday practice, simply emphasize "sheathing sharp edges and dropping petty calculation" so your nervous system can genuinely rest.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：绝学无忧。惟之与阿，相去几何？善之与恶，相去何若？人之所畏，不可不畏。荒兮其未央哉！众人熙熙，如享太牢，如登春台，我独泊兮其未兆，如婴儿之未孩（是未离母腹时）。乘乘兮（是任天而动）若无归（是不著迹）。众人皆有余，我独若遗。我愚人之心也哉，沌沌兮。俗人昭昭，我独昏昏；俗人察察，我独闷闷。澹兮（谓无欲于外）其若晦，漂兮（谓不泥于形）若无所止。众人皆有以，我独顽且鄙。我独异于人，而贵求食于母。

Laozi says: Put an end to contrived learning and there will be no anxiety. Between a respectful "yes" (wei) and a casual "yeah" (e), how much difference is there? Between good and evil, how far apart do they begin? What others dread (or shrink back from), one must not fail to hold in awe. How vast and boundless is this expanse—it has not yet reached its middle!

The crowd bustles in merry excitement, as if enjoying a grand sacrificial feast or climbing a high terrace in spring; I alone remain tranquil without a single stirring sign, like a newborn infant who has not yet learned to smile (Huang Yuanji's note: while still in the mother's womb). Drifting effortlessly along (note: moving purely with Heaven), as if having no fixed home to return to (note: leaving no clinging trace). Everyone else seems to have more than enough, while I alone seem left with nothing. Surely I have the mind of a simpleton—so muddled and undivided! Worldly people are bright and sparkling; I alone am dim and shadowed. Worldly people are sharp and exacting; I alone am blunt and unhurried. Placid (note: without craving for outward things) like deep twilight; drifting freely (note: unattached to physical form) as if with nowhere to stop. Everyone else has clever means and purpose, while I alone seem dull and rustic. Thus I alone differ from other people, prizing above all drawing sustenance from the Mother.

§2
Original (Chinese)

圣人造诣极高，称为绝学，纯是一腔生意，融融泄泄，无虑无思。《诗》曰：「上帝临汝，毋贰尔心。」以故素位而行，一任穷通得丧，无入而不自得，故曰「无忧」。此等境界，以常人不学无术者较之，殆不啻天渊之别。然亦所隔不远焉，如应声然，同一应也，惟者之直与阿者之谀，感犹是也，而所以应者，相去究竟有几何哉？自古圣凡之分，不过善恶，而善恶之别，只在敬肆，所争仅一念之间耳，又相去何若哉？人能尘根悉拔，色相俱空，自有真乐，不待外求，又何忧之有？虽然，无忧之诣，惟圣能之，凡人之所畏而却步者也。有志圣学者，切不可视之为难，而畏人之所畏也。古仙云：「绝学无为闲道人，不求妄念不求真。」《易》曰：「乐天知命故不忧。」只在还于虚寂，纯任自然，适己之天，复己之命而已矣，又何足畏之有耶哉？

The sage's attainment rises to the highest peak—transcending all contrived learning, hence called "ending learning"—filled solely with a warm surge of living vitality, harmonious and at ease, untouched by anxious thought. As the Classic of Poetry says: "High Heaven looks down upon you; harbor no divided heart." Therefore the sage acts faithfully in whatever station he occupies, letting poverty or success, gain or loss come as they may, finding quiet ease wherever he enters—hence "no anxiety."

Compared with an untutored, drifting ordinary person, such a realm seems separated by the gulf between sky and abyss. Yet in truth, the separation is not far at all! Think of answering someone's call: both are simply an answer—how much real distance separates the straightforward, respectful wei from the flattering, casual e when both respond to the exact same call? Since antiquity, the divide between sage and ordinary person has been nothing more than good versus evil, and the divide between good and evil lies solely in reverence versus carelessness—a margin of a single thought! How far apart is that really? Once a person pulls up the roots of worldly craving and sees through illusory appearances, genuine joy wells up naturally from within without seeking outside—what anxiety could possibly remain?

Even so, because this anxiety-free state is achieved only by sages, ordinary people regard it with dread and stop short before it. Anyone aspiring to the learning of the sages must never treat it as unreachable or join the crowd in shrinking back! As the ancient master sang: "The carefree Daoist of no-learning and non-action neither chases deluded thoughts nor grasps at truth." And the Book of Changes says: "Delighting in Heaven and knowing destiny, therefore free of anxiety." All it takes is returning to silent emptiness, trusting pure naturalness, harmonizing with one's Heaven, and restoring one's life (inner nature and life)—what is there in that to fear?

§3
Original (Chinese)

但下手之初，务须收敛神光，一归混沌，于动于静，处常处变，俱如洪荒之世，天地未辟，浩浩荡荡，不啻夜之未央。如此，则中有所主，外物不扰，于以施之事为，措诸政令，自然众人化之。熙熙然，食圣人之德者，如享太牢之荣；游圣人之宇者，如登春台之乐，此岂孤修寂静可比其性量哉？所以，「功满天下而不知功，行满天下而不知行」。众人所喜，我独淡泊恬静，渺无朕兆，如婴儿初胎、孩子未成之时，一团元气浑然在抱，上下升降，运行不息，适与天地流通，杳不知其归宿矣。人有为而我无为，是众人有余地以自容，我竟遗世而独立，迥非众人所能知、所能及也。自人视之，鲜不谓为愚；返而观之，惟觉洗心退藏于密，安其天，定其命，此岂愚人之心哉？不过大智若愚，大巧若拙焉耳。不然，何以使人乐业安居，如此之感而神，化而速也？

At the very outset of practice, however, one must draw back the scattered Light of spirit and return completely to primordial chaos. Whether moving or still, in ordinary days or sudden upheaval, let the heart remain like the primal wilderness before heaven and earth divided—vast and boundless, like a deep night far from dawn. Once settled thus, an unshakable master abides at the center and outward turmoil cannot disturb it. Extending that steadiness into practical affairs and governance, the people are naturally transformed: living happily, nourished by the sage's virtue as though feasting at a grand banquet, dwelling in his shelter as though climbing a bright spring terrace. How could solitary, dried-up quietism ever compare with a heart of such scope? Hence it is said: "Merit fills the world yet he claims no merit; good deeds fill the world yet he notices no deeds."

Amid everything the crowd eagerly chases, I alone remain tranquil and unruffled, without a trace of restless impulse—like a newly conceived infant before even forming a smile, cradling a single undivided sphere of One Breath of Prior Heaven that rises and descends naturally without ceasing, flowing in unison with heaven and earth and vanishing into boundless depths without a fixed harbor. While others strive with restless effort, I rest in non-action; everyone else seems richly supplied with worldly footing, while I stand apart as though lacking everything—wholly beyond the crowd's comprehension or reach. From outside, scarcely anyone fails to think me a fool; yet looking inward, I simply wash the mind clean, withdraw into hidden stillness, rest in my Heaven, and steady my life. Is this really a fool's heart? Merely supreme wisdom looking simple, supreme skill looking clumsy! Otherwise, how could one bring the people peace and contentment with such quiet speed and wonder?

§4
Original (Chinese)

若此者，皆由太极一团，浑沦在抱，沌沌兮如鸡子之未雏，无从见为阴阳，亦且毫无知识。俗人则昭昭然无事不详，我独昏昏然一无所识；俗人则察察然无事不晓，我独闷闷然一无所明。岂真昏而无知、闷而无觉哉？殆晦迹韬光，寓精明于浑厚，日增月益，丹成九转，德极圣人，而成万古不磨之仙也。其大而化也，若天地之晦蒙，万象咸包念内；其妙而神也，若行云流水之无止所，群生悉毓个中。由其外而观之，众人皆有用于世，我独愚顽而鄙陋；就其中而言，道则高矣、美矣，为超群拔萃，绝世特立之圣人，此所由独异于人，而为人不可及也。盖凡人纷驰于外，失其本来之天，圣人涵养于中，保其固有之性。圣异于凡，皆由后天以返先天故耳。夫后天为情，子气也；先天为性，母气也。由情以归性，一如子之恋母，依依不舍，故曰「贵求食于母」。孟子云：「学问之道无他，求其放心而已矣。」圣狂之分，只在一念，道岂远乎哉？术岂多乎哉？人欲修道，不于冲漠无朕之际求之，又从何处用功？故曰：「玄牝玄牝真玄牝，不在心兮不在肾。穷取生身受气初，莫怪天机都泄尽。」生身之初，究何有乎？于此思之，道过半矣。

All of this comes from cradling an undivided sphere of the Supreme Ultimate within—blurred and whole like an egg before the chick hatches, where yin and yang cannot yet be pried apart and conceptual cleverness has not yet sprouted. Worldly people shine brightly, knowing every trivial gossip; I alone appear dim and uninformed. Worldly people scrutinize sharply, missing no small advantage; I alone appear blunt and foggy. Is the sage genuinely muddled and numb? Not in the least! He simply conceals his tracks and sheathes his brilliance, tucking crystalline clarity inside warm, unassuming simplicity. Growing silently day by day and month by month, the Elixir completes its nine cycles, virtue reaches sagely fullness, and one becomes an immortal unmarred through endless ages.

When his greatness transforms everything around him, it resembles the deep dusk of heaven and earth holding all myriad images within a single quiet thought; when his subtlety acts freely, it flows like drifting clouds and running water with no stuck stopping place, nourishing all living creatures within. Viewed from outside, everyone else looks clever and useful in society while I look stubborn and rustic; viewed from within, the Way here is lofty and radiant—a peerless sage standing clear above the crowd. That is why I differ from others and remain beyond ordinary reach.

Ultimately, ordinary people gallop outward in every direction and lose their native Heaven, whereas sages nurture the center and safeguard their innate nature. Everything setting sage apart from ordinary person comes down to reversing Prior Heaven / Posterior Heaven—returning from Posterior Heaven to Prior Heaven. Posterior Heaven emotion and thought are the derived "child breath"; Prior Heaven innate nature is the originating "Mother breath." Gathering restless feelings back into innate nature is just like a child clinging affectionately to its mother, unwilling to part—hence "prizing sustenance from the Mother." As Mencius declared: "The whole Way of learning is nothing else—simply recovering the mind that has run away!"

Sagehood or folly hangs on a single thought: how could the Way be far away, or require a maze of complicated techniques? If one seeks to cultivate the Way yet fails to look in that blank, signless depth before thought stirs, where else could one possibly apply effort? Hence the classic alchemical verse: "Mysterious Female, Mysterious Female, the true Mysterious Female—neither in the physical heart nor in the physical kidneys! Trace all the way back to the very first instant your body received breath—don't blame me for leaking Heaven's entire secret!" Reflect quietly: at that primal instant when life first received breath, what was there? Ponder this deeply, and you are already more than halfway to the Way!

§5
Original (Chinese)

首言圣人绝学，已得常乐我静，并无忧虑，日用行习，一归混沌之天，不彫不琢，无染无尘，所谓「仰之弥高」，令人无从测度，真有可望而不可及者。顾功虽如此之极，究其相隔，不过一念敬肆之分。人可畏其高深莫测，而却步不前耶？颜子谓：「舜何人？予何人？有为者，亦若是」洵不诬也。然却非等顽空之学，了无事功表见于世。圣人自明德，以至新民，使群生食德饮和，嬉游于光天化日。斯道也，何道也？至诚尽己性、人性、物性之道也。噫！尽性至此，复何学哉？不过食母之气而已。

This chapter opens by showing that the sage who has gone beyond contrived learning has already attained constant joy, true selfhood, and stillness, utterly free of anxiety. Every ordinary word and daily habit returns to uncarved primordial wholeness, stainless and untainted—just as Yan Hui praised Confucius: "The higher I look up, the loftier it rises," leaving ordinary observers unable to fathom it, seemingly glimpsed from afar yet unreachable.

Yet lofty as this attainment is, when traced to its root, the whole distance separating us from it is merely one thought of reverence versus carelessness. Why then shrink back in fear as though it were impossibly remote? Yan Hui's bold words—"Who was Shun? Who am I? Whoever exerts himself earnestly can become just like him!"—are utterly true.

At the same time, note well that this is no dead, barren "stubborn emptiness" devoid of service to the world. Starting from clarifying his own bright virtue and extending outward to renew the people, the sage lets all beings feast on virtue, drink in harmony, and live joyfully under open skies. What Way is this? It is the Doctrine of the Mean's Way of utmost sincerity: fulfilling one's own nature, fulfilling the nature of others, and fulfilling the nature of all creatures. Alas! When inner nature is fulfilled to this degree, what further contrived "learning" could one need? Nothing save feeding quietly from the Mother's breath!

§6
Original (Chinese)

他注云：绝学是圣学，断绝之时，别无他忧，惟是非得失之间，有应答而无问难，为可惧耳。惟者未必即阿，而相去正自不远；善恶原是各异，而辨别介于几希，此人所宜戒惧者，不可不知。又云：本文是「不可不畏」，此连二「畏」字，有错。「未央」以下，言修道人要混混沌沌，方得玄关一窍，故人皆智而我独愚，人皆明而我独暗，正养此玄关一窍。「无极之真，二五之精」，正吾人受气之本，是为母气，又曰一粒阳丹，号为母气，人得食之，可以长生。此讲亦是。

Another commentary explains the opening differently: "ending learning" refers to an era when the sages' learning is cut off; in such a time, there is no greater dread than seeing everyone nod along in hollow compliance over right and wrong, gain and loss, without anyone daring to question or examine. A respectful "yes" (wei) is not immediately a servile "yeah" (e), yet the step between them is dangerously short; good and evil are worlds apart in essence, yet the line dividing them at the start is hair-thin—this is what people truly ought to guard against with awe. (That commentator also remarks that the line "What people fear, one must not fail to fear" repeats the character wei twice and may contain a scribal slip.)

From "It has not yet reached its middle!" onward, that commentary continues, Laozi teaches that cultivators must abide in undivided chaos in order to realize the single aperture of the Mysterious Pass; hence while others display cleverness I remain simple, and while others flash brilliance I stay dim—all precisely to nurture this Mysterious Pass. What Zhou Dunyi's Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate calls "the truth of the Boundless and the essence of the Two and Five (yin-yang and the Five Phases)" is the very origin from which we received breath: that is the "Mother breath," also called a single pill of the Yang Elixir—feeding upon it grants longevity. This interpretation is sound and perceptive too.

Key Terms

  • Mysterious Pass (xuanguan): both Huang Yuanji and the additional commentary he cites point out that Laozi's undivided, unassuming state ("others are clever while I am simple; others are bright while I am dim") serves directly to nurture and open the Mysterious Pass.
  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: referred to here as "a sphere of Original Breath," "Mother breath," and "the truth of the Boundless"—the undivided vitality of the "infant not yet smiling," the very source on which "feeding from the Mother" relies.
  • Prior Heaven / Posterior Heaven: this chapter pairs "Mother breath" (Prior Heaven innate nature) with "child breath" (Posterior Heaven feelings and discursive thought), identifying the difference between sage and ordinary person as reversing Posterior Heaven back to Prior Heaven and returning feelings to nature.
  • inner nature (xing) / existence, life (ming): spoke of here as "harmonizing with one's Heaven and restoring one's life" and "fulfilling one's own nature, others' nature, and all creatures' nature"—confirming that anxiety-free ease is active fulfillment, never passive escape.
  • Light (guang): this chapter instructs beginners first to "draw back the spirit's light and return to primordial chaos," gathering outward-chasing eyes and attention back within so vitality is not drained by external spectacles.
  • Prizing sustenance from the Mother (gui qiu shi yu mu): Laozi's closing summary of how he differs from the crowd; Huang Yuanji explains it as Posterior Heaven emotion ("child breath") turning affectionately homeward to Prior Heaven innate breath ("Mother breath") like a child cleaving to its mother.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Let go of hyper-alert, nitpicking cleverness. Modern exhaustion often comes from feeling you must see through every angle, stay on top of every detail, and win every small point ("worldly people are bright and exacting"). Intentionally sheath some of that sharpness and allow a little warm, unhurried "bluntness" in daily life—your vitality stops leaking immediately.
  2. Keep an unruffled center amid the crowd's frenzy. When everyone around you chases trends, hypes, parties, or panics ("the crowd bustles in excitement"), you need neither force yourself to join in nor look down on them with pride—simply keep your own quiet, unhurried pace so the emotional tide does not sweep you away.
  3. Make "recovering the stray mind" a daily reset. After a busy day, attention lies scattered across a hundred external worries and comparisons ("child breath running abroad"). Before sleep or at midday, close your eyes for a few quiet minutes and gather your attention home into easy breathing—that is the simplest everyday "feeding from the Mother."
  4. True detachment never shuts out engagement with the world. Do not misread Laozi's "foolish," "stubborn," and "rustic" as passive withdrawal. As Huang Yuanji insists, this is never "dead emptiness": the quieter and more whole you are inside, the steadier you act and the more spring-like ease you bring to everyone around you.

Translator's Notes

  1. "High Heaven looks down upon you; harbor no divided heart": From the Classic of Poetry ("Major Odes," Daming), originally encouraging unwavering resolve, cited here for the sage's undivided sincerity.
  2. "Act faithfully in one's station... at ease wherever one enters": From the Doctrine of the Mean (Zhongyong), describing quiet equanimity across both favorable and adverse circumstances.
  3. "The carefree Daoist of no-learning and non-action...": Adapting the famous opening lines of Tang Chan master Yongjia Xuanjue's Song of Enlightenment (Zhengdao ge): "Have you not seen the carefree Daoist of no-learning and non-action, who neither eliminates deluded thoughts nor seeks truth?"
  4. "Delighting in Heaven and knowing destiny, therefore free of anxiety": From the Appended Remarks (Xici shang) of the Book of Changes.
  5. "Placid like deep twilight; drifting freely as if with nowhere to stop": Standard Wang Bi editions read "placid like the sea; blowing like wind without pause" (ruo hai, liao xi), whereas Huang Yuanji's base text reads "like deep twilight" (ruo hui) and "drifting" (piao xi), which he develops in §4 as "like the deep dusk of heaven and earth" and "like drifting clouds and running water."
  6. "The whole Way of learning is nothing else—simply recovering the mind that has run away": From Mencius ("Gaozi I").
  7. "Mysterious Female, Mysterious Female, the true Mysterious Female...": A widely circulated Song–Yuan internal alchemy quatrain (often attributed to Bai Yuchan or Zhang Sanfeng), stressing that the Mysterious Pass is not an anatomical organ but the primal breath at the inception of life.
  8. Constant joy, true selfhood, and stillness (chang le wo jing): Adapting the four Buddhist virtues of nirvana—permanence, joy, true self, and purity (jing 净)—frequently written in Daoist commentaries with the homophone "stillness" (jing 静) to underscore tranquil freedom from worry.
  9. "Who was Shun? Who am I? Whoever exerts himself can become just like him": Yan Hui's words quoted in Mencius ("Teng Wengong I"), affirming that sagehood is open to anyone who practices earnestly.
  10. "The truth of the Boundless and the essence of the Two and Five": From Northern Song philosopher Zhou Dunyi's Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate (Taiji tushuo), where "Two and Five" signify yin-yang and the Five Phases.
  11. Textual collation: Several base-text misprints were corrected in the Chinese source, including 日 → 曰, 太军/春合 → 太牢/春台 (matching §3 below), 谵兮 → 澹兮 (matching the self-gloss "without craving"), 无人 → 无入, an extraneous comma removed from 畏人之所畏也, 安念 → 妄念, 已 → 己 in two places, 香不知 → 杳不知, 你之弥高 → 仰之弥高, and 相腐 → 相隔.
