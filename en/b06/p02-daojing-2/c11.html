II · Classic of the Way (Ch. 11–20) · 道经

Chapter 11 · The Usefulness of Non-Being

A wheel hub, clay vessel, and room all function through their empty space; internal alchemy uses tangible yin and yang as instruments, returning in the end to the empty One Breath of Prior Heaven

About This Chapter

This chapter offers one of the most famous everyday analogies in the Daodejing: the hollow hub at the center of a chariot wheel, the empty cavity inside a clay vessel, and the open space framed by the doors, windows, and walls of a house. What actually makes each of them work is precisely the unseen "emptiness" within.

Reading the chapter through the lens of internal alchemy, Huang Yuanji turns Laozi's philosophy into a clear, practical path of entry:

  1. The Way is formless, yet it must manifest through "being." Because the Great Way itself has neither form nor image—invisible to the eye and inaudible to the ear—where can a practitioner begin? The sage's method is "using being to give form to non-being, and substance to reveal emptiness": taking tangible, patterned Posterior Heaven yin and yang as an "instrument" to recognize and unite the formless One Breath of Prior Heaven within them.
  2. The alchemical pivot of "being gives advantage; non-being gives usefulness." Tangible vessels (chariot, pot, room) serve as tools for carrying and moving, while the true utility resides in the vacant space inside. In cultivation, tangible Posterior Heaven breath can serve as a stepping stone at the outset, but it must never be mistaken for the destination. What truly accomplishes longevity is that single spark of Prior Heaven true breath revealed when yin and yang unite and return to emptiness.
  3. From non-being into being; from being back to non-being. The cosmos stirs forth "One Breath" out of sheer emptiness, and from that One Breath arise yin, yang, and the myriad things. Cultivation walks the path in reverse: first borrowing yin and yang as instruments, then returning them to the root of emptiness, until all dissolves into one expanse of clear, empty breath.

Safety note: Terms in this chapter such as "stealing its breath," "ingesting tangible breath," and "refining the formless elixir" belong to classical alchemical terminology pointing to quiet stillness and harmonizing with natural rhythms. They should never be taken to mean forcibly holding the breath, swallowing air, or driving bodily breath with strained willpower. In everyday practice, simply emphasize relaxation and clearing away distracting thoughts.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：三十辐，共一毂，当其无，有车之用。埏埴以为器，当其无，有器之用。凿户牖以为室，当其无，有室之用。故有之以为利，无之以为用。

Laozi says: Thirty spokes converge upon a single hub; it is precisely where the hub is empty—allowing the axle to pass through—that the chariot has its use. Clay is kneaded and fired to shape a vessel; it is precisely where the vessel is empty inside that it has its use for holding things. Doors and windows are cut out to build a room; it is precisely where the walls enclose an empty space that the room has its use for dwelling. Therefore, what is tangible ("being") provides convenience, while what is empty ("non-being") provides actual usefulness.

§2
Original (Chinese)

夫道，生于鸿濛之始，混于虚无之中，视不见，听不闻，修之者又从何下手乎？圣人知道之体无形，而道之用有象，于是以有形无、以实形虚，盗其气于混沌之乡，敛其神于杳冥之地，以成真一之大道，永为不死之神仙焉。所谓「实而有」者何？真阴、真阳，同类有情之物是也。所谓「虚而无」者何？先天大道根源，龙虎二八初弦之气是也。有气而无质，大道彰矣。故曰：「阴阳合而先天之气见，阴阳分而后天之器成。」《易》曰：「形上谓之道，形下谓之器。」是非器无以见道，亦非道无以载器也。

The Great Way is born at the dawn of primordial chaos and merges within sheer emptiness. Looked at, it cannot be seen; listened for, it cannot be heard. Where, then, can one who wishes to cultivate it possibly begin? The sage understands that while the substance of the Way is formless, its functioning shows discernible patterns. Thus the sage uses being to reveal non-being and substance to frame emptiness—drawing forth the vital breath from the realm of primal chaos and gathering the spirit within the deep stillness of the dim and obscure—thereby accomplishing the Great Way of True Unity and becoming an undying immortal.

What, then, is meant by "substantial and existent"? It refers to True Yin and True Yang within the human body—beings of the same kind that naturally attract, resonate with, and cleave to one another. And what is meant by "empty and non-existent"? It is the root source of the Prior Heaven Great Way, which alchemists liken to "the breath of Dragon and Tiger at the balanced two-eights first-quarter moon"—that pure, nascent vitality at the exact balance of yin and yang, just stirring and not yet hardened into coarse matter. When true breath circulates without gross material substance, the Great Way shines forth clearly. Hence the ancient saying: "When yin and yang unite, the Breath of Prior Heaven appears; when yin and yang divide, the vessels of Posterior Heaven take shape." The Book of Changes likewise says: "What is above form is called the Way; what is below form is called a vessel." This shows that without tangible vessels there is no means to manifest the Way, and without the formless Way there is nothing to sustain the vessels.

§3
Original (Chinese)

太上借喻于车曰：车有辐有毂，辐共三十，以象日月之运行，毂居正中，为众轴所贯；毂空其内，辐凑其外，所以运转而无难。若非其中有空隙处，人何以载、物何以贮乎？故曰：「当其无，即车之用。」又如陶器然，以水和土，揉土为器，一经冶炼，外实中空。究之，凡人利用，不在埏埴之实，而在空洞之虚，如陶侃运甓，非其间虚而无物，安能运转自如？故曰：「当其无，即器之用。」再拟诸筑室，必凿户牖于其中，而后光明大放；及入此室处，户牖亦觉无庸，务于空闲之间，乃堪容膝。虽借有形以为室，必从空际以为居。故曰：「当其无，即室之用。」

Laozi first borrows the analogy of a chariot: a wheel has spokes and a hub. The thirty spokes symbolize the thirty-day monthly cycle of sun and moon, while the hub sits right at the center for the axle to pass through. Empty within and ringed by converging spokes without, the wheel turns effortlessly. Were it not for the hollow opening inside the hub and carriage, how could passengers ride or goods be stored? Thus he says, "Where there is nothing, there lies the use of the chariot."

Next, consider a clay vessel: water is mixed with earth and kneaded into shape; once fired in the kiln, it is solid on the outside and hollow within. When all is said and done, what people actually make use of is not the hardened ring of clay walls, but the hollow emptiness inside—just as Tao Kan of the Jin dynasty carried clay tiles back and forth every day to discipline himself: were pottery and hollow tiles not empty within, how could they be handled lightly and moved with ease? Thus he says, "Where there is nothing, there lies the use of the vessel."

Take again the building of a house: once the walls are raised, doors and windows must be cut into them so that light and air stream in and the room grows bright. Yet the moment you step inside to live there, the doors and windows themselves cease to be what holds you; the place where you actually sit and find room to dwell is the open space inside. Though tangible bricks, tiles, doors, and windows are needed to construct a house, one ultimately dwells in the empty expanse they enclose. Thus he says, "Where there is nothing, there lies the use of the room."

§4
Original (Chinese)

从此三者观之，无非有象以为车、为器、为室，无象以为载、为藏、为居，而凡涉于有象者，即属推行之利矣。凡居于无象者，即裕推行之用矣。故曰：「有以为利，无以为用。」有有无无，亦互为其根焉耳。要之，道本虚无，非阴阳无以见；气属阴阳，非道无以生。阴阳者，后天地而生，有形状方所，不可为长生之丹；惟求道于阴阳，由阴阳而返太极，则先后混合，而大道得矣。后之修金丹者，徒服有形之气，不知炼无形之丹，欲其成仙也，不亦南辕而北辙耶？

Looking at these three examples together, it is simply this: what has visible form makes the chariot, the vessel, and the room, while what has no form enables carrying, holding, and dwelling. Everything pertaining to visible form serves as the convenient instrument that sets things in motion; everything abiding in the formless supplies the rich efficacy of that operation. Hence: "Being provides convenience; non-being provides usefulness." Being and non-being are, in truth, each other's mutual root.

In sum, the Way is originally empty and formless, yet without the movement and union of yin and yang it cannot be perceived; breath belongs to yin and yang, yet without the Way as its matrix it cannot arise. Recognize that yin and yang born after the separation of heaven and earth possess shape and location, and by themselves can never form the Elixir of long life. Only by seeking the Way within yin and yang, and returning through their harmonization back to the undivided Supreme Ultimate so that Prior Heaven and Posterior Heaven fuse into one, is the Great Way truly attained. When later students of the Golden Elixir merely swallow and circulate tangible, ordinary breath without knowing how to refine the formless true elixir within emptiness, yet still hope to become immortals—is that not heading north with their chariot poles when they wish to travel south?

§5
Original (Chinese)

道本无名，强名曰道；道本无修，强名曰修。夫以道之为物，至虚至无，方能至神至圣。试观天地，一气清空，了无一物，及伏之久，而气机一动，阴阳生焉。于是形形色色，莫不斐然有文，灿然成章，充满于四塞之中。谁为造之？谁与生之？何莫非道生一气，一气化为阴阳，而万物于是滋生矣。故曰：「道自虚无生一气，便从一气产阴阳。阴阳自是成三姓，三姓重生万化昌。」修行人，欲求至道之真，以成仙圣之体，必先以阴阳为利器，后以虚无为本根，而大道得矣。章内三「无」字，指其空处曰无，大约言修炼人，自无而有，自有还无，以至清空一气，而大道乃成，其意殆取此耳。

The Way is originally nameless; only by forced convention do we call it "the Way." The Way is originally beyond artificial cultivation; only by convention do we speak of "cultivating" it. Precisely because the Way is utterly empty and free of fixation can it exercise a supremely numinous and sagely creative power. Look up and observe heaven and earth: at first there is nothing at all save one clear, empty expanse of breath. After that subtle impulse lies quietly dormant for a long time, it suddenly stirs—and yin and yang are born. At once the manifold shapes and colors of the world unfold in luminous pattern and radiant order, filling every corner of the four quarters. Who fashioned them? Who brought them forth? Is it not all simply the Way giving birth to the One Breath of Prior Heaven, that One Breath differentiating into yin and yang, and the myriad things flourishing from there?

Thus the alchemical verse says: "From emptiness the Way brings forth One Breath; from that One Breath arise yin and yang. Yin and yang then unite to form the Three Families; the Three Families regenerate, and myriad transformations prosper." A cultivator who seeks the truth of the Supreme Way to accomplish the body of an immortal sage must first employ yin and yang as an advantageous instrument, and then take emptiness as the ultimate root—only thus is the Great Way gained. The three occurrences of the character "non-being" (wu) in this chapter each point to the hollow center of an object. Laozi's intention was surely to tell practitioners that cultivation proceeds "from non-being into being, and from being back to non-being," until one merges entirely into a single clear, empty breath and the Great Way is complete.

Key Terms

  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: the primordial, undivided vitality prior to the separation of heaven and earth and before conceptual thought arises; referred to in this chapter as "the Great Way of True Unity" and "one clear, empty breath," in contrast to tangible Posterior Heaven respiratory breath.
  • the dim and obscure: a deep state of tranquil emptiness beyond discursive thought yet free of drowsiness; this chapter speaks of "gathering the spirit within the realm of the obscure."
  • Prior Heaven / Posterior Heaven: this chapter contrasts the Prior Heaven Great Way (the formless elixir) with Posterior Heaven yin and yang (vessels possessing shape and location), teaching that one borrows Posterior Heaven as an instrument to return to Prior Heaven until "Prior and Posterior fuse into one."
  • Breath of Dragon and Tiger at the Two-Eights First Quarter (longhu erba chuxian zhi qi): an alchemical metaphor combining lunar phases with Dragon and Tiger; on the eighth and twenty-third days of the lunar month, yin and yang each occupy half (eight liang each, hence "two-eights"), symbolizing Prior Heaven vitality just stirring in perfect balance before falling into coarse physical form.
  • From non-being into being; from being back to non-being (zi wu er you, zi you huan wu): Huang Yuanji's summary of the cultivation sequence in this chapter—first bringing forth the wondrous being of true yang out of emptiness, then dissolving patterned effort back into clear, empty non-being.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Leave open space in your life so your spirit has room to turn. A hub turns only because it is hollow; a room is livable only because it is open. When schedules are packed airtight and thoughts crowd every corner, both effectiveness and clarity get choked off. Intentionally carve out a daily block of "unscheduled blank space."
  2. Methods are the "convenience"; a clear mind is the "use." Posture, breathing exercises, and structured frameworks are all "tangible conveniences" that help you settle into practice. Do not pour all your attention into tinkering with techniques and forms while forgetting to return to an easy, tranquil mind.
  3. Never force or push tangible breath. When sitting quietly or relaxing, avoid straining to take deep breaths, holding your breath, or shoving sensations around with willpower—that falls into what Huang Yuanji criticizes as "merely ingesting tangible breath." Let the breathing stay gentle and natural, placing your focus on letting go and settling into stillness.
  4. Practice within "being," return to "non-being." You need not flee daily chores and human responsibilities (those are the vessel, the convenience). Instead, once an affair is handled, release your attachment promptly and return to inner spaciousness (that is the Way, the use).

Translator's Notes

  1. Thirty spokes converge upon a single hub: Ancient Chinese chariots commonly used thirty spokes per wheel, symbolizing the thirty days of a lunar month. The hub (gu) is the round wooden block at the center of the wheel bored hollow to receive the axle.
  2. Kneading clay to shape a vessel (shanzhi yi wei qi): Shan means to knead and mix with water; zhi means potter's clay.
  3. Cutting doors and windows to make a room (zao huyou yi wei shi): Hu denotes a single-leaf door and you a window. Ancient builders raised earthen walls and cut openings for doors and windows to enclose a habitable space.
  4. Tao Kan carrying tiles: From the Book of Jin ("Biography of Tao Kan"). While serving as Governor of Guangzhou with light official duties, the Eastern Jin statesman Tao Kan carried a hundred clay tiles from his study out into the courtyard each morning and back inside each evening to train his stamina and guard against idleness. Huang Yuanji invokes the anecdote alongside clay vessels to emphasize lightness and ease of movement enabled by inner emptiness.
  5. What is above form is called the Way; what is below form is called a vessel: Quoted from the Appended Remarks (Xici shang) of the Book of Changes.
  6. "From emptiness the Way brings forth One Breath..." A celebrated alchemical quatrain associated with supplements to the Wuzhen pian (Folio on Awakening to Reality) and Song–Yuan internal alchemy texts (a common variant reads: "Yin and yang reunite to form the Three Bodies; the Three Bodies regenerate and myriad things prosper"). In internal alchemy, the "Three Families" (sanxing) denote Essence, Breath, and Spirit (or Original Essence, Original Breath, and Original Spirit) united as one.
  7. Textual collation: In the base text, printing errors of 日 for 曰 ("says") in the opening citation and Book of Changes quote were corrected; 面 was corrected to 而 in "not in the solidity of the clay, but in the emptiness of the hollow."
