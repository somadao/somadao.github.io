Chapter 16 · Utmost Emptiness and Steadfast Stillness

Reach utmost emptiness and guard steadfast stillness to watch the turning point of cosmic return; the Mysterious Pass lies at the moment just before birth—knowing the constant Way without reckless contrivance brings enduring safety

About This Chapter

"Reach utmost emptiness, guard steadfast stillness; while the myriad things arise together, I thereby observe their return"—across centuries, this passage of the Daodejing has been revered by internal alchemists as the supreme outline of cultivation.

Huang Yuanji pours extraordinary depth into this chapter, unveiling Laozi's lines as "the work at the very root and the learning at the sovereign head" of internal alchemy:

  1. Where exactly do the root of the Way and the Mysterious Pass reside? Not in the featureless void before heaven and earth divided, nor in the bustling multiplicity after creatures have flourished—but precisely in that razor-fine instant "just before opening, just before birth." That subtle pivot is the Living Zi Hour of cosmic return, swift as flint-spark or lightning, lost the moment a stray thought intervenes.
  2. "Returning to the root and restoring life" is the plainest, most constant Way. Just as trees flourish in spring and summer and then draw their vitality inward in autumn, shedding leaves back to the roots to store life for next year, a person's return from agitation to stillness is the most ordinary law of nature. Understanding this "constant Way" is clarity (ming).
  3. Condemning dangerous side-paths and arriving at impartiality and endurance. All who ignore the constant Way to ingest toxic minerals or pursue perverse sexual practices fall into "not knowing the constant, acting recklessly to bring disaster." One who truly knows the constant Way sees that it is as universally shared as water and fire, naturally growing spacious, impartial, kingly, heavenly, and aligned with the Way—so that even when the mortal body ends, true Spirit endures.

Safety note: This chapter explicitly condemns dangerous side-paths such as "harvesting the Seven Metals and Eight Minerals as medicine" and "taking young boys and girls to form an elixir," underscoring that the genuine Way is completely plain and natural. Never trust any ingestion of mineral concoctions or unethical practices. In everyday experience, simply relax into quietness and observe the body-mind's natural rhythm of returning from activity to rest.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：致虚极，守静笃，万物并作，吾以观其复。夫物芸芸，各复归其根。归根曰静，静曰复命，复命曰常，知常曰明。不知常，妄作凶。知常容，容乃公，公乃王，王乃天，天乃道，道乃久，没身不殆。

Laozi says: Reach the utmost extreme of emptiness, and guard stillness with unwavering firmness. While the myriad things all burst forth together, I thereby observe their return. Abundant and teeming, every single thing returns to its own root. Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called restoring life (fuming); restoring life is called the constant; knowing the constant is called clarity (ming). Not knowing the constant and acting recklessly invites calamity. Knowing the constant makes one accommodating; being accommodating leads to impartiality; impartiality leads to kingly breadth; kingly breadth accords with Heaven; according with Heaven aligns with the Way; aligning with the Way endures forever—even when the physical body comes to its end, there is no peril.

§2
Original (Chinese)

人欲修大道，成金仙，历亿万年而不坏，下手之初，不可不得其根本。根本为何？即玄关窍也。夫修真炼道，非止一端，岂区区玄关妙窍，可尽其蕴哉？盖天有天根，物有物蒂，人有人源，断未有无始基而能成绝大之功、不朽之业者。试观天地未开以前，固阗寂无闻也；既辟而后，又浩荡无极矣。谓未开为天根乎？茫荡而无著，固不可以为天根。谓已辟为天根乎？发育而无穷，亦不得指为天根。是根究何在哉？盖在将开未开处也。又观人物未生之时，固渺茫而无象也；既育以后，又繁衍而靡涯矣。谓未生为本乎？冲漠而无状，固不得以为人物之本。谓既育为本乎？变化而靡穷，亦不得视为人物之本。是本果何在哉？亦在将生未生时也。欲修大道，可不知此一而妄作胡为乎？

Anyone who wishes to cultivate the Great Way and attain the imperishable state of a Golden Immortal across eons must, at the very outset, find its root. What is that root? It is the Mysterious Pass (xuanguan qiao). Someone may ask: cultivating truth and refining the Way involve many facets—how could a single Mysterious Pass encompass all its depth? The answer is simple: Heaven has its heavenly root, fruit has its stem, and humanity has its origin. Never has a towering achievement or imperishable work been raised without laying its foundation first.

Consider heaven and earth: before they split open, all was utter silence and stillness; once they split open, boundless vastness unfolded without end. Should we call the state before opening the heavenly root? Too vast and unanchored—that cannot be the root. Should we call the state after opening the heavenly root? Endless proliferation and growth—that cannot be pinned down as the root either. Where, then, is the root? Precisely at the threshold "about to open, yet not yet open"! Look again at human beings and all creatures: before birth, dim and imageless; after birth, multiplying without limit. Call the pre-birth state the root? Too blank and featureless to grasp. Call the post-birth state the root? Endless transformation with no stopping point. Where, then, is the true root of beings? Precisely at the threshold "about to be born, yet not yet born"! If one wishes to cultivate the Great Way, how can one ignore this singular pivot and blunder about recklessly?

§3
Original (Chinese)

太上示人养道求玄之法曰：「致虚极，守静笃，吾以观其复。」此明修士，要得玄关，惟有收敛浮华，一归笃实，凝神于虚，养气于静，致虚之极，守静之笃，自然万象咸空，一真在抱。故《易》曰：「复，其见天地之心乎？」又邵子云：「冬至子之半，天根理极微。一阳初动处，万物未生时。」此时即天理来复，古人喻为活子时也。又曰：「一阳初发，杳冥冲醒。」此正万物返本，天地来复之机，先天元始祖气，于此大可观矣。但其机甚微，其气甚迅，当前即是，转念则非，不啻石火电光，俄顷间事耳。请观之草木，当其芸芸有象，枝枝叶叶，一任灿烂成章，艳彩夺目，俱不足为再造之根、复生之本。惟由发而收，转生为杀，收头结果，各归其根，乃与修士丹头无或异也。归根矣，是由动而返静矣。既返于静，依然复诞降嘉种之初，在物为返本，在人为复命，非异事也。一春一秋，物故者新；一生一杀，花开者谢。是知修士，复命之道，亦天地二气之对待，为一气之流行，至平至常之道也。能知常道，即明大道。由此进功，庶不差矣。

Laozi shows us the method of nurturing the Way and discovering the mystery: "Reach utmost emptiness, guard steadfast stillness; I thereby observe their return." This tells the practitioner unmistakably: to enter the Mysterious Pass, the sole path is to draw in all outward flash and vanity, return wholly to grounded simplicity, condense spirit in emptiness, and nourish breath in stillness. When emptiness reaches its limit and stillness is guarded unshakably, myriad appearances naturally dissolve and the single truth rests in your embrace.

Hence the Book of Changes asks: "In the hexagram Fu (Return), do we not behold the heart of Heaven and Earth?" And the Song master Shao Yong sang: "At the dead midpoint of the zi hour on the winter solstice, the principle of Heaven's Root is utterly subtle—right where a single spark of yang first stirs, before the myriad things have come to birth." That exact instant is the return of heavenly principle, which the ancients likened to the Living Zi Hour. As the alchemical adage puts it: "When the single yang first stirs, one is roused out of the dim and obscure." This is the precise hinge where all things return to their source and heaven and earth turn back—here the Primordial Ancestral Breath (One Breath of Prior Heaven) stands clearly revealed! Yet its impulse is exquisitely fine and its arrival swift as lightning: recognized in the immediate instant, it is it; turn a single conceptual thought toward it, and it is gone—fleeting as a spark struck from flint or a flash across the night sky.

Look at plants and trees: in spring and summer, when their branches and leaves burst out in dazzling bloom, none of that outward splendor is the root of next year's renewal. Only in autumn, when outward expansion turns inward to gathering and maturing—setting seed and letting leaves fall back to the roots—does nature mirror the exact formation of the cultivator's "elixir seed" (dantou). Returning to the root means returning from movement to stillness; once returned to stillness, one stands back at the pristine origin that bears fresh seed. In plants this is called "returning to the root"; in human beings it is called "restoring life"—nothing strange or supernatural about it at all! One spring, one autumn, and the old becomes new; one unfolding, one drawing-in, and blossoms open and fade. Thus the cultivator's path of restoring life is simply the alternation of heaven and earth's two breaths flowing as One Breath—the most ordinary, constant Way imaginable. To know this constant Way is to understand the Great Way; advancing along it, one will never go astray.

§4
Original (Chinese)

世之旁门左道，既不知大道根源，又不肯洗心涤虑、原始要终，或炼知觉之性，或修形气之命，或采七金八石以为药，或取童男幼女以为丹，本之既无，道从何得？又况狃于一偏，走入邪径，其究至于损身殒命者多矣。是皆由不知道为常道，以至索隐行怪，履险蹈危，而招凶咎也。惟知道属真常，人人皆有，物物俱足，知之不以为喜，得之不以为奇，如水火之于人，一任取携自如，休休乎虚而能容，物我一视，有廓然大公之心焉。至公无私如此，则与王者「民，吾同胞；物，吾同与」，「体天地而立极，合万物以同源」，不相隔也，斯非「与天为一」乎？夫天即道，道即天，天外无道，道外无天。惟天为大，惟王则之；惟道独尊，惟天法之。故人则有生而有死，道则长存而不敝。虽至飞升脱壳，亦有殒灭之时，然形虽亡而神不亡，身虽没而气不没。《诗》曰：「文王在上，於昭于天」，其斯之谓欤？是皆从虚极静笃，而观来复之象，乃能如此莫测也。学者可不探其本，而妄作招凶哉？

Followers of heterodox side-paths neither know the true root of the Great Way nor are willing to wash the mind clean and trace life from beginning to end. Some fixate on cultivating mere cognitive awareness; some tinker only with physical breath; some gather the Seven Metals and Eight Minerals to swallow as elixirs; others even attempt to exploit young boys and girls for alchemical drugs. Lacking the very root, where could the Way possibly come from? Worse yet, wedded to one-sided obsessions and straying down crooked paths, countless people end up ruining their health and losing their lives. Every such tragedy stems from failing to realize that the Way is constant and ordinary—leading them instead to chase bizarre mysteries, tread perilous ground, and pull disaster down upon their own heads.

Only one who realizes that the Way is truly constant knows that every human being already possesses it and every creature is complete with it. Understanding it sparks no boastful elation; attaining it feels nothing bizarre—like water and fire in daily life, it is freely at hand whenever needed. Thus one becomes gentle, open, and accommodating, viewing self and all beings alike with a vast, impartial heart. Reaching such utter impartiality bridges every gap with the true sovereign's spirit: "All people are my brothers and sisters; all things are my companions," "embodying heaven and earth to set the standard, uniting all creatures in one common source." Is this not being "one with Heaven"?

Indeed, Heaven is the Way and the Way is Heaven—no Way stands outside Heaven, and no Heaven outside the Way. Only Heaven is truly vast, and the sage-king models himself upon Heaven; only the Way is supreme, and Heaven models itself upon the Way. Mortal human beings have birth and death, whereas the Way endures forever unspoiled. Even when a perfected sage eventually sheds the mortal shell, the visible frame must still pass away—yet though the form perishes, Spirit does not perish; though the body ends, true Breath does not end. As the Classic of Poetry sings: "King Wen is on high—how brightly he shines in Heaven!" Is that not what this means? All of this unfathomable fruition begins simply from reaching utmost emptiness, guarding steadfast stillness, and observing the moment of return. How could any student neglect this root and court calamity through reckless contrivance?

§5
Original (Chinese)

太上示人本原上工夫，头脑上学问。此处得力，则无处不得力。学者会得此旨，则恪守规中，绵绵不息，从无而有，自有而无，虽一息之瞬，大道之根本具焉；即终食之间，大道之元始存焉。从此一线微机，采之炼之，渐渐至于蓬勃不可遏抑，皆此一阳所积而成也。纵浩气塞乎天地，阳神贯乎斗牛，何莫非一点真气所累而致乎？学人不得这个真气，但以后天形神为炼，不过如九牛之一毛、沧海之一粟耳，何敢与天地并论乎？惟行此道，而与天地同体，乃极亿万年而不坏。修道者，须认真主脑，采取不失其时可也。

Here Laozi points straight to the work at the origin and the learning at the sovereign head. Gain real footing at this single point, and you will have footing everywhere. Once a student grasps this purport, diligently guarding the center in an unbroken stream—moving from non-being into being and from being back to non-being—even within the space of a single breath, the root of the Great Way is wholly present; even within the duration of a single meal, the primordial origin of the Great Way abides intact.

Following this single hairline impulse—gently gathering it and warmly refining it until it swells into an unstoppable tide—every bit of it is built up from this one spark of yang. Even when vast, flood-like breath fills heaven and earth and the Yang Spirit pierces the constellations, is not all of it accumulated from that first speck of true breath? If a practitioner fails to obtain this true breath and merely fusses with the Posterior Heaven body and cognitive mind (Prior Heaven / Posterior Heaven), his scope is no bigger than one hair on nine oxen or a single grain in the vast ocean—how could it ever stand alongside heaven and earth? Only by walking this constant Way does one become one body with heaven and earth, enduring unspoiled across eons. Cultivators must recognize this true sovereign head clearly and meet its timing without ever missing the mark.

Key Terms

  • Mysterious Pass (xuanguan): also called the Mysterious Pass aperture; not a fixed anatomical organ, it is revealed here as the subtle threshold "just before opening, just before birth" where a single spark of yang stirs in deep stillness.
  • Living Zi Hour: not the clock hour between 11 p.m. and 1 a.m., but the living moment when utmost emptiness and stillness naturally spark the first stir of Prior Heaven yang ("roused out of the dim and obscure").
  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: called here "Primordial Ancestral Breath" and "one spark of true breath"—the undivided vitality appearing at cosmic return, and the true root of restoring life.
  • the dim and obscure: the deeply tranquil, unforced void out of which the first stir of true yang spontaneously awakens.
  • Prior Heaven / Posterior Heaven: this chapter warns that working solely with Posterior Heaven form and cognitive spirit is like a grain in the sea; only returning to Prior Heaven true breath restores life enduringly.
  • Returning to the root and restoring life (guigen fuming): likened to autumn leaves falling back to the roots to prepare next spring's growth, signifying the return from outward restlessness to quiet origin to renew innate life.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Notice the quiet gap "before a thought arises." Sit quietly or rest your eyes for a few minutes each day; as breathing and chatter settle, gently notice the clear space right after one thought fades and just before the next begins—do not grab at it, simply taste that crisp, open ease. That is an everyday glimpse of "just before birth."
  2. Honor nature's cycle of expansion and gathering. Just as trees cannot bloom nonstop year-round, humans cannot run at full throttle indefinitely. After intense work, deliberately "return to the root"—early sleep, solitude, unplugging—to store vitality for the next cycle of creativity.
  3. Stay clear of every "reckless shortcut" promising miracles. In health, meditation, or work, anything boasting instant dramatic effects, defying natural physiology, or relying on dubious external substances is classic "not knowing the constant, acting recklessly to bring disaster." What truly works is always plain, steady, and in harmony with nature.
  4. Let stillness ripen into warmth and breadth, not aloofness. The chapter gives a built-in test for genuine stillness: "knowing the constant makes one accommodating; being accommodating leads to impartiality." If quiet practice makes you fussier, colder, or more judgmental, something is off; genuine emptiness widens the heart and makes you fairer and easier to live with.

Translator's Notes

  1. "In the hexagram Fu (Return), do we not behold the heart of Heaven and Earth?": From the Tuan commentary on hexagram Fu (䷗, one yang line returning beneath five yin lines) in the Book of Changes, symbolizing the winter solstice renewal of life.
  2. "At the dead midpoint of the zi hour on the winter solstice...": From Northern Song philosopher Shao Yong's poem Chant of the Winter Solstice (Dongzhi yin), widely quoted by internal alchemists for depicting the subtle dawn of one yang.
  3. Seven Metals and Eight Minerals: Raw metallic and mineral substances (gold, silver, copper, iron, lead, tin, mercury, cinnabar, realgar, sulfur, etc.) used in external alchemy; ingesting such toxic compounds frequently caused heavy-metal poisoning and death, leading Qing internal alchemists to denounce the practice fiercely.
  4. "All people are my brothers and sisters; all things are my companions": From Northern Song thinker Zhang Zai's Western Inscription (Ximing), expressing Confucian cosmic solidarity.
  5. "King Wen is on high—how brightly he shines in Heaven!": From the Classic of Poetry ("Major Odes," Wenwang); 於 is pronounced wū (exclamation of awe).
  6. Textual collation: Numerous base-text misprints were corrected in the Chinese source, including 日 → 曰, 妙鈣 → 妙窍, 请末开/请已辟/谓末生为本平？淇漠而无状/请既育 → 谓未开/谓已辟/谓未生为本乎？冲漠而无状/谓既育, 茫汤面无著 → 茫荡而无著, 香冥 → 杳冥, 又祝…走人 → 又况…走入, an extraneous dot in 惟道独尊 deleted, 脱売 → 脱壳, and 安作 → 妄作.
