Front Matter · 卷首

Author's Preface by Huang Yuanji

Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism all spring from one primordial Breath of the Great Void; sages and blades of grass share the same source; commenting on Laozi is not to display eloquence, but to reveal the Mysterious Pass and preserve the lineage of the Way

About This Chapter

This preface was written in the winter of the tenth year of the Guangxu reign (1884) by the Qing-dynasty internal alchemy master Huang Yuanji (personal name Shang) for his Commentary on the Daodejing. Though only three paragraphs long, it lays bare the spirit of the entire work:

  1. One source across the three teachings, already complete within: Confucianism speaks of "utmost sincerity," Buddhism of "true emptiness," and Daoism of the Golden Elixir. Different in name, at bottom they all point to the single primordial Breath of the Great Void—the One Breath of Prior Heaven—that fills the cosmos and pervades all things. This Breath is no greater in a sage and no smaller in an ordinary person, or even in birds, beasts, grasses, and trees: just as water fills an ocean and fills a single ladle alike, the only difference lies in each being's acquired endowment.
  2. Urgency behind the commentary: As public morals decline, the most ordinary and grounded Great Way is dismissed as strange and superstitious, until Confucians cease to resemble Confucians, Buddhists cease to resemble Buddhists, and Daoists cease to resemble Daoists. Unless its root is pointed out and its subtle key unlocked, the Great Way risks hanging suspended in thin air with no one left to carry it on.
  3. Origin of the book: In his later years, Huang Yuanji discoursed with fellow practitioners around the Leyutang (Hall of Joyful Nurture) in Zigong, Sichuan. Elected by the gathering to take the teacher's seat, he expounded the Daodejing day and night as a living verification of self-cultivation and moral refinement, accumulating stacks of lecture notes. Though he modestly admits to many flaws, parts of his commentary happened to resonate with Laozi's subtle intent, and at his companions' earnest request he agreed to have the blocks carved and published for the world.

Reading this preface immediately shows the hallmark of Huang Yuanji's reading of Laozi: eschewing empty mystification, he weaves internal alchemy, Confucian ethics, and sagely bearing into one living path.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

三教之道，圣道而已。儒曰至诚，释曰真空，道曰金丹，要皆太虚一气，贯乎天地人物之中者也。惟圣人独探其源、造其极，与天之虚圆无二，是以成为圣人。能刚能柔，可圆可方，无形状可拟，无声臭可拘，所由神灵变化，其妙无穷，有不可得而窥测者。若皆自然天然本来物事，处圣不增，处凡不减。即等而下之，鸟兽草木之微，亦莫不与圣人同此一气，同此一理。试观汪洋大海，水至难测者也，然而一海所涵，水也；一勺所容，亦无非水；鼋鼍蛟龙所受以生成者此水，而鱼鳖虾蟹所赖以养育者，亦无非此水。太虚之气，亦犹海水一般。天地、圣贤、人物，虽纷纭错杂，万有不齐，而其受气成形之初，同此一气，除此以外，别无生气，亦别无生理，所争者资禀之各殊耳。孟子曰：「尧舜与人同。」又曰：「人之所以异于禽兽者几希。」诚确论也。

The Way taught by Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism is, in the end, simply the Way of the sages. Confucians call it "utmost sincerity," Buddhists call it "true emptiness," and Daoists call it the Golden Elixir; at root, all refer to that single True Breath filling the Great Void and permeating heaven, earth, humanity, and the myriad things.

Only the sage explores this source for himself and carries it to its utmost completion, bringing his own mind into seamless unity with the vacuous roundness of Heaven—and thereby becoming a sage. At that height, he can be firm when firmness is called for and yielding when yielding is needed, round and adaptive or square and principled; no fixed form can be likened to it, no sound or scent can confine it. Hence its numinous transformations and inexhaustible marvels defy all human reckoning.

Yet when all is said and done, this is nothing other than what is naturally and originally present in heaven and earth: in a sage it gains not a whit, and in an ordinary person it loses not a hair. Descending even further, down to the smallest birds, beasts, grasses, and trees, not a single thing fails to share this very same Breath and this very same heavenly principle with the sage.

Look at the vast, boundless ocean: its waters are unfathomable, yet what fills the entire sea is water, and what fills a single ladle scooped from it is still nothing but water. The great softshell turtles, alligators, and flood-dragons owe their birth and growth to this water, and the tiny fish, terrapins, shrimp, and crabs depend for their nourishment on this very same water. The primordial Breath of the Great Void is just like seawater. Heaven and earth, sages and worthies, human beings and all creatures appear infinitely varied and tangled on the surface, yet at the very moment they first receive Breath and take form, all receive the exact same Breath. Outside of it, there is no other life-giving breath and no other principle of generation; the sole difference between them lies in the varying thickness or purity of each one's endowment. Mencius said, "Yao and Shun were the same as other people," and again, "That by which human beings differ from birds and beasts is slight indeed." Those are truly unassailable words.

§2
Original (Chinese)

无如世风日下，民俗益偷，大道虽属平常，而人多以诡怪离奇目之，所以儒益非儒，释亦非释，而道益非道矣。若不指出根源，抉破窍妙，恐大道愈晦而不彰，人心愈坏而难治，势必至与鸟兽草木同群，而圣贤直等诸弁髦，大道益危如累卵，虚悬天壤，无人能任斯文之责矣。

Alas, the ways of the world decline day by day, and popular customs grow ever more shallow and lax. Although the Great Way is thoroughly plain and ordinary, most people view it with eyes tuned to the bizarre and outlandish. As a result, Confucians look less and less like Confucians, Buddhists no longer resemble Buddhists, and Daoists are still further from the Way.

If we do not point out the root and lay open its vital keys, I fear the Great Way will grow ever more obscure and hidden, and human hearts ever more corrupted and ungovernable. Left unchecked, humanity will inevitably sink to keeping company with birds, beasts, grasses, and trees, tossing aside the teachings of sages and worthies like worn-out trifles; the Great Way will become as precarious as a stack of eggs, hanging unanchored between heaven and earth with no one left able to shoulder the transmission of this noble tradition.

§3
Original (Chinese)

恭逢盛世，天下乂安，适遇名山道友谈玄说妙，予窃听久之，实非空谈者流，徒求口耳之用，因得与于其际。群尊予以师席，故日夜讲论《道德》一经，以为修身立德之证，不觉连篇累牍。第其中瑕疵迭见，殊难质诸高明，然亦有与太上微意偶合处，不无小补于世，众友请付剞劂，公诸天下后世。予于此注，实多抱愧，不敢自欺欺人，无奈众友念切，始诺其请。兹值刊刻肇始，予故弁数言于篇首，以叙此注之由来如此。 光绪十年孟冬月谷旦，元吉黄裳自序。

Fortunate to live in a flourishing age when the realm is at peace, I chanced upon fellow seekers from famed mountains discussing the mysteries of the Way. Listening quietly from the side for a long while, I saw that they were truly no empty talkers pursuing mere lip-and-ear chatter, and so I joined their circle to inquire together.

The company honored me with the teacher's seat, and thus day and night I expounded the Daodejing as practical verification for cultivating the person and establishing virtue, until without my noticing, the transcripts piled up into thick volumes. To be sure, flaws and lapses appear throughout, making it scarcely fit to lay before accomplished masters; yet here and there it chances to accord with the subtle intent of Lord Lao, and so may offer some modest benefit to the world. My companions urged me to send it to the woodblock carvers and share it with readers everywhere and in ages to come.

Regarding this commentary I truly feel deep misgiving and dare not deceive myself or others; yet unable to refuse my companions' earnest sincerity, I finally consented to their request. Now, as block-carving begins, I set down these brief words at the head of the volume to recount how this commentary came to be.

Auspicious day of the tenth lunar month, tenth year of the Guangxu reign (1884). Preface by Huang Shang, styled Yuanji.

Key Terms

  • Primordial Breath of the Great Void (taixu yiqi): The cosmic primordial breath—identical with the One Breath of Prior Heaven—that pervades heaven, earth, sages, and all creatures alike, one in substance though diverse in manifestation.
  • Unity of the Three Teachings (utmost sincerity, true emptiness, Golden Elixir): Huang Yuanji treats Confucian "utmost sincerity," Buddhist "true emptiness," and Daoist "Golden Elixir" as different names adopted by the three traditions for one and the same origin.
  • Endowment (zibing): The innate constitution and acquired temperament a being receives at birth; while the primordial Breath itself makes no distinction, disparities among beings stem from their endowment and cultivation.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Establish confidence first: the Great Way is already within you. "In a sage it gains nothing; in an ordinary person it loses nothing." Cultivation is not chasing after some exotic possession you lack, but polishing bright the clear nature already yours.
  2. Do not treat cultivation as novelty-hunting. From the outset Huang Yuanji criticizes viewing the plain Great Way as bizarre or uncanny; genuine, trustworthy practice always comes back to daily self-cultivation, moral grounding, and honest living.
  3. Shun "learning of mouth and ear." Discussing subtle principles can spark insight, yet if it stops at clever talk and pleasant listening, even the loftiest vocabulary cannot heal a restless mind.
  4. Read and share with humility. Even a master as seasoned as Huang Yuanji candidly wrote that he felt "deep misgiving" and dared not deceive himself or others—the deeper one's cultivation, the less room there is for arrogance.

Translator's Notes

  1. Huang Yuanji (Huang Shang): A celebrated Qing-dynasty internal alchemy master from Fengcheng, Jiangxi, active during the Xianfeng, Tongzhi, and Guangxu reigns (styled Yuanji, personal name Shang). In his later years he established the Leyutang (Hall of Joyful Nurture) in the Zigong–Fushun area of Sichuan, where disciples recorded his lectures as Recorded Sayings of the Leyutang, Commentary on the Daodejing, and Essential Sayings of the Daoist Gate.
  2. "No sound, no scent" (wu sheng wu xiu): From the Classic of Poetry ("Greater Odes," King Wen): "The workings of High Heaven have neither sound nor scent," also quoted in the Doctrine of the Mean. Here 臭 is read xiù, meaning "odor" or "scent."
  3. "Yao and Shun were the same as other people" / "That by which human beings differ from birds and beasts is slight": Both from Mencius, "Li Lou II"; jixi (几希) means "very little" or "exceedingly slight."
  4. "Worn-out trifles" (bianmao): Originally the dark cloth cap (bian) worn by noble youths at the capping ceremony and the childhood hair-tuft (mao) discarded once adulthood was reached; by extension, anything useless and casually thrown away.
  5. "The realm is at peace" (tianxia yi'an): The character 乂 (yì) means "well-governed, peaceful"; some editions misprint it as 义安, emended here by context.
  6. "Send to the woodblock carvers" (fu jijue): Ji and jue were specialized carving knives used in traditional Chinese woodblock printing; the idiom means to publish in print.
  7. "Auspicious day of the tenth lunar month" (mengdongyue gudan): Mengdong is the first month of winter (the tenth lunar month), and gudan means an auspicious day. The tenth year of Guangxu corresponds to 1884 CE.
