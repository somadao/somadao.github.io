General Purport of the Daodejing

Self-cultivation and governing the world form one unbroken thread; Heavenly Virtue and the Kingly Way are never two halves; Laozi's learning unites inner and outer through reverence, not quietist withdrawal

About This Chapter

Before opening his chapter-by-chapter commentary on the Daodejing, Huang Yuanji places a brief but weighty general statement aimed squarely at clearing Laozi's name.

Later readers of the Daodejing have often fallen into two extremes: one camp treats Laozi as a world-shunning hermit sitting dryly in deep mountains oblivious to human affairs; the other fixes only on statecraft maxims like "governing a great state is like cooking a small fish," discarding inner cultivation of mind and nature. Huang Yuanji cuts straight to the heart of the matter: Heavenly Virtue and the Kingly Way, self-cultivation and governing the world, form a single continuous thread and must never be split in two.

The pivot of this manifesto comes as a surprise: rather than launching into arcane alchemical jargon, Huang borrows a plain Confucian watchword—"reverence" (jing)—to unite inner and outer: - Heaven and earth move themselves by a single Primordial Breath and nourish all creatures by that very same Breath; wind, clouds, thunder, and rain are Heaven's ordinances, yet they are not identical with "the true body of Heaven." - The sage nourishes himself with a single reverent, lucid mind and meets ten thousand affairs and settles the people's hearts with that very same mind; joy, anger, sorrow, and delight, along with rites, music, laws, and administration, are the sage's enactments, yet they are not identical with "the true mind of the sage."

Once we grasp this unifying thread—"the Way that joins inner and outer"—Huang Yuanji's seamless movement throughout the commentary between alchemical cultivation and wise engagement with the world feels completely natural.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

太上修身治世之道，原是一贯，不分两事。若不推开说明，只云修身即以治世，治世厥惟修身，如《大学》开口说「自明明德」一句便了，而「新民」一概不管，亦属一偏之学，不足以见圣道之宏，体用兼赅，本末并进者也。盖圣人之道，不外一「敬」而已。人果以敬存心应事，天下有何难治者哉？孔子曰：「能以礼让为国乎，何有？不能以礼让为国，如礼何？」自古圣贤，无有只修其身，不盛乎世者，观天地即可知圣贤矣。天地以一元之气自运，即以一元之气育民，其间寒暑温凉，与夫风云雷雨，即天之行其政令，以施生化之功，虽变幻无穷，而天只顺其气机之常。其在圣贤，以此敬自持，即以此敬及物，其间哀怒喜乐，与夫礼乐刑政，即圣人顺行其治道，以定人物之情，虽风土不齐，而圣人只尽其在己之性，故曰：「风云雷雨，天所不能无，而不得谓风云雷雨之即天；哀怒喜乐，圣人不能无，而不得谓哀怒喜乐之即圣。天有真天体，圣有真圣心。」总皆主之以敬，一任天下事变万端纷纭来前，无一不得其当。

Lord Lao's Way of cultivating the person and governing the world has always been a single, unbroken continuum—never two separate undertakings. If we fail to unfold this clearly and merely repeat in generalities that "cultivating the self is governing the world, and governing the world is simply cultivating the self"—as if the Great Learning stopped at its opening line "to brighten one's own luminous virtue" and ignored "renewing the people" altogether—that too becomes a lopsided doctrine, unable to reveal the grandeur of the sagely Way in which substance and function embrace each other and root and branch advance together.

In truth, the Way of the sages comes down to a single word: "reverence" (jing)—an inner composure that stays attentive, dignified, and never lax. If a person truly holds fast to this reverent mind both in nurturing the heart and in handling affairs, what under heaven could be difficult to govern? Confucius asked: "If one can govern a state through ritual deference, what difficulty is there? If one cannot govern a state through ritual deference, what good are rituals?"

From antiquity onward, there has never been a sage or worthy who cultivated only himself without blessing his age. One need only observe heaven and earth to understand the sages and worthies. Heaven and earth revolve of themselves by the One Primordial Breath (One Breath of Prior Heaven), and by that very same Breath they nurture the people. Within this cycle, cold and heat, warmth and coolness, together with wind, clouds, thunder, and rain, are Heaven enacting its ordinances to carry out the work of generation and transformation. Though their shifts are endless, Heaven simply follows the constancy of its own energetic movement.

So too with sages and worthies: they steady themselves by this very reverence, and by this same reverence they reach out to all things. Within this, sorrow, anger, joy, and delight, together with rites, music, laws, and governance, are simply the sage naturally carrying out his Way of ordering affairs to settle the feelings of people and creatures. Though local customs differ everywhere, the sage simply fulfills the inner nature within himself. Hence the ancients said: "Wind, clouds, thunder, and rain are things Heaven cannot be without, yet one must not say that wind, clouds, thunder, and rain are Heaven itself; sorrow, anger, joy, and delight are things a sage cannot be without, yet one must not say that sorrow, anger, joy, and delight are the sage himself. Heaven has its true heavenly body; the sage has his true sagely mind." Taking reverence as the master throughout, let ten thousand shifting events surge forward from every side—not one will fail to be met with complete appropriateness.

§2
Original (Chinese)

噫！大道不明久矣！论道者但曰虚静无为，言治者但曰功业彪炳，天德王道，分而为二，此三代下所以难索解人也。太上所说，修身治世，不分两事，不是板执修己、全不理治民事，亦不是理治民事、不从内修己来。识得此旨，以修诸己者，即以治诸人，则内无损于己，外无损于人，即《中庸》云「成己，仁也；成物，知也；性之德也，合内外之道也」。处为圣功，出为王道，谁谓老子之学寂灭无为也哉？

Alas! For so long now the Great Way has gone uncomprehended! Those who discourse on the Way speak only of "emptiness, stillness, and non-action"; those who speak of governance fix their eyes solely on "resplendent achievements." Inner Heavenly Virtue and outer Kingly Governance have been cleaved in two—which is why, ever since the Three Dynasties, it has been so hard to find anyone who truly understands.

In Lord Lao's teaching, self-cultivation and governing the world are never two separate matters: it is neither rigidly clinging to solitary self-cultivation while ignoring the people's welfare, nor managing public affairs by external calculation without grounding them in inner cultivation. Once you recognize this guiding purport, you apply the very capacity by which you cultivate yourself directly to ordering human affairs; thus inwardly you do no harm to yourself, and outwardly you do no harm to others. This is precisely what the Doctrine of the Mean declares: "Completing oneself is humaneness; completing things is wisdom; both are virtues of our inner nature—the Way that unites inner and outer." In quiet retirement, it is the inner work of the sage; stepping forth to serve the world, it is the Kingly Way. Who could ever claim that Laozi's learning is a dead, world-negating quietism?

Key Terms

  • One Primordial Breath (yiyuan zhi qi): The original true breath by which heaven and earth revolve and nourish all living beings, synonymous with the One Breath of Prior Heaven.
  • Reverence (jing): A core watchword shared by Song–Ming Neo-Confucianism and Qing internal alchemy; it denotes an undistracted, lucid, dignified awareness that serves both as inner discipline and as the standard for outward conduct.
  • Heavenly Virtue and the Kingly Way (tiande wangdao): A shared Confucian–Daoist ideal pairing inner cultivation of heart and nature ("Heavenly Virtue") with outward ordering of society ("the Kingly Way"); this essay stresses that the two are seamlessly one.
  • Dead quietism (jimie wuwei): Here used for the vulgar misreading of Laozi's "non-action" (wuwei) as fleeing reality, sitting like a dry stump, and refusing all engagement.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Separate passing emotions from your true mind. Joy, anger, sorrow, and delight are like wind, clouds, thunder, and rain—no human being is without them, yet they are surface weather, not your real "heart-substance." Do not let a burst of feeling carry you away; stay anchored in the clear awareness behind it.
  2. Never split cultivation from everyday work. Do not assume practice only happens on a quiet cushion while office or family life runs on anxiety and scheming. "Holding reverence in heart and affairs"—bringing the steadiness of stillness into busy hours—is real skill.
  3. "Non-action" is not giving up or slacking off. True wuwei means refraining from meddling and forced contrivance, doing what belongs to the moment in harmony with principle and conscience—neither burning yourself out inside nor harassing others outside.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Brighten one's own luminous virtue" / "renewing the people": From the opening chapter of the Great Learning: "The Way of great learning lies in brightening luminous virtue, in cherishing the people (read by Zhu Xi as 'renewing the people'), and in resting in utmost goodness."
  2. "If one can govern a state through ritual deference...": From the Analects of Confucius, Book IV ("Li Ren").
  3. "The Three Dynasties" (sandai): The Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties, traditionally revered as the golden age when the Way and governance remained unified.
  4. "Completing oneself is humaneness...": From Chapter 25 of the Doctrine of the Mean; here 知 stands for 智 ("wisdom").
  5. Textual note: In the original printed edition, 本末 was misprinted as 本未, 敬自持 as 散自持, 修诸己 as 修请己, and 寂灭 as 叔灭; all have been emended according to context.
