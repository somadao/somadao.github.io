Chapter 49 · The Sage Has No Fixed Mind

The sage is like a bright mirror or still water, holding no preconceived mind and embracing all beings; applied to self-cultivation, this means meeting stray thoughts with an even mind and ordinary breathing with harmonious breath, never forcing them down in impatience

About This Chapter

"The sage has no fixed mind; he takes the mind of the people as his own mind" is usually read as a statement of political philosophy about loving and tolerating the people. Huang Yuanji first lays out the transforming power of the sage, who is like a bright mirror and still water, utterly impartial and selfless; then, in the second passage, he immediately turns it into the gentlest and most down-to-earth method for settling the mind in quiet sitting in the whole book:

  1. Only without a preconceived mind is there great embrace (§2): The sage's mind is empty and open, clear and luminous—it does not go out to greet things before they arrive, does not cling to or linger over things once they are past, and does not stick to or fixate on things in the present. Toward the good and the not-good, the trustworthy and the untrustworthy alike, he extends the same sincerity and generous tolerance, and in this way actually transforms the not-good into good and the untrustworthy into trustworthy.
  2. How, in self-cultivation, to "treat the not-good as good and trust the untrustworthy" (§3): In the classic, "the sage" is a metaphor for the original mind, and "the realm" for the human body. When you first begin quiet sitting, how could you instantly be free of stray thoughts and keep ordinary breathing from stirring? Huang Yuanji reveals the crucial key: when the Original Spirit appears, meet it with an even mind; when stray thoughts are not yet cleared away, meet them with an even mind too. When the original Breath stirs, receive it with harmonious breath; when coarse, turbid ordinary breath comes, meet it with harmonious breath as well.
  3. Why impatient confrontation only makes practice go more and more astray (end of §3): If, the moment you notice a stray thought, you give rise to aversion and impatience and use thoughts to forcibly suppress thoughts, you are only adding fuel to the cognitive spirit and to ordinary breath. Only by letting go of opposition and keeping a whole sphere of peaceable innocence will the Original Spirit and original Breath return of their own accord.

Safety note: The later part of this chapter speaks of "quietly guarding the Cinnabar Field and nurturing the original Breath." When you first take up quiet sitting and are troubled by stray thoughts or uneven breathing, never force them down impatiently, rigidly fixate on the lower abdomen, or hold your breath; instead follow the gentle principle Huang Yuanji teaches in this chapter—"meet other thoughts with an even mind, meet ordinary breath with harmonious breath"—without haste or agitation, letting things take their natural course.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：圣人无常心，以百姓心为心。善者吾善之，不善者吾亦善之，德善矣；信者吾信之，不信者吾亦信之，德信矣。圣人在天下，惵惵为天下浑其心。百姓皆注其耳目，圣人皆孩之。（惵惵，诚切貌。孩之，以赤子育之也。）

Lord Lao says: The sage has no fixed, unchanging private mind or preconceptions; rather, he takes the wishes of the people as his own mind. Those who are good, I treat with goodness; those who are not good, I also treat with goodness—in this way everyone is transformed toward goodness. Those who are trustworthy, I trust; those who are not trustworthy, I also meet with sincerity—in this way everyone is transformed toward trustworthiness.

When the sage stands in the realm, he is always earnest and cautious, melting his own mind, for the sake of all under heaven, into one undivided, selfless whole. The people all turn their eyes and ears attentively toward the sage, and the sage, as one cherishing innocent little children, embraces and nurtures everyone under heaven. (Huang's note: "Diédié" describes an earnest, caring manner; "treating them as children" means loving, caring for, and teaching them as one would raise an infant.)

§2
Original (Chinese)

圣人之心，空空洞洞，了了灵灵，无物不容，却无物不照，如明镜止水，精光四射，因物付物，略无成心，何其明也？大无不载，小无不包，妍媸美恶，毫无遗漏，何其容也？虽然，究何心哉？不矫情，亦不戾物，故曰：「圣人无常心。」盖谓圣人，未至不先迎，已过不留恋，当前不沾滞，无非因物赋形，随机应变，以百姓之心为心而已。夫百姓，又有何心哉？不过好善恶恶而已。所以圣人于百姓之善者，奖之劝之；于百姓之不善者，亦无不诱之掖之，是善与不善，圣人皆以阔大度量包容之，自使善者欣然神往而益勉于为善矣，不善者亦油然心生而改不善以从善矣，斯为「德善」矣。上好善，则民莫敢不从，其感应之机，自有如此之不爽者。圣人又于百姓之信者，钦之仰之；于百姓之不信者，亦无不爱之慕之，是信与不信，圣人俱以一诚不二包涵之，自使信者怡然理顺而弥深于有信矣，不信者亦奋然兴起而易不信以从信矣，斯为「德信」矣。上好信，则民莫敢不用情，其施报之理，不诚有如此之至神哉？民德归厚，又何疑乎？况人同此心，心同此理，圣人以一心观众心，一理协万理，天下虽大，纳之以诚，百姓虽繁，括之以义，纵贤奸忠伪，万有不齐，而圣人大公无我，一视同仁，开诚布公，推心置腹，浑天下为一体，自有民日迁善而不知为之者，其过化存神之妙，岂若后世劝孝劝忠、示礼示义，所能几及耶？故曰「惵惵然为天下浑其心」焉。盖视天下为一家，合中国如一人，其仁慈在抱，浑然与百姓为一如此。故百姓服德怀仁，无不爱之如父母，敬之若神明，仰之同师保，凡系耳之所闻、目之所见，恒视圣人之声容以为衡，此外有所不知，故曰：「百姓皆注其耳目。」百姓之望圣人如此，圣人亦岂有他哉？惟御众以宽，使众以慈，如父母之于孩子，贤否智愚，爱之惟一，提携保抱，将之以诚。如此而天下有不化者，未之有也。无为之治如此，以视夫言教法治者，相距不啻天渊矣。

The sage's state of mind is empty and open, without the least dregs of selfish desire, yet perfectly clear and luminously aware: there is nothing it cannot embrace and no situation it cannot illuminate. Like a mirror polished to brilliance or a pool of still water without a ripple, its clear light naturally reflects in every direction; whatever comes, it shows exactly as it is, without a trace of prejudgment—how clear it is! So great that there is nothing it does not carry, so fine that there is nothing it does not contain, it receives beauty and ugliness, good and bad, all just as they are, without omitting the least thing—how tolerant it is!

That said, what kind of mind is this, in the end? It is neither affected nor contrary to the nature of things; hence the classic says, "The sage has no fixed mind." This means that the sage's mind does not go out in vain imagination to greet what has not yet come, does not turn back to cling to and linger over what is already past, and does not stick rigidly to or fixate on what is right before it; it simply responds according to the natural face of things, adapting to circumstances, and takes the common mind of the people as its own mind.

And what, then, do the people of the realm wish for? Nothing more than to love goodness and loathe evil. So toward the good among the people, the sage commends and encourages them; toward those who are not yet good, he never fails to guide them patiently, support them, and lift them up. Whether good or not good, the sage embraces them all with a broad and magnanimous heart—and in this way the good naturally rejoice wholeheartedly and strive all the harder to do good, while those who were not good feel shame and a longing for goodness spontaneously welling up in their hearts, and they reform themselves to follow goodness. This is called "virtue becoming good." When those above truly honor goodness, there are none among the people who do not comply and are not transformed; the resonance between human hearts is that precise, without the slightest discrepancy!

In the same way, toward the people who are trustworthy, the sage honors and esteems them; toward those who are not yet trustworthy, he still treats them with loving kindness and sincerity. Whether trustworthy or not, the sage embraces and nurtures them all with a heart of utmost, undivided sincerity—and in this way the trustworthy naturally feel at ease and hold all the more firmly to good faith, while the untrustworthy are roused to shame and awaken, cast off their falseness, and turn toward good faith. This is called "virtue becoming trustworthy." When those above truly honor good faith, there are none among the people who do not respond with genuine feeling; is not this principle of cause and response marvelous beyond measure? That the people's ways should return to simplicity and generosity—what doubt can there be?

What is more, all people are born with this same mind, and every mind shares this same principle. The sage uses a single clear mind to illuminate the minds of the multitudes, and a single fundamental Way to harmonize the myriad principles of the realm: though the realm is vast, he receives it entirely with utmost sincerity; though the people are numerous, he orders them entirely with justice and right principle. Even though, among the crowds, the worthy and the wicked, the loyal and the false differ in countless ways, the sage remains utterly impartial and selfless, regarding all with the same benevolence, open and sincere, putting his heart into the hearts of others, melting the whole realm into one undivided body. In time, the people naturally move toward goodness day by day without feeling that anyone is forcing them—this marvel of subtle, silent transformation, of "transforming wherever he passes and working like spirit wherever he abides," how could the later practice of shouting slogans urging filial piety and loyalty, or of setting up elaborate rules to display ritual and preach righteousness, ever come close to it?

Therefore the classic says, "Earnestly, for the sake of the realm, he melts his mind into one," meaning that the sage regards the realm as one family and all the regions of the land as one person; embracing such profound benevolence and compassion, he becomes one with the people, earnestly caring for them. So the people are moved by the sage's virtue and loving kindness, and without exception love him as they love their parents, revere him as they revere the gods, and look up to him as to their teachers and guardians. Whatever their ears hear and their eyes see, they take the sage's words, conduct, and bearing as their measure, with no other stray thoughts beyond this—this is "the people all turn their eyes and ears toward him." When the people trust and look up to the sage like this, what private devices could the sage have? He simply treats people with generosity and employs the people with compassion, just as parents look after their children: whether clever or slow, promising or clumsy, their love is the same, leading them by the hand and carrying them in their arms, wholly with sincere devotion. When things reach this point, that anyone in the realm should remain untransformed and unwilling to follow has never happened! The difference between this state of "governing through non-action" and governing merely by preaching with words or restraining with harsh punishments and strict laws is as great as that between heaven and earth.

§3
Original (Chinese)

经中「圣人」喻心，「天下」喻身。圣人之修身，不外元神、元气。然人有元神，即有凡神；有元气，即有凡气。下手之初，岂能不起他念，不动凡息？惟知道者，养之既久，自有元神出现，我以平心待之，即他念未除，我亦以平心待之。如此而元神有不见者，未之有也。元神既生，修道有主，又当静守丹田，调养元气，我于此时，于元气之自动，当以和气处之，即凡气之来停，亦当以和气待之。如此而元气有不生者，亦无之也。须知，元神为凡神遮蔽，如明镜为尘垢久封，不急磨洗，岂能遽明？元气被凡气汩没，犹白衣为油污所染，不善浣濯，焉得还原？于此而生一躁心，动一恶念，是欲寻元神以为体，而识神反增其势，欲求见性，不亦难乎？是欲得元气以为主，而凡气愈觉其盛，欲求复命，岂易事哉？惟圣人之治天下，不论善恶诚伪，一以仁慈忠厚之心待之，善者善之，不善者亦善之，信者信之，不信者亦信之，一团天真，浑然在抱。即此是虚，即此是道，虚自生神，道自生气，应有不期然而然者。否则，心若不虚，已先无道，而欲虚神之克见，道气之长存，其可得乎？修身治世，道同一道，理无二理，知治世即知修身，明外因即明内理，故以此理喻之，其示学者至深切矣。学人用功，当谨守真常，善养虚无，则元神、元气，自然来归。若起一客念，动一客气，恐不修而道不得，愈修而道愈远矣。学者慎之戒之！

Bringing this passage of the classic back to the actual practice of internal alchemy: in the classic, "the sage" is a metaphor for the human mind, and "the realm" for the human body. When the sage cultivates himself, it all comes down to nothing other than the two things of the Original Spirit and the original Breath (the One Breath of Prior Heaven). Yet in every person, alongside the pure Original Spirit there is also the ordinary spirit given to wild imaginings (the cognitive spirit); alongside the gentle prior-heaven original Breath there is also the coarse, turbid, fluctuating ordinary breath (ordinary breathing). When you have only just set your hand to quiet sitting, how could not a single stray thought arise and not the slightest ordinary breath stir?

Those who truly understand the Way know how to respond: after long nurturing, a clear Original Spirit will naturally appear, and at that time I meet it with an ordinary, even mind; even if stray thoughts in my head have not yet been completely cleared, I meet them likewise with an ordinary, even mind—neither pleased with myself nor fighting them with aversion. When one nurtures peaceably like this, it has never happened that the Original Spirit fails to appear brightly!

Once the Original Spirit gradually grows clear and one's practice has a mainstay at heart, one should next quietly and steadily guard the central palace of the lower Cinnabar Field and gently nurture the original Breath. At this time, when the prior-heaven original Breath stirs faintly of itself, I receive and go along with it using a gentle, steady mind and breath; even when coarse, turbid posterior-heaven ordinary breath rises up, stalls, and disturbs, I meet it likewise with a gentle, steady mind and breath. When one deals with things peaceably like this, it has likewise never happened that the original Breath fails to arise naturally!

You must understand that when the Original Spirit is obscured by the posterior-heaven ordinary spirit, it is like a bright mirror thickly sealed under dust—without patient polishing, how could it shine all at once? When the original Breath is submerged by posterior-heaven ordinary breath, it is like a white garment stained with grease—without good, gentle washing, how could it regain its whiteness? Yet many people, the moment they sit down and discover stray thoughts and turbid breath, immediately give rise to an impatient mind and stir up a feeling of aversion and disgust, wishing they could swat the stray thoughts dead with one slap. As a result, though they meant to seek the Original Spirit as their master, their irritation and confrontation only make the cognitive spirit all the more arrogant—to hope for clarifying the mind and seeing one's nature like this, is it not doubly difficult? Though they meant to nurture the original Breath as their master, their stifled impatience only makes ordinary breath surge all the more—to hope for nurturing and restoring life like this, how could it be easy?

Look at how the sage governs the realm: whether the people are good or evil, honest or false, he embraces and transforms them all with a heart of benevolence, compassion, loyalty, and generosity—the good I treat with goodness, and the not-good I also treat with goodness; the trustworthy I trust, and the untrustworthy I also trust—always embracing a whole sphere of undivided innocence. In truth, this mind that is unbiased, unhurried, generous, and peaceable is, right here and now, "emptiness," right here and now "the Way"! When the mind is empty, the Original Spirit naturally appears; when one accords with the Way, the original Breath naturally arises; everything will come to fruition of itself without one's noticing, as water flowing forms its own channel. Conversely, if the mind is not empty to begin with and is filled with opposition and impatience, one has already lost the Way at the root—how could one then expect the Original Spirit to appear and the Breath of the Way to endure?

Cultivating the self and governing the world originally follow one and the same Way and the same single principle: understand how to govern the realm, and you understand how to settle body and mind; grasp the principle of outwardly transforming people's hearts, and you grasp the secret of inwardly regulating spirit and breath. In using the governing of the world as a metaphor for self-cultivation, Laozi's guidance to students is earnest in the extreme! When students apply themselves, they should carefully guard that ordinary, true and constant mind and nurture well the state of empty, peaceable calm; then the Original Spirit and the original Breath will naturally return and gather. If at every turn impatient, grasping "guest thoughts" and restless, combative "guest breath" arise, then I fear that while not cultivating certainly will not attain the Way, cultivating blindly like this will only take one farther and farther from the Way! Students must be careful and take this as a warning!

Key Terms

  • Original Spirit and cognitive spirit (ordinary spirit): The pure, aware, and illuminating original mind is the Original Spirit; thoughts that agitate, judge, and impatiently confront are the cognitive spirit. Meet both the Original Spirit and stray thoughts alike "with an even mind," and the cognitive spirit subsides of itself.
  • One Breath of Prior Heaven (original Breath) and ordinary breathing (ordinary breath): The prior-heaven, supremely harmonious living impulse is the original Breath; the coarse, turbid, fluctuating breathing and restless energy of posterior heaven is ordinary breath. Meet both alike "with harmonious breath," and ordinary breath transforms of itself.
  • Lower Cinnabar Field: The lower central space of emptiness where one quietly guards and gently warms the original Breath. This chapter reminds us that it must be guarded lightly with a peaceable mind, without giving rise to impatience or stirring up guest breath.
  • Guest thoughts and guest breath: Restless, deluded thoughts and moods that come from outside and disturb incidentally, as opposed to the inherent, true and constant Original Spirit and original Breath.

Putting It into Practice

  1. When practicing quiet sitting, never "fight" your stray thoughts. Many people get irritated and give up after five minutes of sitting because they feel that "the moment a thought pops into my head, I've failed." Following Huang Yuanji's teaching: when a stray thought comes, look at it with an even mind, as you would a mischievous child—neither despising it nor running after it—and, unable to stay put, it will quiet down on its own.
  2. Use the "three don'ts" to reduce mental exhaustion. Remember the nine-character formula of the sage's mind: "Don't greet what has not yet come (no anticipatory anxiety), don't linger over what has passed (no repeated rumination), don't stick to what is present (focus on the present without rigid clinging)." Remind yourself of it once a day, and your mind will immediately feel half as heavy.
  3. When leading or living with others, label less and offer more ways forward. Affirm and encourage those who do well, and do not rush to dismiss or label those who are not doing well for now; instead "guide and support them"—offering concrete goodwill and trust often does more than harsh criticism to spark positive change in the other person.
  4. Notice your own "guest breath." When you meet with words that grate on your ears or events that weigh on your heart, and a surge of impatient heat suddenly wells up in your chest, first recognize it inwardly—"this is guest breath"—take one long breath out to let it ease, and do not let it make decisions for you.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Diédié" (惵惵): Pronounced dié dié, describing an earnest, cautious manner. The common Wang Bi edition reads 歙歙, the Fu Yi edition 惵惵, and the Heshang Gong edition 怵怵. Huang's base text consistently reads 惵惵 in both classic and commentary, except that the interlinear note in the classic text was mis-OCR'd as 慄慄; it has now been uniformly corrected to 惵惵.
  2. "When those above love good faith, none among the people dare fail to respond with genuine feeling": From the Analects, "Zilu": "When those above love ritual, none among the people dare be irreverent; when those above love righteousness, none among the people dare not submit; when those above love good faith, none among the people dare fail to respond with genuine feeling."
  3. "The people's virtue returns to generosity": From the Analects, "Xue'er": "Be careful at the end and remember those long gone, and the people's virtue will return to generosity."
  4. "Does not greet what has not yet come, does not linger over what is past, does not stick to what is present": Adapted from Zhuangzi, "Responding to Emperors and Kings": "The perfect person uses the mind like a mirror—neither escorting nor welcoming, responding but not storing; therefore he can master things without being harmed."
  5. Textual note: In the base text's title, an extra dash misread as 一 has been deleted; in the interlinear note of the classic text, 慄慄 has been corrected to 惵惵 in line with the main text; in the commentary, 空室洞洞 has been corrected to 空空洞洞, 故日 to 故曰, the traditional form in 不沾滯 normalized to the simplified 不沾滞, 诱之披之 corrected to 诱之掖之, and 散之若神明 to 敬之若神明. In 凡气之来停, the last character might perhaps be 侵 or 冲, but reading it as "stalling and accumulating" also makes sense, so the original character is kept for now. 凡气泪没 has been corrected to 凡气汩没, 岂易事战 to 岂易事哉, and 动一答气 to 动一客气, following the parallel with 客念 in the preceding clause.
