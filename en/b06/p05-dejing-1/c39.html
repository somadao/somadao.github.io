Chapter 39 · Taking the Lowly as the Root

Heaven, earth, spirits, valleys, the myriad things, and rulers all stand firm through attaining the One; in internal alchemy, cultivating nature lies in vigilance in solitude, while refining life requires drawing forth pure yang vitality from the humble lower Cinnabar Field

About This Chapter

Chapter 39 of the Daodejing celebrates the vast power of "attaining the One": through the One, heaven is clear, earth is tranquil, spirits are numinous, valleys are full, the myriad things come to life, and rulers stand as the upright pivot of the realm. Then Laozi turns sharply with a timeless reminder: "Nobility takes the lowly as its root; height takes the low as its foundation." The higher one's rank, the more one must guard a humble base—just as princes call themselves "orphaned," "bereft," and "unworthy"—preferring plain wholeness in embracing the One over dazzling piles of jade or towering, precarious stacks of stone.

Huang Yuanji transforms the chapter's "attaining the One" and "lowly foundation" into a lucid lens on dual cultivation of inner nature and life:

  1. Source and worldly conduct: The "One" is the undivided Boundless prior to chaos and the Supreme Ultimate that engenders all things, embracing galaxies while penetrating a single mote of dust. Because all loftiness grows from below, one must never perch on self-importance and lose one's inner center; like someone frantically trying to push several carts at once and ending up with none, chasing many things never matches quietly embracing the One.
  2. Cultivating nature and refining life in alchemy: Most brilliantly, Huang distinguishes "cultivating nature" from "refining life" with crystal clarity. The text's "rulers guarding the One" points to cultivating nature, summed up in "vigilance in solitude"—constantly illuminating within so that solitary, lucid awareness never slips. Meanwhile, "nobility takes the lowly as its root; height takes the low as its foundation" points to refining life: one must shine spiritual fire downward into the lowest, seemingly turbid region of the lower Cinnabar Field to refine a surging breath of pure yang—what the ancients called "seeking yang within yin, stealing treasure from the ghost's cave." At the close, he sweeps away every subtle clinging: even terms like refining the elixir, advancing fire, and withdrawing tallies are provisional signposts; cling to them, and they too become obstacles like "counting carts until no cart remains."

Safety note: The chapter's talk of "shining spiritual fire downward into the turbid lower field" is a traditional alchemical metaphor for refining life, underscoring humility and solid foundational care. At the same time, Huang Yuanji explicitly breaks attachment at the end: never cling rigidly to technical labels such as "advancing fire, withdrawing tallies, harvesting, and sealing," for the slightest mental clinging becomes an obstacle on the Way.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：昔之得一者：天得一以清，地得一以宁，神得一以灵，谷得一以盈，万物得一以生，侯王得一以为天下贞。其致之，一也。天无以清，将恐裂；地无以宁，将恐发；神无以灵，将恐歇；谷无以盈，将恐竭；万物无以生，将恐灭；侯王无以贞贵高，将恐蹶。故贵以贱为本，高以下为基。侯王自谓孤、寡、不穀，此其以贱为本也，非乎？故致数车无车，不欲琭琭如玉，洛洛如石。（琭，音禄）

Lord Lao says: Of old, those who attained the One: heaven attained the One and became clear; earth attained the One and became tranquil; spirits attained the One and became numinous; valleys attained the One and became full; the myriad things attained the One and came to life; lords and kings attained the One and became the upright standard of all under heaven. What brought all of this about is the One.

Conversely, without what makes it clear, heaven would fear splitting apart; without what makes it tranquil, earth would fear quaking and collapsing; without what makes them numinous, spirits would fear fading away; without what makes them full, valleys would fear drying up; without what gives them life, the myriad things would fear extinction; without what keeps them upright despite noble rank and height, lords and kings would fear stumbling and falling.

Therefore, nobility takes the lowly as its root, and height takes what is below as its foundation. Lords and kings call themselves "orphaned," "bereft," and "unworthy"—is this not taking the lowly as their root? Truly, is it not so? Thus, counting out too many parts—or straining to drive too many carts at once—leaves one with no cart at all. The sage neither wishes to glitter abundantly like piles of jade (lulu) nor to tower precariously like stacked rough stones (luoluo). (Note: 琭 is pronounced lù.)

§2
Original (Chinese)

大道无他，一而已矣。一者何？即鸿蒙未判之元气，混沌未开之无极，生成万物之太极。要之，元气无形，谓之无极；万物皆从无极而有形，实为天下之根，谓之太极。即此是道，圣人无可名而名之，故曰一。若无一则无物，无物便无一，得之则生，失之则没。自昔元始以来，其得一而成形生象，绳绳不已，生生不息者，大周沙界，细入微尘，无或外也。《中庸》云：「视之不见，听之不闻，体物不可遗」，孰非此一乎？故综而计之，天之清也，得一而清；地之宁也，得一而宁；神之灵也，得一而灵；谷之盈也，得一而盈；万物之生也，得一而生；侯王之正己以正天下也，无非得一以贞而已。纵或大小异象，贵贱殊途，表里精粗，幽明人鬼，至于不可穷诘，孰能外此一以为包罗哉？即如天至高也，无一将恐崩裂；地至厚也，无一将恐发决；神至妙也，无一将恐不灵；空谷传声，气至盈也，无一则恐竭矣；万物负形，气至繁也，无一则恐灭矣；侯王至高而至贵也，无一以贞天下，恐位高则危，名贵则败矣，是一安可忽乎？果能由一散万，浩荡无垠，渊深莫测，则天地神谷、万物侯王，俱赖此一以为主宰，而蟠天际地，弥纶无隙，充周不穷，如此其极，是高莫高于道，贵莫贵于一也。虽然，自无而有，有何高焉？由微而著，又何贵焉？即使贵莫与京，亦由气之自微而显，故曰「贵以贱为本」；即使高至无极也，亦由气之自下而上，故曰「高以下为基」。他如世之位高如侯，分贵如王，知道之自下而高、由贱而贵，故自称曰「孤」、曰「寡人」、曰「不穀」，此非以贱为本欤？否或不居于贱，自置太高，则中无主而道不立，心已纷而神不凝，欲于事事物物之间，合夫大中至正，复归于一道，盖亦鲜矣。犹推数车者，不能居中制外，反不如驱一车者之尚处其内而得以操纵自如。噫！有车而等于无车，贪多诚不如抱一。又如玉之琭琭而繁多，多则贱生焉；如石之落落而层叠，叠则危起焉，均太上所不欲也。何若抱一者之自贱而自下，后终至于高不可及、贵莫可言之为愈哉！

The Great Way has no extra tricks—it is simply the "One." What is this One? It is the primordial Breath before chaos divided, the Boundless before primal unity split, and the Supreme Ultimate that gives birth to the myriad things. In brief, while primordial Breath is formless, it is called the Boundless; when all things take shape out of the Boundless so that it becomes the root of all under heaven, it is called the Supreme Ultimate. This very reality is the Way; having no name to give it, the sages reluctantly called it the "One."

Without the One, nothing exists; without things, the One is nowhere revealed—gain it and you live, lose it and you perish. From the primordial beginning onward, everything that has attained the One to take form and figure, continuing endlessly in unbroken renewal, spans realms as countless as the sands of the Ganges at its grandest and enters a single speck of dust at its finest, leaving nothing outside. As the Doctrine of the Mean says, "Look for it and it cannot be seen; listen for it and it cannot be heard; yet it forms the substance of all things and omits none"—what is that if not this very One?

Summing it all up: heaven is clear because it attained the One; earth is tranquil because it attained the One; spirits are numinous because they attained the One; valleys are full because they attained the One; the myriad things live because they attained the One; and lords and kings straighten themselves so as to straighten the realm solely by attaining the One to stand upright. Though creatures differ in size and shape, diverge in noble or humble lot, span outer and inner, fine and coarse, unseen and visible, human and spirit—more varied than thought can ever exhaust—which of them can stand outside the embrace of this One?

Look from the opposite side: heaven is supremely high, yet without the One it would fear splitting apart; earth is supremely thick, yet without the One it would fear quaking and bursting; spirits are supremely subtle, yet without the One they would lose their efficacy; an empty valley echoes sound because breath fills it, yet without the One it would fear drying up; the myriad things carry diverse forms teeming with vitality, yet without the One they would fear extinction; lords and kings hold supreme height and honor, yet without the One to steady the realm, high station brings peril and exalted fame invites ruin. How could this One ever be neglected for a moment?

When the One unfolds into the ten thousand things—vast without shore, fathomless in depth—heaven, earth, spirits, valleys, all creatures, and rulers alike rely on this One as their master. Spanning heaven and reaching earth, woven seamlessly without a gap, filling all things without exhaustion, it reaches the utmost peak: nothing is higher than the Way, nothing more noble than the One.

Even so, emerging from non-being into being, what "height" did it have at the start? Growing from subtle faintness into manifest brilliance, what "nobility" did it possess at first? Even when its nobility is beyond all compare, it grew from primordial Breath rising from the faint to the manifest—hence "nobility takes the lowly as its root." Even when its height reaches the Boundless, it rose from primordial Breath ascending from below to above—hence "height takes the low as its foundation."

Look at worldly lords of lofty rank and kings of noble station: understanding that the Way rises from below to above and from humble to noble, they deliberately style themselves "the orphaned one," "the bereft one," and "the unworthy one." Is this not taking the lowly as their root? Conversely, if one refuses to dwell in humility and perches oneself too high, there is no steady master within and the Way cannot stand; the mind is already scattered and Spirit cannot gather. Trying amid the tangle of daily affairs to accord with the great center of uprightness and return to the single Way will then be rare indeed.

It is just like someone trying to push several carts at once: racing about without a center from which to steer, they fare far worse than a driver who sits calmly inside a single cart and guides it with ease. Alas, having many carts yet ending up with none—craving quantity truly cannot compare to embracing the One! Or like jade piled up in dazzling abundance (lulu)—too much of it turns cheap; or like rough stones stacked higher and higher (luoluo)—the higher the stack, the closer it leans to collapse. Lord Lao rejects both. How much better is the one who embraces the One, willingly taking a humble, lowly place, and in the end attaining a height none can match and a nobility words cannot express!

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言修道成真，只是此一，无有二也。孔子曰：「吾道一以贯之。」孟子曰：「夫道，一而已矣。」然究何一哉？古人谓：鸿鸿濛濛中，无念虑，无渣滓，一个虚而灵、寂而惺者之一物也。此物宽则包藏法界，窄则不立纤尘，显则九夷八荒无所不到，隐则纤芥微尘无所不察，所谓无极之极、不神之神，真无可名言、无从想象者。性命之道，惟此而已。太上以侯王喻人之心。心能常操常存，勿忘勿助，刻刻返观，时时内照，即不失其一。一即独也。独知独觉之地，戒慎恐惧，斯本来之至高至贵者，庶可长保。然此是修性之学，故一慎独便可了得。若炼命，则有为有作，倘非从下处做起，贱处炼来，药犹难得，何况金丹？下即下丹田也，贱即下部污秽处也。学者欲一阳来复，气势冲冲，非由下而升至于顶上，安得清刚之气，以为我长生至宝？非从下田浊乡，以神火下照，炼出至阳之气，何以为药本丹基？古人谓「阴中求阳，鬼窟盗宝」，洵不诬也。尤须有一心、无两念，方是守一之道。到得自然，人我俱忘，即得一矣。修士到此地位，一任天下事事物物，无不措之而咸宜，处之而恰当，所谓「得一而万事毕」，其信然耶！倘著形著象，纷纷驰逐，与夫七情六欲、身家妻孥，死死牵缠，不肯歇手，则去道远矣。莫说外物纷纭，不可言道，即如存心养性、修道炼丹，进火退符，采取封固，一切名目，皆是虚拟其象，为后之学者立一法程。若其心有丝毫未净，即为道障。太上所以说致数车无车，不欲琭琭如玉、落落如石焉。夫道只一道，学者又何事他求哉？

This passage teaches that cultivating the Way and attaining perfection comes down to this single "One," with no second path. Confucius said, "My Way is threaded together by a single One," and Mencius said, "The Way is One and nothing more." Yet what exactly is this One? The ancients described it as that single reality dwelling in primordial stillness—free of stray thought and worldly dregs, at once empty yet luminous, utterly silent yet brightly awake. Expanded, it encompasses the entire cosmos; contracted, it admits not a single speck of dust. Manifest, it reaches the farthest corners of the earth; hidden, it discerns the tiniest mustard seed or mote. This is the "utmost of the Boundless, the spirit beyond spirit," truly beyond speech and beyond imagination. The entire Way of inner nature and life lies right here.

Lord Lao uses "lords and kings" as a metaphor for the human heart-mind. When the mind is steadily held and gently preserved—neither forgotten nor forced—reflecting inward at every moment and shining within at every hour, it never loses its One. This One is also what Confucians call "solitude" (du): in that intimate space known and perceived by oneself alone, maintaining quiet awe and vigilance preserves our originally supreme and noble luminous nature for good.

Notice, however, that this belongs to the learning of "cultivating nature," where vigilance in solitude suffices to settle the matter directly. When it comes to "refining life," concrete work is involved: unless you start honestly from the "low" place and refine from the "humble" place, even the initial Medicine is hard to obtain, let alone the Golden Elixir! Here "low" refers to the lower Cinnabar Field, and "humble" refers to the lower bodily region ordinarily looked down upon as impure. If a student hopes for the return of one pure yang surging upward with full vitality, unless true Breath rises from the very bottom to the crown, how could one refine that clear, resilient vitality as a treasure of longevity? Unless one shines lucid spiritual fire down into the humble, turbid lower field to draw forth pure yang vitality, what could serve as the root of the Medicine and the foundation of the Elixir? The ancient saying, "Seek yang within yin; steal treasure from the ghost's cave," is completely true!

Above all, throughout this work one must keep "one undivided mind without a second thought"—that alone is guarding the center and embracing the One. When practice ripens into effortless naturalness and the boundary between self and other fades away, one has truly attained the One. Reaching this ground, no matter how tangled the affairs of the world may be, the practitioner handles every situation seamlessly and responds with perfect aptness—proving the truth of the saying, "Attain the One, and all affairs are accomplished."

By contrast, if you stay glued to outer forms and chase after fame, gain, sights, and sounds—entangled relentlessly in the seven emotions, six desires, and household anxieties without ever letting go—you are miles from the Way. Leave aside worldly distractions that disqualify one from speaking of the Way: even all the familiar cultivation terms we repeat—"preserving mind and nourishing nature," "cultivating the Way and refining the elixir," "advancing fire and withdrawing tallies," "harvesting and sealing"—are nothing more than provisional metaphors set up by past masters as stepping-stones for later learners! If the practitioner's heart clings to even a hair's breadth of attachment to these names and formulas, they immediately turn into an obstacle on the Way.

That is the deeper reason Lord Lao spoke of "counting carts until no cart remains" and warned against glittering like piles of jade or towering like stacked stones. The Way is simply one single Way—why should any seeker race about hunting elsewhere?

Key Terms

  • Attaining the One (deyi): Directly realizing and abiding in the undivided source of all things (the "One" being the Boundless, the Supreme Ultimate, and primordial undifferentiated Breath).
  • Vigilance in solitude (shendu): A core principle of cultivating inner nature shared by Confucianism and Daoism—maintaining crisp, watchful awareness in the private depths of one's own mind where no one else looks, never losing the innate One.
  • lower Cinnabar Field: The Cavity of Breath below the navel, corresponding here to "height takes the low as its foundation," signifying that refining life must lay its base and gently kindle warmth in the humble lower region.
  • Seeking yang within yin: Also called "stealing treasure from the ghost's cave," referring to shining lucid spiritual fire gently down into the yin, turbid lower region so that pure yang vitality (Medicine) is born out of utmost stillness.
  • Obstacle on the Way (daozhang): Any lingering mental attachment in practice—here specifically treating auxiliary labels like "refining the elixir, advancing fire, withdrawing tallies, harvesting and sealing" as solid things to cling to, contrary to embracing the One.

Putting It into Practice

  1. The higher you climb, the deeper you must keep your roots below. As rank, reputation, or skill grows, it is easy to float away from reality. Stay grounded in humility and hands-on basics so your height never turns precarious.
  2. Embracing the One beats chasing many. Pursuing ten goals or juggling five practices at once leaves you stretched thin like someone pushing multiple carts at once—having many yet steering none. Choose the single most essential thread and go deep, and everything else falls into place.
  3. Cultivate mind through solitude; strengthen body through basics. Watching over your intentions when no one is looking cultivates inner nature; steadily caring for unglamorous daily foundations—sleep, diet, and lower-body vitality—refines life.
  4. Do not let spiritual jargon trap you. Lineages, technical terms, and stages of practice are road signs, not trophies or burdens. Read them, put them to use, then set the labels down and return to an uncluttered, single mind.

Translator's Notes

  1. Orphaned, bereft, unworthy (gu, gua, bugu): Traditional self-deprecating pronouns used by ancient princes and kings: gu ("fatherless orphan"), gua ("scant in virtue and support"), and bugu ("unworthy," where gu means "good").
  2. "Counting carts until no cart remains," "glittering like jade," "stacked like stones": Standard editions of the Daodejing often read zhi shu yu wu yu (or zhi yu wu yu, "seeking too much praise ends with no praise") and write 珞珞 or 落落 for 洛洛. Huang Yuanji reads "many carts" as overextending oneself trying to drive several carts at once—and by extension clinging to myriad alchemical labels while losing the One—interpreting lulu jade as cheapening through excess and luoluo stones as risking collapse through stacking too high.
  3. "Look for it and it cannot be seen...": From the Doctrine of the Mean, originally praising the omnipresent workings of spirits and creation, cited here by Huang to describe the ubiquity of the One.
  4. "Beyond all compare" (gui mo yu jing): Jing means "great"; echoes the Zuo Commentary (Duke Zhuang, Year 22), "After eight generations, none shall match their greatness."
  5. "My Way is threaded together by a single One" / "The Way is One and nothing more": From the Analects ("Liren") and Mencius ("Teng Wengong I"), respectively.
  6. "Completely true" (xun bu wu ye): Xun means "truly, indeed"; wu means "false or baseless"—literally "indeed no empty claim."
  7. Textual note: In the classic text, 球球如玉 and 球，音禄 in the base edition are corrected to 琭琭如玉 and 琭，音禄 to match Huang's commentary below; OCR/printing errors in the commentary (including 鸿蒙, 不可穷诘, 弥纶, 贵莫与京, 一以贯之, 何一哉, 鸿鸿濛濛, 渣滓, 寂而惺, 不立纤尘, 无所不察, 污秽, 洵不诬也, 琭琭, 他求哉) have been collated against standard texts and context.
