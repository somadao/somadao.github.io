Chapter 45 · Great Perfection Seems Lacking

The deeper one's attainment, the less one sees any perfection or fullness in oneself; cultivating the Way takes emptiness as its principle, and progress lies not in piling up skills but in "effort reaching forgetting, and then forgetting forgetting"

About This Chapter

When people in the world learn a craft, the more skilled they become, the more they feel their abilities have grown; cultivating the Way is exactly the opposite—the closer one comes to completion, the more empty and ordinary one feels inside, with nothing at all to show off. Following "great perfection seems lacking, great fullness seems empty," Huang Yuanji offers a precise yardstick for telling true attainment from false:

  1. Why the more one attains, the more one feels "lacking" (§2, first half): While one's practice is still immature, one can clearly feel "I am applying effort, I have gained something," and so easily grows smug. Once one is truly one with the Way—I am the Way and the Way is me—it is like a fish forgetting rivers and lakes: one can no longer see where any "perfection" or "fullness" lies.
  2. How clarity and stillness transform cold and heat and set the world right (§2, second half): The classic says, "Movement overcomes cold, stillness overcomes heat; clarity and stillness are the standard for the world." Huang points out that true clarity and stillness is not forcibly suppressing agitation, but an inner mind clear, luminous, and empty, returned to profound, peaceful harmony—when upright Breath gathers within, outer cold and heat naturally cannot shake it.
  3. Cultivating the Way is not one of the hundred crafts (§3): This is the finishing touch of the whole chapter. Learning a craft means "adding things" every day; cultivating the Way means "forgetting things" every day—practice reaches progress only when you forget that you are practicing, and enters the realm of transformation only when you forget even the "forgetting" (forgetting forgetting).

Safety note: In this chapter's commentary, the phrase "one can withstand the coldest nine-day spells of winter and forget the three dog-day periods of summer" describes the improved resilience of body and mind when stillness is full; it is traditional alchemical praise for a state of harmony between body and mind. Never take it as a reason to deliberately endure freezing cold or hunger in severe winter or summer heat, to refuse warmth or protection from heat, or to forcibly drive the inner "lead fire." Everyday cultivation is about clarity, stillness, and calm, and keeping a routine in step with the seasons.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：大成若缺，其用不敝。大盈若冲，其用不穷。大直若屈，大巧若拙，大辩若讷。躁胜寒，静胜热，清静为天下正。

Lord Lao says: The most complete achievement looks as though something is lacking, yet its usefulness never wears out; the fullest state looks as though it were empty and unfilled, yet its usefulness is never exhausted. What is most straight looks bent; the most consummate skill looks clumsy; the greatest eloquence looks tongue-tied and slow of speech. Activity overcomes cold; calm steadiness dispels heat; and keeping clear, still, and free of contrivance is the true standard for settling the world.

§2
Original (Chinese)

道本虚无自然，顺天而动，率性以行，一与天地同其造化，日月同其升恒，无有而无不有，无为而无不为也。当大道未成未盈之时，不无作为之迹，犹有形象可窥，觉得自满自足，不胜欣然。乃至大成之候，又似缺陷弥多，大成反若无成焉；大盈之余，又似冲漠无状，大盈反若未盈焉。是岂愈学而愈劣，愈优而愈绌乎？非也。盖道本人生固有之良，清空无物，静定无痕。一当形神俱妙，与道合真，我即道，道即我，有何成何盈之有？若使有成有盈，犹是与道为二，未底神化之域。是以修道之士，愈有愈无，愈多愈少，绝不见有成与盈也，故大成若缺，大盈若冲。以故万象咸空，一真独抱，因物为缘，随时自应，诚塞乎天地，贯乎古今，放之而皆准也，其用岂有敝哉？其用岂有穷哉？当其心空似海，神静如岳，又觉毫无足用者然。及其浩气常伸，至刚至大，抑何直也，乃反觉屈郁之难堪。神妙无方，可常可变，抑何巧也，乃惟觉愚拙之无知。言近指远，词约理微，非义不言，非时不语，辩何大乎，而总觉讷讷然，如不能出诸口。惟其如屈、如拙、如讷若此，是以心愈虚，志愈下，德愈广，业愈崇焉。此殆道返虚无，学归自在，一与天地之运转而不知，日月之往来而不觉，所以其成大且久也。若皆本太极之理，顺阴阳之常，久久薰蒸，铅火充盈，寒数九而堪御，蒲团镇定，伏经三而可忘，太上所谓「躁胜寒，静胜热」者，其即此欤？至于清明在躬，虚灵无物，一归浑穆之天，概属和平之象，又何躁、何寒、何静、何热之有哉？学者具清静之心，化寒暑之节，而吾身之正气凝，即天下之正道立矣，又何患旁门之迭出耶？

The Great Way is by nature empty and spontaneous: it moves in accord with Heaven and acts by following its nature, sharing fully in the creative transformations of heaven and earth and in the rising and constancy of sun and moon. It seems to possess nothing, yet there is nothing it does not hold; it seems to do nothing, yet there is nothing it does not accomplish.

When the Great Way has not yet been cultivated to completion and fullness, one inevitably still bears traces of deliberate effort and can still clearly see some image and result of one's practice; at such times one is most likely to feel very fulfilled and satisfied, delighted inside. But once one truly reaches the stage of "great perfection," one feels instead that one's shortcomings keep multiplying, so that great perfection looks as if nothing had been achieved; and on reaching "great fullness," one feels instead that the heart is spacious, detached, and without any trace, so that great fullness looks as if it were not full at all.

Does this mean that the more one learns the worse one gets, and the more excellent one becomes the clumsier? Not at all! For the Great Way is the innate goodness and capacity with which everyone is born, originally clear and empty of anything, still and settled without a trace. Once body and spirit both reach the marvelous and one merges with the Way in truth, I am the Way and the Way is me, undivided—so how could any thought of "I have perfected it" or "I am full" remain? As long as one still feels "I have achieved something" or "I am already full," that shows "I" and "the Way" are still two separate pieces and one has not truly entered the realm of spiritual transformation.

Therefore those who truly cultivate the Way feel all the more empty the more they possess, and all the more scant the richer they become; they never see any "perfection" or "fullness" in themselves—this is "great perfection seems lacking, great fullness seems empty." And precisely because all phenomena are empty and one embraces only that single point of original truth (guarding the center and embracing the One), responding naturally to things at every moment according to their conditions, such merit truly fills heaven and earth, runs through past and present, and holds true wherever it is applied—how could its usefulness ever wear out? How could it ever be exhausted?

When a person's mind is as empty as the ocean and spirit as still as a high mountain, they feel instead that they are capable of nothing and have nothing worth showing. Their inner flood-like upright Breath constantly extends, supremely great and supremely firm—how straight indeed! Yet they often feel humbled and restrained, unwilling to show off ("great straightness seems bent"). Their responses are marvelous without limit, fitting whether keeping to the constant or adapting to change—how skillful indeed! Yet they feel only dull and clumsy, knowing nothing ("great skill seems clumsy"). Their words are plain and near yet their meaning deep and far, brief in wording yet subtle in principle; they do not speak unless it is right, do not talk unless the time is ripe—how great their eloquence! Yet to others they always seem slow of tongue, barely able to get the words out ("great eloquence seems tongue-tied"). Precisely because they can be bent, clumsy, and tongue-tied like this, their minds grow ever emptier, their bearing ever lower, their virtue ever wider, and their achievements ever loftier. This is perhaps the state in which the Way returns to emptiness and learning returns to great freedom: like heaven and earth turning day and night without knowing it, and sun and moon coming and going without being aware of it—therefore the achievement is both vast and lasting.

If in ordinary times one nurtures oneself in keeping with the natural principles of the Supreme Ultimate and of yin and yang, warmly steeping day after day and month after month until the true yang within (Lead and Mercury and fire phasing) is abundant and flowing, one can calmly withstand severe cold even in the coldest nine-day spells of winter; sitting firmly settled on the meditation cushion, free of dry fire in the heart, one can forget the scorching heat even in the three dog-day periods of high summer. Is this not perhaps what Lord Lao means by "movement overcomes cold, stillness overcomes heat"—the work of harmonizing yin and yang?

To speak of a still higher level: when body and mind become truly clear and transparent, empty, luminous, and free of anything, everything returning to a state of undivided, mellow profundity, the whole being one mass of peaceful harmony—at that point, where would there be any opposition of agitation and cold, stillness and heat? When students hold this clear, still heart, they can melt away the imbalances of the cold and hot seasons; as long as the flood-like upright Breath of their own person gathers firmly, the upright Way of the world stands up along with it—why then fear the endless rise of side doors and deviant paths?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此明道本至虚至无，至平至常。人未造虚无之境、平常之域，只觉其盈，不见其缺；只觉其优，不见其绌，所以太上云：「少则得，多则惑。」谚云：「洪钟无声，满瓶不响。」洵不虚也。大德不德，是以有德；大为无为，是以有为，非谦词也。道原虚无一气，惟其有得，是以无得，惟其无得，是为有得。故道愈高，心愈下，德弥大，志弥卑，斯与道大适焉。若一有所长，便诩诩然，骄盈矜夸，傲物凌人，其无道无德，大可见矣。太上故云：「为学日益，为道日损，损之又损，以至于无」方为得之。学者切勿视修道炼丹，一如百工技艺之术，自觉有益，斯为进境。若修道，总以虚无为宗，功至于忘，进矣，至于忘忘，已归化境。夫以学道之士，退则进，弱则强，虚为盈，无为有，以反为正，以减为增，故学之进与不进，惟视心之忘与不忘耳。

This chapter makes clear that the Great Way is by nature supremely empty and supremely void, supremely plain and supremely ordinary. Before reaching the realm of emptiness and ordinariness, people tend to fix their eyes only on their own "fullness" and fail to see their own "lack," to see only their own "excellence" and not their own "shortcomings." That is why Lord Lao said earlier, "Having little, one gains; having much, one is confused." A common saying also goes, "The great bell is silent unstruck; the full bottle makes no sound when shaken." How true that is!

The highest virtue does not regard itself as virtuous, and therefore truly has virtue; the greatest deeds show no trace of deliberate doing, and therefore truly accomplish something—this is by no means a polite, modest-sounding phrase. The Great Way is originally the single empty, undivided One Breath of Prior Heaven. Precisely because one has truly obtained it, there is no hindrance in the mind of "I have obtained it"; and precisely because the mind has no such hindrance or clinging, one has truly obtained it. So the higher one's attainment in the Way, the lower one holds one's heart; the greater one's virtue, the humbler one's bearing—only then does one fully accord with the Great Way. If someone has the slightest special talent and at once becomes complacent, boastful, and contemptuous of others, it is plain at a glance that there is neither Way nor virtue in their heart.

That is why Lord Lao says in a later chapter: "In pursuing learning one adds daily; in pursuing the Way one diminishes daily; diminishing and diminishing again until one reaches non-action"—only then has one truly attained the Way. Students must never treat cultivating the Way and refining the Elixir as if they were carpenters or smiths learning a craft. In learning a craft, one counts as progressing when one feels one has learned more techniques and gained more ability; but cultivating the Way always takes "emptiness" as its principle: only when practice reaches the point of forgetting that one is practicing has one truly progressed, and only when even the thought of "forgetting" is forgotten without residue has one truly entered the realm of transformation.

For the student of the Way, retreating is advancing, softness is strength, emptiness is fullness, non-being is true being; going back is the right road, and diminishing is increasing. Therefore, to measure whether one's cultivation has really advanced, look at nothing else—look only at whether this heart of yours can truly "forget" or not!

Key Terms

  • One Breath of Prior Heaven (the single empty Breath): This chapter points out that the substance of the Way is originally empty and undivided, so "precisely because one has obtained, one has not obtained"; merged with the Way, one sees no trace of perfection or fullness.
  • Guarding the center and embracing the One (embracing the single truth alone): All phenomena being empty and only one point of original truth embraced, one can respond to things according to their conditions and naturally at every moment, so that its usefulness is never exhausted.
  • Lead and Mercury and fire phasing (abundance of the lead fire): Alchemists use "lead fire" as a figure for the warm, abundant state of yang Breath within; Huang Yuanji further points out that the high state of clarity, stillness, and calm transcends the opposition of cold and heat, agitation and stillness.
  • Effort reaching forgetting, and then forgetting forgetting: Huang Yuanji's core standard for measuring progress in the Way—forgetting deliberate contrivance is progress, and only when even the attachment to "I am forgetting" falls away completely does one enter the realm of natural transformation.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Give yourself a check-up with "the full bottle makes no sound." The moments when you most want to prove how much you know, how capable you are, or how steady your concentration is are often exactly when your heart is still a "half-full bottle sloshing around." Genuinely solid ability and cultivation are mostly quiet and need no advertising.
  2. Do not treat calming the mind as "collecting points." "Never treat cultivating the Way and refining the Elixir as one of the hundred crafts"—how long you sat today, what sensations came up, how many terms you memorized are not metrics for comparison or bookkeeping. Feeling lighter, looser, and less cluttered with thoughts after practice is the real progress.
  3. Leave a little "clumsiness" and "slowness of tongue" in how you speak and act. There is no need to rush to show off your wit and eloquence on every occasion. "Do not speak unless it is right, do not talk unless the time is ripe"—saying less that is idle or cutting saves mental energy and lets a sense of depth settle in.
  4. When faced with cold or heat, urgency or agitation, return first to "clarity and stillness." Agitated emotions are like scorching summer, low spirits like severe winter; rather than struggling hard against the emotion itself, stop and even out your breathing, letting the mind return to its clear, peaceful ground of "clarity and stillness."

Translator's Notes

  1. "The rising and constancy of sun and moon": An adaptation of the Classic of Poetry ("Lesser Odes," Tianbao): "Like the constancy of the moon, like the rising of the sun"—meaning like the sun just rising, like the waxing moon growing full, never declining.
  2. "One can withstand the coldest nine-day spells and forget the three dog-day periods": Shujiu ("counting the nines") refers to the bitterly cold season counted in nine-day spells from the winter solstice; sanfu ("the three dog-day periods") refers to the scorching first, middle, and last fu periods after the summer solstice.
  3. "Having little, one gains; having much, one is confused": Quoted from Chapter 22 of the Daodejing.
  4. "Great virtue does not regard itself as virtuous, and therefore has virtue": An adaptation of Chapter 38 of the Daodejing: "Highest virtue does not regard itself as virtuous, and therefore has virtue."
  5. "In pursuing learning one adds daily; in pursuing the Way one diminishes daily; diminishing and diminishing again until one reaches nothing": Quoted from Chapter 48 of the Daodejing (where the original reads "until one reaches non-action").
  6. Textual note: A stray dash misread as 一 in the base-text title has been deleted; in the classic text, 太上日 has been corrected to 太上曰; in the commentary, 开恒 has been corrected to 升恒, 岂有努哉 to 岂有穷哉, 出请口 to 出诸口, and 多则感 to 多则惑.
