Chapter 47 · Accomplishing Without Doing

To fathom the principle within oneself is to fathom the principle of the whole world; true cultivation prizes turning back to seek within and vigilance in solitude—acting within non-action and awakening within non-knowing, so as neither to fall into blank emptiness nor to cling to contrived doing

About This Chapter

"Without going out of the door, one knows the world; without peering through the window, one sees the Way of Heaven" is often misread as shutting oneself away and rejecting outside knowledge. Huang Yuanji brings out its true meaning: it speaks of the work of turning the gaze inward, fathoming principle and fully realizing one's nature, not of refusing to know the world:

  1. Why "the nearer, the farther; the more concise, the broader" (§2): The cosmos and the human body spring from one source (Heaven and humanity are one continuum). If you abandon your own original mind and run about outside all day asking after secrets, the more you run, the more confused you become. Conversely, once you first cultivate your own mind and nature through and through, so that spirit settles and wisdom arises, the patterns of the world and of human hearts and the Way of Heaven in the waxing and waning of yin and yang become naturally clear within the Square Inch.
  2. Vigilance in solitude is the gate of inner knowing (§2, latter part): Only by stilling thoughts, forgetting scheming, and not deceiving oneself in the hidden and subtle places others cannot see or hear will one avoid scattering one's resolve by clinging to surface knowledge and views or by vain self-display.
  3. The harmonious middle way between non-action and action (§3): Huang Yuanji particularly warns that Laozi's "non-action" is by no means withered wood and dead ashes. Non-action corresponds to inner nature (Original Spirit), and acting corresponds to the decree of life (the first stirring of the One Breath of Prior Heaven); uniting true Breath with True Intention, there is acting within non-action and awareness within non-knowing, and finally emptiness and form are both forgotten—only then does one avoid clinging to one side and sliding into side doors.

Safety note: The second section of this chapter discusses the internal-alchemy principles of "using True Intention to gather true Breath" and "dual cultivation of nature and life." Never misunderstand "acting and doing" as forcibly guiding or driving the vital current in the body with the mind, nor turn "non-action" into dull, drowsy sitting cut off from real life. In daily practice, simply keep clear, wakeful awareness within relaxed quiet.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：不出户，知天下；不窥牖，见天道。其出弥远，其知弥少。是以圣人不行而知，不见而名，不为而成。

Lord Lao says: Without stepping out of the door, one can understand the great trends and principles of the world; without poking one's head out to look through the window, one can see through the laws by which the Way of Heaven turns. On the contrary, the farther one keeps rushing outward, the less true knowledge one actually comprehends. Therefore the sage who has attained the Way understands things without running about everywhere, explains reality fully without looking around in all directions, and accomplishes great works without deliberate contrivance.

§2
Original (Chinese)

君子万物皆备，不出户庭以修其身，而世道之变迁，人心之更易，与夫推亡固存、反乱为治之机，无不洞晰于方寸。此岂术数为之哉？良以物我同源，穷一己之理，即能尽天下之理，是以不出户而知天下也。古人造化由心，不开窗牖以韬其光，而无言之帝载，不息之天命，与夫生长收藏、阴阳造化之妙，无不了彻于怀。此岂揣摹得之哉？亦以天人一贯，修吾身之命，即能契帝天之命，是以不窥牖而见天道也。若遨游他乡，咨询天下之故，交接良友，讲求天命之微，未尝不有所知，吾恐不求诸己而求诸人，不索之内而索之外，纵有所知，较之务近者，为更少矣。故曰：「其出弥远，其知弥少」焉。明明道在户牖之间，奈何舍近而图远耶？孟子曰：「言近指远者，善言也；守约施博者，善道也。」以此思之，为学愈近愈远，弥约弥博，近与约，安可忽乎哉？是以圣人抱一涵三，观空习定，身不出门庐，足不履廛市，木石与居，鹿豕与游，一步不移，一人不友，似乎孤寂矣，而神定则慧生，虽不行而胜于行者多矣，虽无知而胜于知者远矣。凡人以所见为务，圣人则不见是图，故终日乾乾，惟于不睹不闻之地，息虑忘机，莫见莫显之间，戒欺求慊，只有内知，绝无外见，似乎杳冥矣，而无极则有生，虽不见而弥彰矣，虽无名而愈著矣。至于天下人物之繁，幽冥鬼神之奥，皆此无为之道为之，有伦而有要，成始以成终。所患者，拘于知觉，著于名象，功好矜持，心多见解，致令此志纷驰，不能一德，此心夹杂，不如太虚，所以道不成而德不就，无惑乎枉劳一世精神，终无所得也。若此者，以之治世，不能顺理成章，无为而天下自归画一；以之修身，不能炼虚合道，无为而此身自获成真。彼徒外求，奚益耶？故君子惟慎其独，而人道之要，天命之原，有不求而自知者。

Within the original mind of the noble person, the principles of all things are already complete. Even without stepping out of the door, quietly cultivating themselves, they see with perfect clarity in their own Square Inch of heart the rise and fall and changes of the world, the shifting allegiances of human hearts, and even the key to how to sustain what survives and how to set disorder right. Is this calculated by divination, fortune-telling, or other numerological arts? Certainly not! It is truly because all things and I come from the same source: once one has thoroughly fathomed the principles of nature and life within oneself, one naturally penetrates the principles of the whole world—this is "without going out of the door, one knows the world."

The ancient sages understood that all creative transformation originates in the one mind. Even with windows tightly shut and their brilliance withdrawn (Reverting the Light to shine within), they still fully grasped in their breasts the soundless workings of the Way of Heaven, the ceaseless decree of the cosmos, and the marvels of yin-yang nurture—sprouting in spring, growing in summer, harvesting in autumn, storing in winter. Was this obtained by groundless guessing and speculation? Certainly not! It is truly because the Way of Heaven and the human body are of one continuous vein: once one has cultivated and understood the nature and life of one's own person, one naturally accords with the great decree of heaven and earth's creation—this is "without peering through the window, one sees the Way of Heaven."

If one spends all one's days wandering through distant lands inquiring into the lore of the world, and everywhere befriending famous teachers and worthy companions to discuss the mysteries of Heaven's decree, it is not that one gains nothing at all. I only fear that in this way one forgets to verify within one's own heart, merely hoping to get answers from others' mouths and searching here and there outside oneself; even with a bellyful of things seen and heard, compared with those who steadily turn the gaze inward, one has actually penetrated far fewer principles! That is why Laozi says, "The farther one goes out, the less one knows." The Great Way is plainly right within one's own door and window, within one's present body and mind—why insist on forsaking the near to seek the far?

Mencius said: "Words that are plain and near yet point to what is far are good words; holding to what is concise yet applying it broadly is the good Way." Reflecting carefully along these lines: in learning and cultivating the Way, the closer one stays to one's own original mind, the farther one sees; the more concise what one holds, the broader its use—how can the two words "near" and "concise" be taken lightly?

For this very reason, the sage guards the center and embraces the One, nurturing the Three Treasures of Essence, Breath, and Spirit, contemplating emptiness and practicing stillness. Their body does not leave the thatched hut, their feet do not tread the noisy marketplace; like the great Shun dwelling in seclusion deep in the mountains, living among trees and rocks and keeping company with deer and wild boars, they do not move a single step outward nor deliberately cultivate a single acquaintance, and on the surface seem very lonely indeed. Yet precisely because the mind and Spirit are settled, true wisdom arises: though they do not go out of the door, their insight far surpasses that of those who travel ten thousand li; though they seem to know nothing, their discernment far surpasses that of those who show off their learning!

Ordinary people spend their whole lives fixed on the appearances their eyes can see, while the sage works precisely where there is no form or image: diligent and self-strengthening all day long ("all day long, active and vigilant"), they still anxious thoughts and forget scheming in the hidden and subtle places others cannot see or hear, and in the places most prone to slackness and concealment they guard against self-deception and seek inner ease. In their hearts there is only clear "inner knowing," never any craving for showy "outer seeing." On the surface it seems a blank stillness of the dim and obscure, yet out of the deep stillness of the Boundless, endless true vitality is born—not deliberately looking, the truth becomes all the clearer; not deliberately naming, the Great Way becomes all the more manifest.

As for the abundance of human relations and all things in the world, and the mysteries of hidden creative transformation, all of it is this "Way of non-action" turning naturally, with order and with essentials, from beginning to end. The fault most to be feared is being boxed in by fragmentary posterior perception, clinging rigidly to names and appearances, putting on airs and growing self-satisfied after a little practice, and cramming the head with all sorts of subjective opinions, so that one's resolve rushes off in every direction and cannot stay single in virtue, and the heart is a jumble rather than pure like the Great Void. As a result the Way is not cultivated and virtue does not stand—no wonder one wastes a lifetime of vital spirit in vain and gains nothing in the end! Governing the world with such a restless, outward-seeking mind, one cannot let the world settle naturally and smoothly into peace; cultivating oneself with it, one cannot refine emptiness to merge with the Way and let body and mind become truly pure and complete. What use is it merely to seek outside? Therefore the noble person works only at "vigilance in solitude," and the essentials of the human Way and the root source of Heaven's decree become clear of themselves without any need to force a search outside.

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言道以无为为宗，慎独为要，则无为而无不为，无知而无不知矣，然非枯木槁灰以无为也。吾前云「万象咸空，一灵独照」，此为真意。又曰「一觉而动，一阳发生」，是为元气。采药炼丹，不过炼此性命二者。若无真意，性将何依？若无真气，命由何修？以真意采真气，两者浑化为一，即返于太极之初，斯谓之丹。故无为之中，又要有作有为，无知之内，又要有知有觉，方不堕空、不著有。迨至功力弥深，空即是色，色即是空，久之空色两忘，浑然物化，斯与道大适矣。不知人道，观天道可知。孔子曰：「天何言哉？四时行，百物生」，即是无为之为，斯为至道之精。盖无为，是天性；有作，是天命。无知，是元神；有觉，是元气。天地间，非二则不化，非一则不神。神而不神，不神而神，斯得一而两、神而化之妙境焉。此非吾言所能罄也，在尔修士，长养虚静，常守虚灵，斯性命常存，而大道可成矣。切勿以无为、有为，各执一边，虽正宗也，而旁蹊开焉。请各自揣量可也。

To sum up this chapter: cultivating the Way takes "non-action" as its fundamental principle and "vigilance in solitude" as its crucial handhold; in this way one can achieve non-action with nothing left undone, and non-knowing with nothing left unknown. But do not misunderstand—this is by no means telling you to sit foolishly like withered wood and dead ashes in some deathly-still "non-action"!

I said earlier, "All phenomena are empty, and one point of luminous awareness shines alone"—this is True Intention. I also said, "In utmost stillness a single awakening stirs, and one yang arises"—this is prior-heaven original Breath (One Breath of Prior Heaven). What alchemists call gathering the Medicine and refining the Elixir comes down, in plain terms, to nothing more than cultivating these two: inner nature and life. Without True Intention, where would inner nature rest? Without true Breath, by what would life be cultivated? Using pure True Intention to illumine and unite with the spontaneously stirring true Breath, letting the two merge seamlessly into one and return to the beginning of the Supreme Ultimate before division—this is called the "Golden Elixir."

Therefore, against the great background of "non-action," there must also be "acting and doing" that moves in step with the opportune moment; within the utmost stillness of "non-knowing," there must also be the unclouded luminosity of "knowing and awareness." Only then will one neither fall into the stagnant pool of blank emptiness nor cling to the contrived "being" of the posterior realm. As one's practice grows ever deeper, one will realize the perfect harmony the Buddhists describe as "emptiness is form, form is emptiness"; and in time, even the concepts of "emptiness" and "form" are completely forgotten, and one merges seamlessly into one with all things—only then does one truly accord with the Great Way.

If you do not understand this principle of the human Way, just look up at the Way of Heaven and it will be clear. Confucius said, "Does Heaven say anything? Yet the four seasons run their course and the hundred things are born." This is "the doing of non-action," the very essence of the supreme Way. Put simply, "non-action" corresponds to one's heavenly nature, and "acting" corresponds to one's heavenly decree of life; "non-knowing" corresponds to the still, thought-free Original Spirit, and "awareness" corresponds to the vigorously burgeoning original Breath. Between heaven and earth, without the two sides of yin and yang nothing can be nurtured into being, and without the "One" returned to the Supreme Ultimate nothing can be harmoniously marvelous. Marvelous yet not clinging to the marvelous, seemingly not marvelous yet marvelous everywhere—this is entering the highest realm of one dividing into two, two uniting into one, and marvelous transformative nurture.

This flavor is truly not something my words can exhaust; it depends entirely on each of you who study and cultivate to nourish emptiness and stillness over the long term and constantly guard that point of empty, unclouded luminosity. Then nature and life will naturally endure, and the Great Way will come to completion as surely as water forms a channel. Remember: never split "non-action" and "action" apart and rigidly hold to only one side! Even if you begin on the orthodox Great Way, the moment you cling to one extreme, the forking paths of side doors and deviant ways open at once. Please each weigh this carefully and take it to heart!

Key Terms

  • Square Inch: The luminous ground of the human heart; Huang Yuanji stresses that "all things and I come from the same source," so by turning the gaze inward and reflecting within the Square Inch, one can perceive the principles of the world and the Way of Heaven.
  • Guarding the center and embracing the One (embracing the One and containing the Three): Guarding the empty, still mind so that the Three Treasures of Essence, Breath, and Spirit are naturally held together without scattering.
  • Original Spirit and True Intention: In this chapter Huang Yuanji clearly distinguishes and links the two—empty, still non-knowing is Original Spirit (heavenly nature); the solitary illumination of utmost stillness that moves with the opportune moment to unite with true Breath is True Intention; uniting it with true Breath (the decree of life) as one completes the alchemical Way.
  • Inner nature and life: Non-action belongs to heavenly nature, acting belongs to the heavenly decree of life; the dual cultivation of nature and life requires non-action and action to be harmoniously rooted in each other, neither to be neglected.
  • Vigilance in solitude: The key to self-cultivation of not deceiving oneself, stilling thoughts, and forgetting scheming in the hidden, subtle place of a single thought where no one sees or hears; it is the master pivot for penetrating Heaven and humanity from the inside out.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Hoard less "useful content" frantically from outside; ask your own heart for answers more often. The information age makes it easiest to fall into "the farther one goes out, the less one knows"—saving hundreds of notes and following countless master classes without ever once sitting quietly to observe your own real emotions and the stirring of your thoughts. Ten minutes a day looking inward beats ten hours of blindly scrolling outward.
  2. Use "holding to the concise, applying it broadly" to subtract. Taking one or two of the most fundamental core habits (such as regular sleep, calm breathing, pausing three seconds before reacting) and practicing them steadily and deeply is far more effective than trying ten flashy methods at once.
  3. Your state when alone is your real level of practice. When no one is watching, do you still avoid cutting corners, deceiving yourself, and indulging stray thoughts? Treat "vigilance in solitude" as your daily training ground, and your steadiness will grow fastest.
  4. Neither "give up and lie flat" nor "push hard out of anxiety." Apply Huang Yuanji's "not clinging to either side of non-action and action" to daily life: keep the ground of your mind relaxed and free of anxiety (non-action), focus on acting in step with the rhythm when it is time to act (acting), and let go once it is done—that is a higher kind of ease.

Translator's Notes

  1. "All things are complete within": From Mencius ("Jinxin I"): "All things are complete within me. To turn back to oneself and find sincerity—there is no greater joy."
  2. "Words that are plain and near yet point to what is far are good words; holding to what is concise yet applying it broadly is the good Way": From Mencius ("Jinxin II").
  3. "Living among trees and rocks, keeping company with deer and wild boars": Adapted from Mencius ("Jinxin I"): "When Shun lived deep in the mountains, he lived among trees and rocks and kept company with deer and wild boars."
  4. "All day long, active and vigilant": From the Book of Changes (hexagram Qian, Nine in the Third Place): "The noble person is active and vigilant all day long, and in the evening still wary as if in danger; no blame," describing constant diligence and self-watchfulness.
  5. "Where unseen, where unheard" and "nothing more visible, nothing more manifest": From the Doctrine of the Mean (Ch. 1): "Be cautious and watchful where one is not seen, fearful and apprehensive where one is not heard. Nothing is more visible than the hidden, nothing more manifest than the subtle; therefore the noble person is vigilant in solitude."
  6. "Does Heaven say anything? Yet the four seasons run their course and the hundred things are born": From the Analects ("Yanghuo"), where the original reads: "Does Heaven say anything? The four seasons run their course, the hundred things are born—does Heaven say anything?"
  7. Textual note: A leftover footnote from the previous chapter ("① 之，底本作『知』，改") and the garbled page number "n7" mixed into the base text have been deleted; in the classic text, 太上日 has been corrected to 太上曰; in the commentary, the 已 in 穷一已 and 求诸已 has been corrected to 己, 不出户而知下也 has had 天 supplied according to the classic text to read 知天下也, 不石与居 has been corrected to 木石与居, 香冥 to 杳冥, and 又日 to 又曰.
