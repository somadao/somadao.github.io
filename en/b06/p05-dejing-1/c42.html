Chapter 42 · Blending Breath into Harmony

The Boundless gives birth to the Supreme Ultimate, and yin and yang unite with True Intention to form the triad; refining the elixir prizes emptiness, stillness, and humble softness, strictly warning against forcing circulation without medicine or pressing ahead with aggressive force

About This Chapter

"The Way gives birth to One; One gives birth to Two; Two gives birth to Three; Three gives birth to the myriad things" is the Daodejing's most celebrated cosmological statement. Dissatisfied with forcing this numerical sequence onto the "generating numbers of the Five Phases," Huang Yuanji maps it straight onto the generative sequence and fire-phasing of internal alchemy:

  1. From cosmic creation to the human Three Treasures (§2–§3): The "Way" is the Boundless void; "One" is the Supreme Ultimate stirring out of utmost stillness (prior-heaven original Essence / inner nature); "Two" is the differentiation of yin and yang, stillness and motion (prior-heaven original Breath / life); and "Three" is yin and yang united with the centering presidency of Original Spirit and True Intention. The character chong in "blending breath into harmony" means "center": without harmonious central Breath and True Intention presiding at the center, yin and yang scatter, neither able to generate creatures nor able to form the Elixir.
  2. Losing before gaining; preserving the Elixir through humility (§2, middle): Princes and kings style themselves "orphaned, bereft, and unworthy" because fullness inevitably spills and height invites peril. Cultivation works the same way: one must first diminish thoughts and clinging "day after day" until one yang returns in deep stillness; smugness and haste immediately let the cognitive spirit spark stray fire and spoil the work.
  3. Stern warning against "violent force" and "blind refining" (§2, latter half): Huang issues one of his sharpest practical warnings here—before attaining the Elixir, wait in empty stillness; after attaining it, nourish it with gentle softness. Trying to force circulation while filled with stray thoughts, or cultivating with fierce, aggressive tension, is "blind refining" over an empty furnace—not only futile, but harmful to both inner nature and vital essence.

Safety note: This chapter mentions traditional internal-alchemy terms such as "turning the waterwheel," "harvesting and simmering," "fire phasing," and "warding off illness and extending life." Classical phrases about "warding off illness and extending life" reflect traditional cultural idiom and are not a substitute for modern medical diagnosis or treatment; "harvesting" and "circulating" are advanced alchemical metaphors, and Huang Yuanji himself explicitly warns here that "rashly attempting harvesting, refining, and circulation without medicine or elixir is empty burning that injures inner nature and essence." In daily practice, never deliberately force breath along pathways, hold the breath with strain, or chase bodily sensations—simply keep body and mind relaxed and stillness natural. If head pressure, agitation, heat, chest tightness, or palpitations arise, stop sitting at once and stand up to move gently.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：道生一，一生二，二生三，三生万物。万物负阴而抱阳，冲气以为和。人之所恶，孤、寡、不谷，而王公以为称。故物或损之而益，或益之而损。人之所教，我亦教之。强梁者不得其死，吾将以为教父。（父与甫同，从上声，言其众也，将以之教众人也。）

Lord Lao says: The vacuous Way gives birth to the undivided One of the Supreme Ultimate; the One divides into the Two of yin and yang; yin and yang interacting with the harmonizing central breath give birth to the Three; and from the Three arise all things in the universe. Every creature carries yin at its back and embraces yang in front, relying on the centrally blending Breath to reach harmony.

What people hate most are the names "orphaned," "bereft," and "unworthy," yet kings and dukes take these very words as their titles. For such is the law of things: at times diminishing brings gain, and at times adding brings loss.

What others teach, I too teach: the violent and overbearing never meet a good end. I will take this warning as the foremost teaching for the multitude. (Huang's note: fu 父 is interchangeable with fu 甫 in the rising tone, meaning "the multitude"—meaning "I will use this to teach all people.")

§2
Original (Chinese)

道家始终修炼，惟以虚无为宗。元始天王，道号虚无自然，即是此义。由虚而实，是谓真实；由无而有，是谓真有。倘不虚不无，非但七情六欲，窒塞真灵本体，无以应万事、化阳神，即观空了照，有一点强忍意气持之，亦是以心治心，直将本来面目遮蔽无存。总之，虚无者，道之体；冲和者，道之用。人能如是，道庶几矣。太上曰：「道生一。」道何有哉？虚而已矣。然至虚之中，一气萌动，天地生焉，故曰：「有物混成，先天地生」也。无极之先，混混沌沌，只是一虚；及动化为阳，静化为阴，即「易有太极，是生两仪」是，所谓「道生一，一生二」也。其在人身，即微茫之中，一觉而动，乾坤阖辟，气机往来，静而凝聚者，为阴为精；动而流行者，为阳为气。若无真意主之，则阴阳散乱，无由生人而成道。可见阴阳二气之间，甚赖元神真意主持其际，所谓「二生三」也。由是一阴一阳，一动一静，气化流行，主宰如故，而万物生生不穷矣，所谓「三生万物」也。或曰：「天一生水，金生水也；地二生火，木生火也；天三生木，水生木也；地四生金，土生金也。」以五行所生，解太上「一二三万物」生生之义，总属牵强。不若道为无极，一为太极，二为阴阳，天一地二合而成三，斯为明确之论。「万物负阴而抱阳，冲气以为和」，明明道元始虚无一气，化生阴阳，万物之生，即阴阳为之生。冲者，中也。阴阳若无神气，则中无主而神不宁。物之生也，犹且不能，况修道乎？《易》曰：「天地絪缊，万物化醇。」可见精气神三者俱足，斯阴阳合太极而不分。使阴阳虽具，太极无存，则造化失权，万物之生机尽灭。大凡修道炼丹，虽离不得真阴、真阳，若无太和元气，则丹无由结，道亦难成，盖道原太和一气所结而成也。生人生仙，只是一理，所争在顺逆间耳。惟以元气为体，阴阳为用，斯金丹之道，于是得矣。试观王公大人，位至高也，分至贵也，而自称曰孤、曰寡、曰不谷，其意何居？盖高者易危，满者易倾，电光之下，迅雷乘之。惟高不恃其高，贵不矜其贵，而以谦下柔和之心处之，斯可长保其富贵，而身家不至危殆焉，所以孤、寡、不谷，凡人所恶，王公反以之自称也。然则道为天地至宝，修之者可不矢谦柔之意乎？《书》曰：「满招损，谦受益。」从无有易之者。夫益不始于益，必先损而后益；损不始于损，必先益而后损。可见富贵贫贱，穷通得丧，屈极则伸，伸极必屈，此天道循环，自然之运，虽天地莫能逃，何况人乎？噫！人道如斯，大道奚异？修士欲得一阳来复，必先万缘俱敛，纯是和平之气，绝无躁切之心。如此损之又损，以至于无，则群阴凝闭之中，始有真阳发生，为吾身之益不少。倘或自恃其才，自多其智，心不虚而志自满，未有不为识神误事、邪火焚身者。欲益而反损，天下事大抵如斯，岂独修道乎？至于一切事宜，无非幻景，不足介意，而人犹以为后起者教。须知金丹大道，所为在一时，所关在万世，岂可不以为法耶？太上所以云：「人之所教，我亦教之」也。所教维何？至柔已耳。若不用柔而用刚，必如世上强梁之徒，横行劫夺，终无一人不罹法网而得以善终。是知横豪者，死之机；柔弱者，生之路。此诚修道要术，吾之教人，所以柔弱为先也。修士其可忽乎？《悟真》云：「道自虚无生一气，便从一气产阴阳。阴阳自是成三体，三体重生万物昌。」此即「一生二，二生三，三生万物」之谓。修行人，打坐之初，必先寂灭情缘，扫除杂妄，至虚至静，不异痴愚，似睡非睡，似醒非醒，此鸿濛未判之气象，所谓道也。忽焉一觉而动，杳冥冲醒，我于此一动之后，只觉万象咸空，一灵独运，抱元守一。或云真意，或云正念，或云如来正等正觉，此时只一心，无两念焉。观其阳生药产，果能蓬勃絪缊，即用前行二候法，采取回宫一候，归炉封固一候。是即一动为阳，阳主升；一静为阴，阴主降。再看气机壮否？若已大壮，始行河车运转，四候采取烹煎，饵而服之，立干己汞。此即采阳配阴，皆由一而生者也。至于一呼一吸，一开一阖，无不自一气而分为二气。然心精肾气，心阴肾阳，无不赖真意为之采取烹炼、交媾调和。此即阴阳二气，合真意为三体，皆自然而然，无安排、无凑合也。而要必本于谦和退让，稍有自矜自强之心，小则倾丹，大则殒命，故曰：「强梁者不得其死，吾将以为教父。」学者须知，未得丹时，以虚静之心待之；既得丹后，以柔和之意养之，慎勿多思多虑、自大自强可也。此为要诀中之要诀，学者知之。否则满腔杂妄，道将何存？如此而炼，是瞎炼也。一片刚强，即得亦丧，如此而修，是盲修也。似此无药无丹，遽行采炼运转，不惟空烧空炼，且必伤性伤精，其为害于身心不小，乃犹不肯自咎，反归咎于大道非真，金丹之难信。斯其人殆不知，道之为道，至虚至柔。惟以虚静存心，和柔养气，斯道乃未有不成也已。

From start to finish, Daoist cultivation takes emptiness and non-being as its sole guiding principle. That is why the Primordial Heavenly Sovereign bears the title "Vacuous, Non-Being, and Spontaneous." Fullness born from emptiness is true fullness; being born from non-being is true being. Without emptiness and non-being, not only do the seven emotions and six desires choke our true luminous substance so we can neither meet events rightly nor refine the Yang Spirit—even if you practice "contemplating emptiness and lucid awareness," the moment you add a shred of grit-your-teeth effort to hold it together, that is still "using mind to suppress mind," completely veiling your original face. In sum, emptiness is the substance of the Way, and central harmony is its function; attain these two, and you are close to the Way.

Lord Lao says, "The Way gives birth to One." What does the Way possess? Nothing but emptiness! Yet within utmost emptiness, a single primordial Breath (One Breath of Prior Heaven) subtly stirs, and heaven and earth are born—hence the earlier chapter says, "There is something formed in chaos, born before heaven and earth." Prior to the Boundless, undivided chaos is simply emptiness; when movement becomes yang and stillness becomes yin, that is the Book of Changes saying, "In the Changes there is the Supreme Ultimate, which engenders the Two Modes"—meaning "the Way gives birth to One, and One gives birth to Two."

Within the human body, it is the very same: out of faint, dim stillness, a single spark of awakening stirs; Qian and Kun open and close, and the vital current comes and goes—what gathers in stillness is yin and Essence, what flows in motion is yang and Breath. Without True Intention presiding at the center, yin and yang scatter out of control, unable either to engender human life or to accomplish the Way. Clearly, between yin and yang everything depends on Original Spirit and True Intention mediating in the middle—this is "Two gives birth to Three." Thereafter, one yin and one yang, one stillness and one movement circulate smoothly while the central master holds steady, and the myriad things flourish without end—this is "Three gives birth to the myriad things."

Some try to explain Laozi's "One, Two, Three, myriad things" through the Five-Phase generating numbers ("Heaven 1 engenders water, Earth 2 fire, Heaven 3 wood, Earth 4 metal"), which always feels forced. Far clearer is to recognize the Way as the Boundless, One as the Supreme Ultimate, Two as yin and yang, and Heaven-One plus Earth-Two uniting as Three.

"All things carry yin and embrace yang, blending breath into harmony" plainly tells us that the single vacuous Breath of the origin engenders yin and yang, and all things are born from their union. Chong means "center." If yin and yang lack centering Spirit and Breath, the middle has no master and Spirit cannot rest—even ordinary creatures could not come to life, still less could one cultivate the Way! As the Book of Changes says, "Heaven and earth mingle densely, and all things ripen in purity." When Essence, Breath, and Spirit are all complete, yin and yang fuse indivisibly with the Supreme Ultimate. If yin and yang appear while the Supreme Ultimate is absent, creation loses its pivot and all vitality withers.

In every work of cultivating the Way and refining the Elixir, though true yin and true yang are indispensable, without that single Breath of Supreme Harmony no elixir can form and the Way cannot succeed—for the Way itself is condensed from that harmonious primordial Breath. Begetting human beings and becoming an immortal follow one single principle, differing only between forward flow and reversal. Take primordial Breath as substance and yin-yang as function, and the Great Way of the Golden Elixir is right in hand.

Now look at kings and great lords: holding supreme rank and highest nobility, why do they style themselves "orphaned," "bereft," and "unworthy"? Because high places invite peril, brimful vessels spill over, and right beneath a flash of lightning strikes the crashing thunder. Only by not presuming on height or flaunting nobility, and instead dwelling in humble softness, can they preserve wealth and honor for long without endangering themselves and their families. That is why kings and dukes choose the very titles ordinary people detest. How much more must cultivators of the Way—heaven and earth's supreme treasure—vow humility and softness?

The Book of Documents says, "Pride invites loss; humility receives gain"—an iron law that has never changed. Gain never starts from gain; it always begins with diminishing before gaining. Loss never starts from loss; it always begins with excess before losing. Wealth and poverty, success and hardship, gain and loss—pushed to the extreme, contraction extends and extension contracts. That is Heaven's natural cycle, which even heaven and earth cannot escape, let alone humanity!

Alas, if human affairs work this way, how could the Great Way be any different? To welcome the return of one yang (Living Zi Hour), the practitioner must first gather in all worldly entanglements until body and mind are pure peaceful harmony without a trace of impatient urgency. Diminishing distractions again and again down to nothing, right within the deep stillness of gathered yin, true yang naturally arises and brings immense benefit to the body. Conversely, anyone who prides themselves on cleverness and talent—heart unemptied, ambition puffed up—inevitably lets the cognitive spirit ruin the work and stray fire scorch the body. Seeking gain only to reap loss: almost everything in the world works like this, not only cultivation!

Even though worldly affairs are passing illusions scarcely worth brooding over, people still draw lessons from them to teach the next generation. How much more must the Great Way of the Golden Elixir—whose work is done in a single lifetime yet bears on ten thousand ages—be held up as an enduring standard? That is why Lord Lao says, "What others teach, I too teach." And what is that teaching? Nothing but "utmost softness." Reject softness in favor of rigid force, and you become like violent brigands rampaging across the world—not one escapes the net of law or dies a peaceful death. Know well: blustering force is the pivot of death; yielding softness is the road of life. This is truly a vital secret of cultivation, which is why I always put gentleness first—how dare any practitioner take it lightly?

The Awakening to Reality says: "From emptiness the Way gives birth to One Breath; from One Breath arise yin and yang; yin and yang unite into three bodies, and from the three bodies all things flourish." That expresses "One gives birth to Two, Two gives birth to Three, Three gives birth to the myriad things." Put into direct practice: at the start of sitting, quiet all emotional attachments and sweep away wandering thoughts until you enter utmost emptiness and stillness—appearing as simple and unassuming as a fool, half asleep yet not asleep, half awake yet not strained awake. That is the undivided state before chaos opened, called the "Way." Suddenly a single spark of awakening stirs out of the dim and obscure; in the wake of that stirring, all phenomena stand empty while one luminous awareness operates alone, guarding the center and embracing the One. Call it True Intention, call it Right Mindfulness, or call it the Tathāgata's supreme awakening—at that instant there is only one undivided heart, never a second thought.

Observing that rising yang and nascent Medicine, if it genuinely swells with warm, dense harmony, classical alchemical texts speak first of the "two phases"—gathering back to the central palace as one phase, returning to the furnace and sealing quietly as the second, matching movement as yang rising and stillness as yin descending. Next one discerns whether the vital current has grown truly robust; only when it is fully abundant do the texts speak of turning the waterwheel (Celestial Circuit), following the four phases of fire phasing to simmer, harmonize, and absorb within, thereby calming one's own restless cognitive Mercury (see Lead and Mercury). This is harvesting yang to match yin, all springing from that single One. Even ordinary breathing—each exhalation and inhalation, each opening and closing—divides from one Breath into two; while heart-spirit and kidney-breath, heart-yin and kidney-yang, rely entirely on True Intention to harmonize and unite (conjunction of Kan and Li). Thus yin and yang united with True Intention form the "three bodies"—altogether spontaneous, never contrived or forced together.

Yet the root of all of this must rest in humble yielding; the slightest hint of pride or aggressive striving overturns the Elixir at best and endangers life at worst. Hence: "The violent and overbearing never meet a good end; I will take this as the foremost teaching for the multitude." Remember this above all: before the Elixir forms, wait with an empty, still heart; after it forms, nourish it with gentle softness—never overthink, never boast, never force! This is the secret within the secret; mark it well.

Otherwise, with a chest full of stray thoughts, where can the Way abide? Refining like that is "blind refining." With a heart full of stiff aggression, even what you gain slips away at once; cultivating like that is "blind cultivation." With neither true Medicine nor Elixir inside, rushing to imitate harvesting, refining, and circulation not only burns an empty furnace in vain, it inevitably injures both inner nature and vital essence, harming body and mind severely. Worse yet, people who hurt themselves this way refuse to blame themselves, blaming instead the Great Way as unreal and the Golden Elixir as untrustworthy! They never grasped that the Way is supremely empty and supremely soft. Only by keeping an empty, still heart and nourishing breath with gentle softness can this Way never fail to succeed.

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言道家修炼，却病延年，成仙作圣，不外精气神三宝而已。然精非交感之精，所谓「元始真如，一灵炯炯」，前云「惚兮恍，其中有象」是，是由虚而生。虚即道。「道生一」，即虚生精，精即性也。气非呼吸之气，所谓「先天至精，一气氤氲」，前云「恍兮惚，其中有物」是，是由一而生。一即精。「一生二」，即精生气，气即命也。神非思虑之神，所谓「灵光独耀，惺惺不昧」，前云「杳兮冥，其中有精，其精甚真，其中有信」是，自二而化。二即气。「二生三」，即气化神，神即元神真意也。要皆太和一气之所化也，惟以柔和养之，斯得之耳。若著一躁心，生一暴气，皆不同类，去道远矣。去道既远，保身犹难，安望成仙？所以有强梁之戒也。太上以「忍辱慈悲」为教，故其言如此。孔子系《易》，尝于「谦卦」三致意，而金人、欹器之类，示训谆谆，其即此意也欤？

Summing up this chapter, Daoist cultivation—whether preserving vitality or attaining sagehood—comes down to nothing other than the Three Treasures of Essence, Breath, and Spirit.

Here, however, "Essence" does not mean the coarse posterior reproductive fluid emitted in sexual union; it is what the classics call "primordial true suchness, one luminous spark shining clear," corresponding to Chapter 21's "dim and indistinct, yet within it is an image"—born from emptiness. Emptiness is the "Way," so "the Way gives birth to One" means emptiness engenders prior-heaven Essence, which is in fact our innate inner nature.

Nor does "Breath" here mean the ordinary breathing passing through nose and mouth; it is "prior-heaven supreme essence, one dense swirling Breath," corresponding to "indistinct and dim, yet within it is a thing"—born from One. One is prior-heaven Essence, so "One gives birth to Two" means Essence engenders Breath, which is our innate life (ming).

Nor does "Spirit" here mean the wandering cognitive spirit lost in idle thinking; it is "the solitary radiance of luminous light, vividly awake and unclouded," corresponding to "deep and obscure, yet within it is essence; that essence is wholly true, and within it is trustworthiness"—transformed from Two. Two is prior-heaven Breath, so "Two gives birth to Three" means Breath transforms into Spirit, which is Original Spirit and True Intention.

At bottom, all Three Treasures are transformations of that single Breath of Supreme Harmony, and can only be obtained by nurturing them with soft, humble gentleness. The moment an impatient thought enters the heart or a flash of harsh temper stirs the body, you become incompatible with harmonious Breath and drift miles from the Way. Drifting so far from the Way, even keeping yourself safe becomes difficult, let alone attaining longevity and realization! That is why Laozi lays down such a stern warning against violent force. Lord Lao always teaches forbearance, softness, compassion, and humility—hence these words. Likewise, when Confucius wrote his commentaries on the Book of Changes, he returned repeatedly to the hexagram "Humility" (Qian), and his earnest warnings before the bronze statue with sealed lips and the tilting vessel in the ancestral temple convey the very same truth.

Key Terms

  • One Breath of Prior Heaven (Breath of Supreme Harmony): Corresponds to the "One" in "the Way gives birth to One," and to the central harmonizing vitality in "blending breath into harmony."
  • Original Spirit and True Intention: The pure, wakeful awareness presiding over the union of yin and yang; Huang Yuanji explains "Two gives birth to Three" as yin and yang uniting with True Intention.
  • cognitive spirit: The competitive, prideful, overthinking posterior mind; when smug or impatient, it sparks "stray fire" that disrupts stillness.
  • guarding the center and embracing the One: The undivided state following a single awakening out of deep stillness, when all distractions vanish and one pure mind abides without a second thought.
  • Celestial Circuit (turning the waterwheel) and fire phasing: Traditional alchemical metaphors for natural internal circulation once vitality is abundant and for calibrating mental intensity; Huang strictly warns against "empty burning without medicine."
  • Essence, Breath, and Spirit (Prior-Heaven Three Treasures): In §3 Huang maps "One, Two, Three" onto prior-heaven Essence (inner nature), prior-heaven Breath (life), and Original Spirit / True Intention, sharply distinct from posterior fluids, ordinary breathing, and discursive thought.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Subtracting matters more than adding. When exhausted or agitated, resist piling more techniques, routines, or pep talks onto your schedule. Practice "gathering in all entanglements" first—cut down draining distractions, comparisons, and emotional friction, and vitality naturally rises again.
  2. Never use strained willpower in quiet sitting. Even when practicing focus or contemplating emptiness, the instant you feel jaw-clenching grit ("using mind to suppress mind"), you have missed the mark. Drop the shoulders, soften the breath, drop expectations of quick gains—that is the true doorway.
  3. Watch out for the turning point of success. When work goes smoothly, mood runs high, or practice feels pleasant, "pride invites loss" strikes fastest. Intentionally leave margin and stay low-key—that is the most reliable safeguard against a fall.
  4. Drop haste and hot temper. "Blustering force is the pivot of death; yielding softness is the road of life." Refraining from pointless outbursts and winning petty arguments—turning daily sharpness into calm evenness—is already the finest nourishment of vitality.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Orphaned, bereft, unworthy" (gu, gua, bugu): Ancient self-deprecating royal titles. Bugu (anciently written 不穀) means "unworthy" (not "valley"); the base OCR misread it as 不殺, standardized here to 不谷.
  2. "Fu is interchangeable with fu in the rising tone": Huang Yuanji's own interlinear phonetic gloss, reading 教父 not as "father/root of teaching" (as many later editions do) but as 教甫 ("teaching the multitude").
  3. "Heaven and earth mingle densely, and all things ripen in purity": From the Appended Remarks of the Book of Changes, depicting the rich union of yin and yang that matures all creatures.
  4. "Pride invites loss; humility receives gain": From the Book of Documents ("Counsels of Great Yu").
  5. "From emptiness the Way gives birth to One Breath...": Quatrain from Zhang Boduan's Northern Song classic Awakening to Reality (Wuzhen pian), the Southern Lineage's most famous poetic gloss on "the Way gives birth to One."
  6. Bronze statue and tilting vessel (jinren, qiqi): Recorded in School Sayings of Confucius and Xunzi ("Youzuo"). Visiting the Zhou ancestral temple, Confucius saw a bronze statue with its mouth triple-sealed warning against loose speech and aggression, and in Duke Huan's temple saw a tilting vessel that leaned when empty, stood upright when moderately filled, and overturned when filled to the brim—illustrating humility and balance.
  7.
