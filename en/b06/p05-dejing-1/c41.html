Chapter 41 · A Great Vessel Matures Late

The Great Way is supremely plain and ordinary yet conceals the marvelous within the everyday; true practitioners veil their light, advance by stepping back, and never rush for quick results so that a great vessel may mature

About This Chapter

This chapter is one of the Daodejing's most recognizable collections of paradoxes: the bright Way seems dim, advancing on the Way seems like retreating, utmost whiteness seems stained, a great vessel matures late, the greatest sound is nearly silent, and the greatest image has no fixed form. Beginning from the "three grades of people who hear the Way," Huang Yuanji brings each of these seemingly contradictory sayings down to earth in the lived conduct and inner fire-phasing of actual practice:

  1. Why the lower scholar laughs aloud on hearing the Way (§2, first half): People imagine cultivation as miraculous feats of flying through the skies; when told that internal alchemy starts from ordinary yin and yang and a single clear awakening out of stillness, they find it plain and laughable. They fail to see that "the marvelous hides right inside the ordinary"—cultivating truth by reversal follows the very same principle as ordinary human generation, differing only in forward versus reverse direction.
  2. Nourishing brightness through obscurity, advancing through retreat (§2, middle): Those who truly attain the Way never flash their sharp edges. Bright yet appearing dim, advancing yet appearing to retreat, abundant in virtue yet feeling insufficient, upright yet seemingly pliable—this is never fake modesty, but sinking one's strength deep within, nourishing it quietly without seeking quick payoff, and maturing slowly into a great vessel.
  3. Guarding the Way without inviting attack (§3): Growing smug and boasting over a tiny bit of progress is like roofing a house with dry thatch that invites a spark to burn it down. Simply apply yourself quietly to your own cultivation without seeking applause from others; then external praise or blame will be like "holding a torch to burn empty air"—finding nothing to ignite, it dies out on its own.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：上士闻道，勤而行之；中士闻道，若存若忘；下士闻道，大笑之，不笑不足以为道。故建言有之：明道若昧，进道若退，夷道若类，上德若谷，大白若辱，广德若不足，建德若偷，质直若渝，大方无隅，大器晚成，大音希声，大象无形，道隐无名。夫惟道，善贷且成。

Lord Lao says: When a person of superior capacity hears the Way, they practice it earnestly and steadfastly; when a person of middling capacity hears the Way, it seems sometimes present and sometimes forgotten; when a person of inferior capacity hears the Way, they burst out laughing—if it were not laughed at by shallow minds, it would not be worthy of being called the Way.

Hence the established sayings of old declare: the bright Way looks dim; advancing on the Way looks like retreating; the level Way looks rough and uneven; highest virtue looks like an empty valley; utmost whiteness looks sullied; expansive virtue looks insufficient; stalwart virtue looks slack and lazy; plain uprightness looks changeable; the greatest square has no sharp corners; a great vessel matures late; the greatest sound is scarcely heard; the greatest image has no fixed shape. The Way lies hidden and nameless. Yet the Way alone excels at bestowing endlessly upon all things and bringing them to completion.

§2
Original (Chinese)

天地未有之先，原是虚虚无无，鸿鸿濛濛，一段氤氲太和之气，酝酿久之，气化充盈，忽焉一觉而动，太极开基矣。动而为阳，轻清之气上浮为天；静而为阴，重浊之气下凝为地。天地开辟而人物滋生，芸芸万姓有几能效天地之功用哉？惟圣人从混沌中一觉而修成大丹，以此治身，即以此淑世。虽未敢缄口不言，却亦非概人而授，随缘就缘，因物付物，方合天地大公无我之量。时而遇上士也，闻吾之道，欣然向往，即勤而行之，略无疑意，此其人，吾久不得见之矣。时而遇中士也，出于予口，入于伊心，亦属平常，了无奇异，未始不爱之慕之，一蹴而欲几之，无奈世味浓而道味淡，圣念浅而凡念深，或迁或就，若存若亡，知不免焉。至于下等之士，习染日深，气性多戾，一闻吾道，不疑为妖言惑世，便指为聚众敛财，讵知君子之修，造端夫妇，圣人之道，不外阴阳，顺则生人，逆则成仙，其事虽殊，其理则一，而贸贸者乃谓神仙为幻术，岂有如此修持遂能上出重霄乎？否则谓天地至广，万物至繁，如此成性存存，即上下与天地同流乎？何以自古仙圣，至今无几也？于是笑其言大而夸，行伪而僻。噫！斯道只可为知己者道，难与浅见寡闻者言矣！夫蜉蝣不知晦暮，蟪蛄不知春秋，井蛙不知江海，又何怪其笑耶？不笑不足以见道之至平而至常，至神而至奇，神奇即在平常中也。况道本无声色，何有所言？其有所言，亦因后之修士，无由循途而进，历阶而升，故不得不权建虚词、假立名号以引之。人果知虚无为道，自然为功，尤须自阴而阳，由下而上。昧为明本，退为进基，虽明也而若昧，庶隐之深而明之至焉；虽进也而若退，庶却之愈速而进之弥远焉。道原远近皆具，我虽与道大适，亦若于己无增，于人无减，夷若类焉。道本大小兼赅，我虽与德为一，亦若无而不有，虚而不盈，德若谷焉。时而大显于世也，啧啧称道，不绝于口，我若无益于己，反多抱愧，故曰「大白若辱」。时而德充于内也，处处施为，不穷于用，亦若有缺于中，益形支绌，故曰「广德若不足」。即其修德立身，建诸天地而不悖，我若自安偷薄，绝无振拔之心，故曰「建德若偷」。或已至诚尽性，质诸鬼神而无疑，我若常变可渝，毫无坚固之力，故曰「质直若渝」。如此存养心性，惕厉神明，虽有谗言，无间可入，纵多乱德，何隙可乘？世有修道明德而遭侮辱者，其亦返观内省？果如此藏踪敛迹，卑微自下，怍辱为怀，德广而不居，德建而弗信，亦若忠直难言，诪张为幻者耶？吾知其未有此也。纵或数有前定，劫莫能逃，天之所危，人当顺受，安于命而听诸天，是以「君子有终身之忧，无一朝之患」，我于此益信焉。且道无方所、形状、声臭可言，彼世之廉隅自饬者，规规自守，不能圆转自如，我则大方无方，浑然一团，不落边际，又何模棱之有？凡物之易就者不美观，急成者非大器，我能循循善造，弗期近效，不计浅功，久于其道，自可大成，又何歉于己乎？要之，道本希言自然，恍惚为状，我能虚极静笃，则无音而大音出矣，无象而大象形矣。施之四海皆准，传之万世不穷，岂仅推重于一时而不能扬徽于万代耶？《诗》曰：「在彼无恶，在此无斁。」道之建施，实有如此神妙者。其间孰是为之，孰是与之？亦曰：夫惟道，善贷且成而已。此言抱道人间，用无不足，给万物而不匮，周沙界而有余，且使化功大成，真上士也。

Before heaven and earth came into being, all was vacuous, nameless, and undivided—a dense, swirling expanse of harmonious primordial Breath (One Breath of Prior Heaven). Brewing for long ages until vitality grew brimful, a sudden spark of awakening stirred into motion, founding the Supreme Ultimate. The moving aspect became yang, its light and clear breath rising to form heaven; the still aspect became yin, its heavy and turbid breath condensing below to form earth. Once heaven and earth opened, humanity and all creatures multiplied, yet among the teeming multitudes, how many can truly emulate the creative workings of heaven and earth?

Only the sages recognize that single awakening out of primordial stillness (the first stirring of the Mysterious Pass) and refine the Great Elixir—using it both to govern their own bodies and to benefit the world. Though they dare not lock their lips in silence, neither do they hand it out indiscriminately; they meet each person according to affinity and give what fits the vessel, matching the vast impartiality of heaven and earth.

Occasionally they meet a person of superior capacity who hears the Way, rejoices in longing for it, and immediately puts it into diligent practice without a trace of doubt—alas, it has been a long time since I saw such a person! Sometimes they meet a person of middling capacity: the words leave my mouth and enter their ears, sounding quite ordinary with nothing sensational about them. It is not that they dislike or fail to admire it—they may even hope to master it in a single leap—yet worldly flavors are thick while the taste of the Way is subtle; noble resolve runs shallow while habitual thoughts run deep. Swaying back and forth, keeping it one day and forgetting it the next, they can hardly avoid wavering.

As for people of inferior capacity, steeped in worldly habits and warped in temperament, the moment they hear our Way they either suspect it as wild heresy to bewitch the crowd or accuse it of being a scheme to gather followers and money. Little do they know that the noble person's Way begins in the ordinary union of husband and wife, and the Way of the sages never departs from yin and yang: flowing forward it begets human children, flowing in reversal it brings forth immortals—the activities differ, yet the underlying principle is identical! Blindly shallow people dismiss the art of the immortals as conjuring tricks, scoffing, "How could such ordinary quiet practice ever carry one beyond the mortal realm?" Or else they jeer, "Heaven and earth are so vast and creatures so countless—could merely nurturing one's inner nature flow together with heaven and earth? Then why have so few sages and immortals appeared since antiquity?" So they mock practitioners for grand empty talk and eccentric pretense.

Alas! This Way can only be spoken to kindred hearts; it is impossible to debate with narrow, unseeing minds. A mayfly born at dawn and dead by dusk knows nothing of nightfall; a summer cicada knows nothing of spring and autumn; a frog at the bottom of a well knows nothing of great rivers and seas—so why wonder at their laughter? If they did not laugh, one could not see how the Way is at once utterly plain and ordinary yet supremely subtle and wondrous—for the true marvel hides right inside the ordinary.

Besides, the Way originally has neither sound nor color—what is there to say? If sages still speak, it is only because later learners would otherwise have no path to follow and no steps to climb; thus they provisionally coin expressions and borrow names to guide them. Whoever truly grasps that emptiness is the Way's substance and naturalness its work must also understand moving from yin to yang and from below to above: obscurity is the root of clarity, retreat the foundation of advance. Bright within yet appearing dim, the deeper one hides, the fuller one's clarity becomes; advancing steadily yet appearing to step back, the more readily one yields, the farther one travels.

The Way encompasses both near and far; even when deeply attuned to it, I appear to add nothing to myself and take nothing from others—so the level Way looks rough and uneven ("the level Way looks rough"). The Way embraces both great and small; even when one with Virtue, I remain empty and unclaiming—so highest virtue looks like an empty valley ("highest virtue looks like a valley").

When my name grows renowned in the world and praise pours unceasingly from every mouth, I feel it adds nothing to me and instead feel humble shame—hence "utmost whiteness looks sullied." When inner virtue overflows and serves every need without running dry, I still feel lacking within and stretched thin—hence "expansive virtue looks insufficient." Even when my moral bearing accords with heaven and earth without flaw, I appear content in modest quietude without striving to stand out—hence "stalwart virtue looks slack." Even when utmost sincerity fulfills nature so that heaven and spirits find no fault, outwardly I seem pliable and changeable without rigid obstinacy—hence "plain uprightness looks changeable."

When one nourishes mind and nature like this and keeps one's Spirit constantly alert, even swirling slanders find no crack to enter, and malice finds no opening to exploit. When people in the world who claim to cultivate the Way meet insult and hostility, should they not look inward? Have they truly hidden their tracks, taken the low place, carried humble modesty, held vast virtue without claiming credit, built steady goodness without boasting, and refrained from putting on theatrical airs? I suspect most have not! Even if some fated calamity cannot be escaped and Heaven tests one with peril, one accepts it calmly, resting in destiny and entrusting oneself to Heaven. Thus Mencius's words—"The noble person has lifelong concern [for virtue], yet no sudden calamity of a single morning"—ring truer to me than ever.

Moreover, the Way has no fixed location, shape, sound, or scent. Worldly moralists who pride themselves on sharp rectitude cling stiffly to rules and cannot turn freely with events; by contrast, the adept of "the greatest square has no sharp corners" is a seamless sphere of harmony, unbound by rigid edges—where could any friction remain? Whatever is thrown together easily lacks beauty, and whatever is rushed into shape is never a great vessel. If I build steadily step by step, seeking no quick results and counting no petty gains, enduring long in the Way will naturally bring great completion—what cause is there for self-reproach?

In short, the Way speaks little and moves spontaneously, manifesting in the formless state of the dim and obscure. When I reach utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness, the greatest sound rings out without vocal noise, and the greatest image takes shape without physical outline. Applied across the four seas, it proves true everywhere; handed down for ten thousand generations, it never runs dry—far more than momentary acclaim, its radiance shines across the ages. As the Classic of Poetry says, "Unresented there, unwearied of here"—so wondrous is the Way in action! Who makes this happen, and who bestows it? Simply this: "The Way alone excels at bestowing endlessly and bringing all to completion." To walk through the human world embracing this Way—never running short in use, sustaining all creatures without depletion, spanning countless worlds with abundance to spare, and bringing the work of transformation to full fruition—that is the mark of a true person of superior capacity.

§3
Original (Chinese)

太上为世之不自韬光养晦、立德修身者言，彼稍有所得，便矜高自翊，五蕴未空，六尘不净，犹屋盖草茅，火有所借而燃。若只修诸己，不求诸人，浑浑乎一归于无何有之乡、广漠之野，纵有外侮，犹举火焚空，终当自息。如此修己，真修己也。惟其如此，故人与己两相安于无事之天，否则于道无得，反招尤也。孔子曰：「无而为有，虚而为盈，约而为泰」，其见恶于人也宜矣。修道者知此，可以免务外之思，亦可无外侮之患焉。

Lord Lao addresses this chapter to those in the world who refuse to veil their light, nourish themselves quietly, and build character through inner cultivation. The moment such people gain a tiny shred of experience, they swell with pride and praise themselves, even though their inner attachments (the five aggregates) are still unemptied and their senses (the six sense-objects) are still unpurified—just like a roof thatched with dry grass, where the slightest stray spark finds ready fuel and bursts into flame.

If instead you simply work on yourself without seeking approval or applause from others, blending quietly into the vast, unobstructed "Land of Nothing-at-All and Broad Desert," then even when insults or attacks strike from outside, they are like lifting a torch to burn empty air: finding nothing to catch on, the fire dies out by itself. Cultivating oneself like this is genuine self-cultivation. Only thus do both others and oneself dwell together in quiet peace; otherwise, you gain nothing in the Way and only invite resentment and trouble.

Confucius said, "Having nothing yet pretending to have, being empty yet pretending to be full, being straitened yet pretending to be abundant"—it is only fitting that people find such pretense loathsome. Once practitioners understand this, they free themselves from outward show and spare themselves the trouble of external hostility.

Key Terms

  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: The undivided, harmonious primordial Breath existing before heaven and earth separated, ultimate source of yin, yang, and all things.
  • Mysterious Pass: Interpreted by Huang Yuanji as "a sudden awakening stirring out of chaos, founding the Supreme Ultimate"—the prior-heaven pivot of vitality appearing naturally in utmost stillness.
  • Reversal: In contrast to worldly forward expenditure ("flowing forward begets human children"), gathering Essence, Breath, and Spirit back to the root ("flowing in reverse brings forth immortals").
  • The dim and obscure: The luminous, undivided, formless state reached in utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness, free of fixed concepts and clinging.
  • "The level Way looks rough" (yidao ruo lei): Lei (类) is an ancient loan for 纇 (a knot or unevenness in silk thread), signifying that the utterly plain, straightforward Way appears awkward or unappealing to worldly minds fond of detours and novelties.
  • "Excels at bestowing and completing" (shandai qie cheng): Dai means to give, lend, or supply; the Way continually nourishes all creatures without depleting itself, bringing each to full completion.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Do not let plainness discourage you. Truly effective self-cultivation and steadying of the mind are usually as unremarkable as eating, sleeping, and breathing quietly. The more eagerly you chase thrills, visions, or overnight shortcuts, the closer you edge toward the "inferior scholar's" mistake.
  2. When you make a little progress, learn to keep quiet first. Boasting or lecturing others the moment you feel a small shift is like "roofing your house with dry thatch"—it drains your own steadiness and invites needless friction. Keep gains tucked inside so real strength can mature.
  3. Give yourself permission to go slow. "Whatever is rushed into shape is never a great vessel." Whether restoring health or mastering a craft, drop anxious impatience and accumulate steady daily practice; time will supply real depth.
  4. Meet criticism with self-reflection rather than hard pushback. When hostility arrives, check first whether you are still holding onto vanity's "dry thatch." Empty your own clinging, and outside fire becomes "burning empty air"—unable to singe you at all.

Translator's Notes

  1. "The level Way looks rough" (yidao ruo lei): Most received editions write 纇 (lèi, a knot in silk thread, hence uneven); Huang's base text consistently uses the ancient phonetic loan 类 in both classic and commentary, preserved here with its loan meaning explained.
  2. "A summer cicada knows nothing of spring and autumn," etc.: Echoes Zhuangzi, "Free and Easy Wandering" ("The morning mushroom knows nothing of dusk and dawn; the summer cicada knows nothing of spring and autumn") and "Autumn Floods" ("You cannot speak of the sea to a well-frog").
  3. "Begins in husband and wife" and "tested before spirits without doubt": Both from the Doctrine of the Mean (Ch. 12 and Ch. 29).
  4. "The noble person has lifelong concern, yet no sudden calamity of a single morning": From Mencius ("Lilou II"), meaning the noble person worries lifelong about living up to virtue rather than panicking over sudden external blows.
  5. "Unresented there, unwearied of here": From the Classic of Poetry ("Hymns of Zhou," Zhenlu); 斁 (yì) means to grow weary of.
  6. "Having nothing yet pretending to have...": From the Analects ("Shu'er").
  7. "Theatrical airs" (zhouzhang weihuan): From the Book of Documents ("Wuyi"), meaning deceitful, showy illusion-making.
  8. Textual note: Numerous OCR errors in the base edition have been corrected: in the classic text, 太上日 → 太上曰, 中土 → 中士, 善货且成 → 善贷且成 (also in commentary); in the commentary, 人于伊心 → 入于伊心, 圣念浅而念深 → 圣念浅而凡念深, 若存着亡 → 若存若亡, 祝道 → 况道, 若味 → 若昧, 秉赅 → 兼赅, 亦着无 → 亦若无, 不绝人口 → 不绝于口, 已 → 己 (in 无益于己, 修己, 人与己), 无间可人 → 无间可入, 请张为幻 → 诪张为幻, 模梭 → 模棱, 上土 → 上士, and 浑浑子 → 浑浑乎.
