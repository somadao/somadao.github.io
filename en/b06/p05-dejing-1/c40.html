Chapter 40 · Being Is Born from Non-Being

The shortest chapter in the entire classic sums up the three great principles of internal alchemy: the return of one yang as movement, humble softness as function, and the cycle of being and non-being returning to true emptiness as completion

About This Chapter

Chapter 40 of the Daodejing contains only twenty-one Chinese characters, yet it is one of the richest philosophical and alchemical summaries in the entire five-thousand-word classic: "Return is the movement of the Way; softness is the function of the Way. All things under heaven are born from being; being is born from non-being." In three brief lines, it reveals the direction in which the Way moves (cyclic return), the manner in which the Way acts (yielding gentleness), and the origin of all creation (being arising from non-being and returning to non-being).

Skipping the usual split between statecraft and self-cultivation, Huang Yuanji devotes both sections straight to the heart of internal alchemy, unpacking these twenty-one characters into a miniature Complete Manual of the Reverted Elixir:

  1. The three great principles: - "Return is the movement of the Way" is the starting foundation of refining the elixir: clouded by posterior desires, one relies on concentrating Spirit, regulating the breath, silencing calculations, and forgetting scheming so that the prior-heaven impulse of one yang returns. - "Softness is the function of the Way" is the guiding attitude from beginning to end: from the first sitting until completion of the elixir, one remains humble, patient, and gentle ("concentrating breath to attain utmost softness"). Even a trace of coarse bravado sparks ordinary fire and harms the elixir. - "Being is born from non-being" reveals the ultimate cycle: cultivation passes through three rhythmic turns—from non-being into being (subtle yang rising in deep stillness), from being back into non-being (abundant vitality mellowing into quiet simplicity), and once more from non-being into being (maturing into the spontaneous Dharma Body).
  2. Harvesting the medicine and protecting nascent yang: The second commentary focuses on the delicate moment when utmost stillness stirs into motion, warning that this newborn yang impulse is extraordinarily tender and subtle; the slightest carelessness lets it slip downward into tangible posterior essence—confirming the alchemical adage, "What you can see cannot be used; what can be used cannot be seen." Even after true Medicine is gathered, rough or hasty refining still scorches the harmonious primordial Breath. Only by gently nourishing it with softness, returning every tangible trace to formless truth, can the Great Elixir stand on unshakable ground.

Safety note: This chapter specifically warns that "the slightest coarseness or bravado stirs ordinary fire and harms the Way," and that "improper harvesting or harsh refining injures the Breath of Supreme Harmony." In quiet practice, avoid chasing intense sensations or rushing for results; keeping an attitude of humility, softness, relaxation, and plain simplicity is the surest way to protect primordial vitality.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：反者道之动，弱者道之用。天下万物生于有，有生于无。

Lord Lao says: Returning in cycles back toward the source is the movement of the Way; humility, softness, and non-contention are the function of the Way. All things under heaven are born from "being" (tangible existence), and "being" itself is born from "non-being" (the formless Way).

§2
Original (Chinese)

大道，人人具足，个个圆全，又何待于复哉？不知人自有生而后，气拘欲蔽，知诱物化，斯道之为所汩没者多矣。苟非内祛诸缘，外祛诸扰，凝神调息，绝虑忘机，安得一阳发生，道气复返乎？故曰：「反者道之动。」此炼丹之始基也。迨至药已归炉，丹亦粗结，汞铅浑一，日夜内观，而金丹产焉。自此采取之后，绵绵不绝，了了常存，以谦以下，以辱以柔，就是还丹之妙用。然非但还丹当如此，自下手，以至丹成，无不当冥心内运，专气致柔。盖丹乃太和一气炼成。修道者，当以谦和处之，苟稍有粗豪，即动凡火，为道害矣。故曰：「弱者道之用。」天下万事万物，虽始于有形有象、有物有则，然其始不自有而肇也。圣人当大道之成，虽千变万化，无所不具，而其先必于至虚至无中采之炼之，然后大用流行，浩气充塞于两大。若非自无而炼，焉得弥纶天地，如此其充周靡尽乎？故曰：「有生于无。」学人修养之要，始也自无而有，从静笃中炼出微阳来；继也自有而无，从蓬勃内复归于恬淡；其卒也，又自无而有，混混沌沌，人我俱忘，久之自炼出阳神三寸，丈六金身。可见有有无无，原回环不已，迭运靡穷。学者必照此行持，方无差忒。

The Great Way is inherently complete in every person and whole in every being—if nothing was ever missing, why would it need to "return"? Alas, once human beings enter the world, we are hemmed in by temperamental biases and clouded by private desires; our minds are lured outward by sensory objects until we are swept away by things, burying our innate Way deep beneath layers of dust. Unless we cast off tangled attachments within and worldly disturbances without—steadily concentrating Spirit, regulating the breath, silencing anxious thoughts, and forgetting scheming—how could a single spark of yang ever stir and the primordial Breath of the Way return? Hence the classic says, "Return is the movement of the Way"—this is the very first foundation of refining the Elixir.

Once the true Medicine has returned to the furnace and the inner elixir begins to take shape, Lead and Mercury (Breath and Spirit) blend seamlessly into one; gazing gently inward day and night, the Golden Elixir comes to birth. From this gathering onward, the practice continues in an unbroken, luminous thread—always clear and present, held with humility, lowliness, forbearance, and gentleness. That is the wondrous function of the Reverted Elixir.

Indeed, this holds not only at the stage of the Reverted Elixir: from the very first day of sitting until the Great Elixir reaches completion, one must always quiet the mind, work silently within, and concentrate breath to reach utmost softness. For the Elixir is refined out of one Breath of Supreme Harmony. The cultivator must tend it with humble gentleness; if even a hint of coarse bravado or impatient force creeps in, agitated ordinary fire flares up at once and ruins the Way! Hence the classic says, "Softness is the function of the Way."

Consider also the myriad affairs and creatures of the world: though they appear with tangible shape, form, substance, and pattern, their true origin never began out of "being." Likewise, when a sage accomplishes the Great Way—possessing myriad wondrous transformations with nothing lacking—at the outset it was inevitably gathered and refined within utmost emptiness and non-being; only then does its vast function flow freely, filling heaven and earth with boundless vital Breath. Had it not been refined out of "non-being," how could it weave through heaven and earth so fully and inexhaustibly? Hence the classic says, "Being is born from non-being."

The essence of cultivation follows three cyclic movements: first, moving from non-being into being—refining a delicate first spark of true yang out of utmost stillness; next, moving from being back into non-being—never clinging to or parading that burgeoning vitality, but gently mellowing it back into tranquil simplicity; and at last, moving once more from non-being into being—resting in undivided wholeness where self and other are both forgotten, until over long ripening one naturally brings forth the transcendent Yang Spirit (spontaneous Dharma Body, known in Daoism as the "three-inch Yang Spirit" and in Buddhism as the "sixteen-foot Golden Body"). Clearly, being and non-being revolve in an endless, alternating cycle. Students must practice in accord with this rhythm to stay free of error.

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言金丹大道，非有他也，只是真气流行，充周一身。其静也，如渊之沉；其动也，如潮之涌。惟清修之子，冥心内照，自考自证，方能会之，非言语所能罄也。人能明得动机，是我生生之本，彼长生不老之丹，岂外是乎？况人人共有之物，无异同、无欠缺，只为身动而精不生，心动而气不宁，于是乎生老病死苦，辗转不休，轮回不已。若欲脱诸一切，非先致养于静，万不能取机于动，返我生初元气。但此个动机，其势至微，其气至嫩，稍不小心，霎时而生癸水、变经流，为后天形质之私，不可用矣。故曰：「见之不可用，用之不可见。」由此一动之后，采不失时，则长生有本，大丹有根。如执所有而力行之，笃所好而固守之，虽得药有时，成丹可俟，无如冲气至和，而因此后之采取不善、烹炼不良，一团太和之气，遂被躁暴凡火伤之。道本至阳至刚，必须忍辱柔和，始克养成丹道，太上所以有「挫锐、解纷、和光、同尘」之教也。然道虽有气动，犹是无中生有，有而不以弱养之，则不能返于虚无之天，道又何自而成乎？人第知一阳来复，乃道之动机，而不知返本还原、有象者仍归无象。盖有象者，道之迹；无象者，道之真也。知此，则修炼不患无基矣。

This passage explains that the Great Way of the Golden Elixir is nothing bizarre or exotic—it is simply true prior-heaven Breath flowing smoothly and filling the entire body. In stillness, it is deep and calm as an abyss; in motion, it swells naturally like a rising tide. Only a pure-hearted practitioner who quiets the mind, gazes inward, and tests and verifies it in direct experience can truly comprehend it; words alone can never exhaust its taste.

Once you recognize this subtle impulse of stillness stirring into motion, you see that it is the very root of our unending vitality—where else could the elixir of longevity be found? Moreover, it is a treasure shared equally by every human being, without difference between sage and commoner and without the slightest lack. Only because restless bodily activity prevents true Essence from engendering and restless mental churning keeps primordial Breath from settling do people fall into the endless turning of birth, aging, sickness, death, and sorrow. To step free of all that affliction, unless you first nourish yourself thoroughly in stillness, you can never meet the true impulse in motion or recover the primordial Breath of your origin.

Yet be intensely alert: this first stirring of one yang (Living Zi Hour) is extraordinarily subtle in momentum and tender in quality. With the slightest carelessness, in an instant it slips downward into tangible posterior secretions and reproductive fluids—once fallen into posterior physicality and desire, it can no longer be used for alchemy! That is why ancient elixir classics warn: "Once you can see it in tangible form, it can no longer be used; when it can truly be used, it is still formless and unseen."

If you meet that first subtle stirring without missing the moment and gently gather and nurture it, longevity gains its root and the Great Elixir takes hold. Yet even when you press on wholeheartedly with what you have gained—when obtaining the Medicine seems assured and completing the elixir within reach—remember that harmonious primordial Breath is supremely mild and balanced. If your subsequent gathering is stiff or your refining fire phasing is impatient, that precious sphere of Supreme Harmony is instantly scorched and ruined by agitated ordinary fire!

Though the Way in its essence is supremely yang and resilient, it can only be brought to maturity through forbearance, humility, and softness—that is why Lord Lao taught in Chapter 4 to "blunt the sharpness, untangle the knots, soften the glare, and mingle with the dust." Furthermore, even when true Breath stirs within, that is still only the stage of "being emerging from non-being"; if you gain that "being" yet fail to nourish it with yielding softness, it cannot return to the clear sky of emptiness, and how could the Way ever reach completion?

Most people know only that the return of one yang is the moving pivot of the Way, without realizing the deeper return to the origin: every tangible experience and phenomenon ("having form") must ultimately dissolve back into "having no form." For whatever has shape and sensation is merely a footprint left by the Way; only formless clarity is the true substance of the Way. Once you see cleanly through this, your cultivation rests on an unshakable foundation.

Key Terms

  • Return is the movement of the Way: The natural law by which extremes reverse toward their source; in internal alchemy, reversing posterior depletion so that prior-heaven yang vitality returns out of utmost stillness (Living Zi Hour).
  • Softness is the function of the Way: The guiding attitude throughout cultivation—"concentrating breath to attain utmost softness," maintaining humility, forbearance, and gentleness to nurture harmonious Breath while preventing coarse impatience from igniting ordinary fire.
  • Lead and Mercury: Alchemical metaphors borrowed from laboratory metals for prior-heaven true Breath (true Lead) and prior-heaven Original Spirit (true Mercury); "Mercury and Lead blending into one" means the complete union of Spirit and Breath.
  • "What you can see cannot be used; what can be used cannot be seen": A classic alchemical dictum indicating that prior-heaven vitality (Medicine) can only be gathered while still formless at its subtle onset; once it descends into tangible posterior fluids, its prior-heaven efficacy is lost.
  • Three-inch Yang Spirit / Sixteen-foot Golden Body: Traditional Daoist and Buddhist emblems of realized perfection—respectively the pure-yang spontaneous Dharma Body refined by returning Spirit to emptiness, and the perfected Buddha-body—cited by Huang to illustrate the full cycle of non-being → being → non-being → being.

Putting It into Practice

  1. For energy to return, first give body and mind a real stop. "When the body stays restless, essence is not born; when the mind stays restless, breath cannot settle." After prolonged burnout, the real reset is never another shot of stimulation—it is shutting out noise, sleeping deeply, and sitting quietly so vitality flows back on its own.
  2. Protect fragile new good states gently. Whether it is a subtle warmth and calm during sitting or a freshly sprouting good habit or insight in daily life, its beginnings are always "faint in momentum and tender in breath." Do not rush to show it off or push harder; quiet protection lets it take root.
  3. Strip away coarse, hot-headed force. The stronger a person or endeavor becomes, the more it needs the soft covering of "blunting sharpness, untangling knots, softening glare, mingling with the dust." One flare of impatient temper can scorch months of accumulated harmony.
  4. Never mistake milestones and sensations for the finish line. Physical sensations, praise, and achievements are only "footprints of the Way." Acknowledge them lightly, let them return to unassuming calm, and life naturally opens into its next cycle of growth.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Hemmed in by temperament and clouded by desire; lured by perception and transformed by things": The first phrase adapts Zhu Xi's commentary on the Great Learning; the second comes from the Record of Music in the Book of Rites, describing how unrestrained likes and dislikes let external objects sweep away innate heavenly principle.
  2. "The Two Greats" (liangda): Traditional expression for heaven and earth (also called the Two Modes).
  3. "Concentrating breath to attain utmost softness" (zhuanqi zhirou): From Chapter 10 of the Daodejing ("Concentrating your breath to reach utmost softness, can you be like a newborn infant?").
  4. "Error" (chate): Te (tè) means deviation, mistake, or going astray.
  5. Gui-water and flowing secretions (guishui, jingliu): In traditional Chinese medicine and alchemy, tiangui refers to the source of reproductive fluids. Huang uses these terms to illustrate how nascent prior-heaven yang, if not gently held while still formless, instantly slides into posterior physical depletion.
  6. "Blunt the sharpness, untangle the knots, soften the glare, mingle with the dust": From Chapter 4 (and Chapter 56) of the Daodejing, advising one to sheathe sharp edges, dissolve entanglements, veil brilliance, and stay unpretentious among ordinary people.
  7. Textual note: OCR/printing errors in the base edition—including 泪没 → 汩没, 荷非 → 苟非, 葯已归炉 → 药已归炉, 篓时 → 霎时, and 成丹可侯 → 成丹可俟—have been corrected from context, and a stray comma in the clause 太上所以有 has been removed.
