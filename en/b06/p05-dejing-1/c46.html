Chapter 46 · When the World Has the Way

Calamity and disorder begin with a single thought of craving, and inner refining is no different: when Essence is full, one simply follows and guards it without fuss; only when Essence scatters must fire be used to mend the leaks—and it all comes back to vigilance in solitude and knowing contentment

About This Chapter

"When the world has the Way, galloping horses are sent back to haul manure; when the world lacks the Way, warhorses foal in the borderlands" was originally a famous saying against war and for frugality. Huang Yuanji not only explains thoroughly, through the rise and fall of states, "how a single thought of craving brews a calamity that fills the sky," but in his second section also likens "the world" to the human body and "using horses" to the fire phasing of internal alchemy, offering a highly illuminating layer of inner-refining doctrine:

  1. A single thought of desire decides survival or ruin (§2): Why does an age of peace and prosperity slide toward ruin? Usually not in a single day's collapse, but starting from that single thought of craving—"having gained this, one covets that." When the desiring mind arises, greed is born; the more one seeks, the more one loses, the mind toiling and growing clumsier by the day. Only by knowing contentment and resting in one's lot can one keep lasting peace.
  2. "Having the Way" and "lacking the Way" as alchemical metaphors (§3, first half): If a person's body is originally full of Essence and abundant in Breath (the world has the Way), there is no need at all to go to great lengths with refining methods; one simply follows nature and guards it. It is precisely because posterior living has worn things away, Essence dwindling and Breath scattering (the world lacks the Way), that one must enlist Original Spirit and True Breath to kindle the fire and repair oneself, "fighting in the field and guarding the city." Once the grime is cleared and clear light restored, one must still withdraw the troops and return to stillness, like "sending the horses back to Mount Hua."
  3. Nipping things in the bud, common to Daoism, Confucianism, and Buddhism (end of §3): Laozi's three consecutive warnings against "the desirable," "not knowing contentment," and "the wish to acquire" are, in Huang Yuanji's view, exactly the same as the Confucian "vigilance in solitude" and "guarding against self-deception to seek inner satisfaction," and the Buddhist "right awakening"—all guard the pass at the very first stirring of a thought.

Safety note: The second section of this chapter uses "warhorses foal in the borderlands" and "galloping horses are sent back to haul manure" as figures for "advancing the fire and withdrawing the tallies," "fighting in the field and guarding the city," and "using fire to refine the elixir" in internal alchemy. These are all symbolic expressions from ancient alchemy for how to calm the mind, regulate oneself, and clear away the turbid to restore the clear after imbalance; never read them literally, and never forcibly hold the breath to drive the fire or fix the mind with intense concentration. Everyday cultivation should rest on knowing contentment, having few desires, giving up inner friction, and following what is natural.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：天下有道，却走马以粪；天下无道，戎马生于郊。罪莫大于可欲，祸莫大于不知足，咎莫大于欲得。故知足之足常足。

Lord Lao says: When the world has the Way and government is clear and bright, even warhorses that can gallop a thousand li a day are sent back to the farmland to pull carts carrying manure for the fields; when the world lacks the Way and wars come one after another, pregnant warhorses cannot even return to their stables and must give birth to their foals out in the wild borderlands.

There is no crime greater than indulging selfish desire, no calamity greater than not knowing contentment, no fault greater than greed for possession. Therefore the sufficiency that comes from knowing contentment is the true sufficiency that never runs dry.

§2
Original (Chinese)

天下有道，君民皆安，征伐无用，故放马归林，开田辟地，以期粪其田而已。天下无道，世已乱矣，时有为焉，盗贼叠兴，干戈日起，不用兵马，乌能已乎？故戎马养于郊野，以待国家之需用。是马之却也，为有道；马之生也，因无道，马之关于天下大矣。呜呼！安得君君臣臣，父父子子，型仁讲义，敦诗说礼，长安有道之天乎哉？无如升平久而享用隆，嗜好兴而贪婪出，既得乎此，又羡乎彼，而奇技淫巧之物，悉罗列于前，鲜衣美食之不足，又思及乎琼室瑶台，千里邦畿犹不广，复念及于万里圻封。呼嗟！内作色荒，外作禽荒，又益之以尚利急功，穷兵黩武，苛求不已，贪得无厌，内外侮乱，不亡何待？缘其故，皆由一念之欲肇其端也。欲心起而贪心生，贪心生而未得期得，既得恐失。若此者，纲常不坏，祸患不兴，国家不至覆败，天下不底灭亡，未之有也，故曰：「罪莫大于可欲。」假使无欲，贪何由生？贪既不生，则苟合、苟完、苟美之风，不难再见也。其曰「祸莫大于不知足」，夫人既欲心不起，此志常满，此心常泰，无求于世，无恶于人，事之得也听之，事之不得也亦任之，祸从何而起乎？又曰「咎莫大于欲得」，人既知足，自能守分安命，顺时听天，无谄无骄，不争不夺，率由坦平之道，长沐太和之风，又何咎之有哉？况真心内朗，真性内凝，修己以敬，常乐于中，素位而行，不愿乎外，自然有天下者常保其天下，有国家者常保其国家，有身命者常保其身命。所患者，欲心一起，不克剪除，卒至穷奢极欲，而莫之救也，欲求天下有道，得乎？自古得失所关，只在一念，一念难回，遂成浩劫，此罔念所以致弥天之祸也。存亡所系，介于几希，几希克保，定启鸿图，此克念所由造无穷之福也。如此则知，一念之欲，其始虽微，其终则大，可不慎欤？故曰：「知足之足常足。」彼不知足者，愈求愈失，因愈失而愈求，遂至力倦神疲，焦劳不已，有何益耶？岂知穷通得丧，主之在天，非人力所能为，与其劳劳而日拙，何若休休之为得也。若知足者，顺其自然，行所无事，何忧何虑？不忮不求，又焉往而不臧耶？人其鉴诸！

When the world has the Way, ruler and people alike live in peace and contentment, and there is no need to wage wars on every side; so the warhorses are released back to the forests and returned to the fields, used simply to open up farmland and pull carts to manure the fields. When the world lacks the Way, the age has already fallen into great disorder, with unrest arising everywhere, bandits springing up one after another, and battles flaring daily—how could it be quelled without mobilizing troops and horses? So the warhorses can only be kept year-round out in the wild borderlands, ready to answer the call of war at any moment. Thus the warhorse being sent back to the farmland is because the world has the Way; the warhorse foaling in the borderlands is because the world lacks the Way—the whereabouts of a single small horse reflects the order or disorder of the whole world!

Alas! How can rulers fulfill the way of rulers and ministers the duties of ministers, fathers be kind and sons filial, everyone honoring humaneness and righteousness and holding fast to the Odes and the rites, so that this peaceful age of the Way may be enjoyed for long? But when days of peace last long, enjoyment grows ever more extravagant; and as enjoyment multiplies, all kinds of cravings and greed spring up along with it. Having just gained one thing, one eyes another; strange, ingenious, and decadent trinkets are all laid out before one's eyes. Wearing brocades and eating delicacies is still not enough, and one thinks of building jade towers and agate terraces; possessing a royal domain of a thousand li still seems too small, and one dreams of extending the borders ten thousand li beyond.

Ah! Within the palace, wallowing in sensual pleasure ("lust for women"); outside, obsessed with hunting and roaming ("lust for the chase"); add to that a rush for profit and quick success, militarism exhausting the army, endless harsh levies and taxes, and boundless greed—until troubles at home and threats from abroad abound and everyone turns away. What else can await but ruin? Trace it back to its source, and all of it began with that first faint thought of selfish desire!

Once a thought of desire arises, greed is born; once greed is born, one strives desperately to obtain what one does not yet have, and once one has it, one worries all day about losing it. When things come to this, it is utterly impossible for the bonds of human relations not to decay, for calamities not to arise, for the state not to be overturned, for the world not to be destroyed! Therefore Laozi says, "There is no crime greater than the desirable." If no selfish desire arises in the heart, where could greed come from? And once greed does not arise, the simple, contented spirit of the ancients—"barely enough, and it seems good; a little saved, and it seems complete; living in comfort, and it seems beautiful"—will not be hard to see again.

Next it says, "There is no calamity greater than not knowing contentment." As long as a person does not give rise to a greedy heart, their aspirations are always fulfilled and their state of mind always at ease; they make no forced demands on the world and bear no grudge against others; when things succeed they accept it calmly, and when they do not they let it go as fate allows—so from where could calamity sneak in? It also says, "There is no fault greater than the wish to acquire." Once a person knows contentment, they can naturally keep to their own station, accord with the times and with Heaven's decree, neither flattering nor arrogant, neither contending nor grabbing, walking steadily on the level, upright road and bathing long in the clear breeze of supreme harmony—what fault could there be?

What is more, if one lets the true heart shine clear within and the original nature gather steadily within, cultivating oneself with reverence, always carrying inner tranquility and joy, resting in one's present station without wildly seeking what lies outside oneself, then those who possess the world will naturally keep the world, those who govern a state will naturally keep their state, and ordinary people who possess this body and life will naturally keep their body and life. What is most to be feared is failing to cut off a desire the moment it first sprouts, letting it snowball until one ends up in the most extreme extravagance and indulgence, beyond all remedy—and then still hoping for the world to have the Way: how could that be possible?

Since antiquity, the key to success and failure, gain and loss, has lain in a single thought: one indulgent thought that cannot be called back can brew a calamity that fills the sky—this is how indulging deluded thought brings disaster. The dividing line between survival and ruin is only the slightest thread; hold on to that thread of clarity, and one will surely open up a grand future—this is how mastering oneself with right thought creates boundless blessing. From this one sees that a faint thought of craving, though extremely slight at the start, becomes astonishingly great in the end—how can one not be supremely careful?

Therefore the classic says, "The sufficiency of knowing contentment is lasting sufficiency." Those who do not know contentment lose all the more the harder they strive, and strive all the more anxiously the more they lose, until they are utterly exhausted and battered—what benefit have they gained in the end? Little do they understand that the ups and downs, gains and losses of life have their own timing and conditions, and can never be forced simply by selfish, reckless effort. Rather than toiling and scheming all day and growing clumsier the busier one gets, how much better to be broad-minded, at ease, and free! Those who truly know contentment follow nature and act without forcing anything—what is there to worry or fret about? As the Classic of Poetry says, "Neither envious of others nor grasping for outer things—wherever one goes, how could it not turn out well?" Let people take these words as a mirror for themselves!

§3
Original (Chinese)

此以天下比人身，以马比用火炼丹。人如有道，则精盈气足，何事炼为？惟顺而守之足矣。如其无道，则精消气散，不得不用元神真息，以修治其身心。但下工之始，养于外田，故曰「戎马生于郊」。俟其阳生药产，而后行进火退符之功、野战守城之法，收归炉内，慢慢温养，迨垢秽除尽，清光大来，一如天下乂安，国家无事，归马华山，故曰「却走马以粪」。但天下之乱，一身之危，莫不由一念之欲所致。若不斩除，潜滋暗长，遂至精髓成空，身命莫保，可悲也夫！凡人欲心一起，必求副其愿而后快，即令事事如愿，奈欲壑难填，贪婪无厌，得陇望蜀，辗转不休，有天下者遂失天下，而有身命者又岂不丧其身命乎？《诗》曰：「不忮不求，何用不臧？」惟知足者，可以安然无事，而常居有道之天，不须功行补漏，但顺其自然，与天为一而已矣。太上戒人曰「罪莫大于可欲」三句，是教人杜渐防微、戒欺求慊工夫，与孔门言「慎独」、佛氏云「正觉」，同一道也。学者曾见及此否？

From the standpoint of alchemical cultivation, this chapter uses "the world" as a figure for the human body and "warhorses" as a figure for applying fire phasing to refine the Elixir. If a person already accords with the Great Way, with Essence full and Breath abundant, and the prior-heaven endowment unleaked, what need is there to struggle to refine anything? It is enough simply to follow nature and guard it steadily. It is precisely because most people have lost the Great Way, overspending in posterior life until Essence dwindles and Breath scatters, that they must call upon Original Spirit and True Breath to repair and remedy their own body and mind.

When one first sets hand to practice, the vital current still floats outside and has not returned to the central palace—this is like "warhorses foaling in the borderlands." Only once, in the depth of stillness, yang arises and the Medicine is produced do alchemical methods speak of the measured use of advancing the yang fire and withdrawing the yin tallies, and of the work of first driving out stray thoughts ("fighting in the field") and then holding to clarity and stillness ("guarding the city"), gathering the vitality back into the furnace and cauldron of the central palace to be slowly, warmly nourished. When at last the grime and habits of body and mind are washed clean and clear Light shines brightly again, it is like the world at peace and wars subsided, the warhorses released onto the southern slopes of Mount Hua—this is "galloping horses are sent back to haul manure."

Yet in the end, whether it is the turmoil of the world or the peril of an individual's life, none of it fails to stem from that first faint "thought of desire." If one does not resolutely cut it off at that moment but lets it quietly grow in the dark, it will ultimately drain a person's marrow and essence until even their life cannot be kept—how lamentable! When an ordinary person's desiring mind stirs, they always need to gratify the thought before they feel satisfied; yet even if you truly got everything you wished for, alas, the gulf of human desire can never be filled, and greed has no end—having taken Longxi, one sets one's sights on Shu, tossing and turning without a day's peace. Those who hold the world lose their realm because of it; and ordinary people who possess a precious body and life—how could they not ruin their lives because of it too?

The Classic of Poetry says, "Neither envious nor grasping—what could one do that would not turn out well?" Only those who truly know contentment can remain at peace and dwell long in the clear, pure realm of "having the Way," with no need whatsoever to rack their brains over the hard work of mending leaks; they simply follow nature and become one with Heaven's Way. Lord Lao's three warnings to the world, beginning "There is no crime greater than the desirable," teach precisely the real work of nipping things in the bud and not deceiving oneself so as to seek inner ease (guarding against self-deception to seek inner satisfaction). This is in fact entirely one and the same great road as the Confucian "vigilance in solitude" and the Buddhist "right awakening." Students—have you seen through to this layer?

Key Terms

  • Original Spirit and True Breath: The pure prior-heaven illuminating awareness and gentle true Breath used to gather in body and mind and harmonize yin and yang when posterior Essence dwindles and Breath scatters.
  • Fire phasing (advancing the fire and withdrawing the tallies): In internal alchemy, a figure for the rhythm of one's effort and self-regulation and the measure of advancing and withdrawing; Huang Yuanji stresses that it is a "leak-mending" method used out of necessity after Essence has scattered, and that once clear light is restored one must in the end "send the horses back to Mount Hua" and return to non-action.
  • Fighting in the field and guarding the city: Alchemical texts traditionally borrow military strategy as a figure for practice—gathering in the scattered, outward-rushing Essence, Breath, and Spirit and subduing stray thoughts is "fighting in the field"; bringing them back to the central palace to be steadily, warmly nourished is "guarding the city."
  • Vigilance in solitude and guarding against self-deception to seek inner satisfaction: The core work of Confucian self-cultivation, meaning not deceiving oneself and keeping one's innate knowing clear at the first rising of a thought where no one else can see; Huang Yuanji points out directly that this is the same mind-method as Laozi's "nipping things in the bud" and the Buddhist "right awakening."

Putting It into Practice

  1. Brake promptly at the "first thought." Staying up late scrolling on your phone, impulse buying, comparison, and envy are usually not momentous events, but the failure to brake that first faint thought of "just one more look, just a little more." Learning to recognize a thought the moment it surfaces is a hundred times less effort than regretting it afterward.
  2. Distinguish "what you truly need" from "the unfillable gulf of desire." Much anxiety stems not from lack but from "having gained this, one covets that." Regularly taking stock of the health, time, and stability you already have is the most immediately effective "sufficiency of knowing contentment."
  3. The highest art of nourishing life is not "intense tonifying" but "fewer leaks." "With Essence full and Breath abundant, what need is there to refine?" First shutting off the funnels that drain vitality—late nights, binge eating and drinking, overthinking—is far wiser than overdrawing yourself while hunting for miraculous remedies.
  4. Learn to switch your energy from "battle mode" back to "farming mode." After an intense sprint, deliberately schedule time for complete relaxation and recovery, and do not let body and mind stay for long in the stress state of "warhorses foaling in the borderlands."

Translator's Notes

  1. "Within, lust for women; outside, lust for the chase": From the Book of Documents ("Songs of the Five Sons"); "lust for women" (sehuang) means being besotted with sensual pleasure, "lust for the chase" (qinhuang) means being obsessed with hunting and amusements.
  2. "Barely enough, a little saved, living in comfort" (gouhe, gouwan, goumei): From the Analects ("Zilu"), where Confucius praises Prince Jing of Wei for managing his household with contentment: when he first had some savings, he said "that is about enough"; when he had a little to spare, "that is about complete"; when he was well off, "that is about beautiful"—without any thought of greed.
  3. "Act according to one's present station and wish for nothing beyond it": From the Doctrine of the Mean (Ch. 14), meaning to conduct oneself in keeping with the position one currently occupies, without coveting what lies beyond one's lot.
  4. "Deluded thought" and "mastering thought" (wangnian, kenian): From the Book of Documents ("Duofang"): "The sage who neglects thought becomes a madman; the madman who masters thought becomes a sage"—meaning that even a sage who lets himself go thoughtlessly becomes wild and foolish, while the foolish who restrain themselves with right thought can become sages and worthies.
  5. "Toiling and growing clumsier by the day" and "broad-minded at ease" (xiuxiu): Adapted from the Book of Documents ("Officers of Zhou"): "Practice virtue, and the mind is at ease and grows better daily; practice falsehood, and the mind toils and grows clumsier daily," and ("Oath of Qin"): "His mind is broad and at ease, as if able to contain all."
  6. "Neither envious nor grasping—what could one do that would not turn out well?": From the Classic of Poetry ("Airs of Bei," Xiongzhi); zhi 忮 means envious and harmful, zang 臧 means good, well.
  7. "Sending the horses back to Mount Hua": From the Book of Documents ("Successful Completion of the War"): "He sent the horses back to the south of Mount Hua and released the oxen in the wilds of Taolin, showing the world he would no longer use them"—a figure for the world pacified and wars ended.
  8. "Guarding against self-deception to seek inner satisfaction": From the Great Learning: "Do not deceive yourself... this is called being satisfied within," meaning to give up self-deception and attain a heart truly open and content.
  9. Textual note: In the base text, the 四 in the classic's 知足之四足常足 and the @ in the commentary's 知足之@足常足 are both misread footnote markers, accompanied by a stray note "① 之，底本作『知』，改"; all have been cleaned up together. In the commentary, 千戈 has been corrected to 干戈, 罪奠大于 to 罪莫大于, 荷完 to 苟完, 其日, 又日, 故日, and 《诗》日 all to 曰, 祝真心 to 况真心, 不知是者 to 不知足者, 日抽 to 日拙, 怪慢温养 to 慢慢温养, 天下义安 to 天下乂安, 银转不休 to 辗转不休, and 防徽 to 防微.
