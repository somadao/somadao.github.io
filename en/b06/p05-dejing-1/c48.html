Chapter 48 · In Pursuing the Way, One Decreases Daily

Learning a craft thrives on steady accumulation, but cultivating the Way thrives on peeling away layer after layer and forgetting each step as one refines it; even the rising and falling of the fire must be left wholly to nature—this is non-action within action

About This Chapter

"In pursuing learning, one increases daily; in pursuing the Way, one decreases daily" is the most representative methodology of Daoist study and cultivation. Huang Yuanji first contrasts it with reading, composition, and the skills of the various crafts; he then uses the opposite examples of Zhuge Liang and Xu Shu from the art of war to show thoroughly how one "returns to having no affairs in the midst of affairs"; and finally he points straight at the core of actual internal-alchemy practice:

  1. Peeling the plantain, unreeling the cocoon, spitting out the sugarcane pulp (§2, first half): Worldly learning keeps loading more and more into the head, but if cultivation of the Way likewise greedily chases breadth and piles up opinions, it buries the original mind, the Supreme Ultimate within. True "daily decrease" means letting go of your attachment to each stage once you have practiced it—like chewing sugarcane, swallowing the true sweetness and spitting out the pulp—until you finally return to purity, stillness, and non-action.
  2. How to stay "without affairs" in the midst of affairs (§2, second half): Governing the realm or leading an army can never be free of events; the key is that "the mind is not thrown into disorder." Zhuge Liang, amid an army of a million in silk kerchief and with feather fan, planned at ease, treating affairs as though there were none; Xu Shu, frantic with worry for his mother, lost all composure and could devise nothing—this is letting affairs throw the mind into disorder.
  3. "Forgetting as you refine" and following the natural course (§3): Someone asks: internal alchemy speaks of gathering the Medicine and of fire phasing, so how can that count as non-action? Huang Yuanji answers in a single stroke: rise as the movement of Breath rises of itself, descend as it descends of itself, never forcing or patching things together beforehand with selfish intent—even when there is some guiding and transforming, it is a "non-action" left entirely to nature. Rigid swallowing of saliva and forced ingestion of breath, by contrast, fall into the deliberate methods of side doors.

Safety note: The later part of this chapter mentions traditional terms such as "gathering the medicine and refining the elixir, advancing the fire and withdrawing the tally," "deliberate conveying," and "swallowing saliva and ingesting breath." Huang Yuanji here explicitly opposes posterior-heaven contrivances such as deliberately conveying the breath or forcibly ingesting it, and stresses "leaving everything wholly to nature, without the slightest contrivance." In everyday quiet sitting and self-care, never forcibly hold the breath or deliberately steer the rising and falling of the breath, and do not mistake ancient hyperbolic praise in the text such as "sun, moon, and stars revolve at my command" for a supernatural ability to control the outer world.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：为学日益，为道日损，损之又损，以至于无为，无为而无不为矣。故取天下者，常以无事；及其有事，不足以取天下。

Lord Lao says: In pursuing worldly learning and skills, one must increase one's knowledge and accumulation day by day; in cultivating the Great Way, one must decrease one's stray thoughts and attachments day by day. Decrease and decrease again, until one arrives at the state of complete purity, stillness, and non-action; and once one has reached true non-action, there is nothing that cannot be accomplished. Therefore those who can settle and win the realm usually rely on an attitude of "having no affairs"—not stirring up trouble or making work. Once they grow eager for grandiose achievements and keep stirring up affairs and disturbances, they are no longer fit to bear the great charge of governing the realm.

§2
Original (Chinese)

学者记诵词章，与百工技艺之务，皆贵寻师访友，多见多闻，而后才思生焉，智巧出焉，知能愈广，作为愈多，始足以援笔成文，运斤成风，故曰「为学日益」。若为道，则反是。如以博览群书、泛通故典为事，不克返观内照，静守一心，则搜罗遍而识见繁，必心志纷而神明乱，虽学愈多，道愈少，久则浑然太极，汩没无存矣。故为道者，须如剥蕉抽茧，愈剥愈少，弥抽弥无，以至于无无之境，斯为得之。修道至此，自然神妙莫测，变化无方，其聚则有，其散则无，欲一则一，欲万则万，日月星辰，随我运转，风云雷雨，听我经纶，其大为何如哉？虽然，学者行一节，丢一节，如食蔗然，吃尽丢尽，仍返于无，故曰「为道日损，损之又损，以至于无为」，无为而无不为得矣。试观取天下者，不得不兴兵动马，称干比戈，乌得无事？然有事之中，须归无事，庶能一心一德，运筹帷幄，则心志不纷，谋猷始出。故出征者，号令严明，耳不听外言，目不见外事，心不驰外营，始能运用随机，取天下犹如反掌。不然，纷纷扰扰，事愈多则心愈乱，心愈乱则神愈昏，贼甫至而不能静镇自持，兵初交而遂至凌乱无节，如此欲一战成功，难乎不难？又况东夷未靖，西戎又兴，彼难未平，此波复起，若不知静以制动，逸以待劳，鲜有不委去者。古之败北而走，倾城而亡，莫不由有事阶之厉也。兵法所以有出奇制胜、设疑设伏之谋，敌人望之，旌旗满目，草木皆兵，虽大敌当前，亦心惊胆落，未有不望风先遁者。惟有事视如无事，万缘悉捐，一心内照，如武侯于百万军中纶巾羽扇，自在清闲，所以西蜀偏安，得延汉祚于危亡之际。若有事于心，则方寸已乱，灵台无主，似徐元直之为母归曹，不能再献奇谋，佐先帝以中兴，乌足取天下乎哉？

Scholars who memorize and recite literary compositions, and artisans of every trade who study their crafts, all value seeking out teachers and visiting friends, seeing much and hearing much; only when their store has grown thick do talent and ideas well up and ingenious skill take shape. The broader their knowledge and ability and the more practiced their performance, the more they are able to take up the brush and produce an essay, or swing the axe so that it whistles like the wind—this is "in pursuing learning, one increases daily."

Cultivating the Way is exactly the reverse. If you treat reading widely and stuffing yourself with anecdotes and classics as the real business of the Way, yet cannot turn your gaze back to illuminate within and quietly guard this one original mind, then the more miscellaneous your collection and the more numerous the opinions in your head, the more scattered your will and the more clouded your Spirit will inevitably become. The more books you read, the farther you stray from the Great Way; and over time the originally undivided, pure Supreme Ultimate of the original mind is entirely submerged beneath layer upon layer of knowledge and stray thoughts until nothing of it can be seen.

Therefore those who cultivate the Way must be like someone peeling a plantain stalk or unreeling a silkworm's cocoon: peeling layer after layer outward, there is less and less; drawing out strand after strand, it grows emptier and emptier, until one peels down to the state of "not even nothing," where even attachment to "nothing" is gone—only then has one truly attained the Way. When one's cultivation reaches such a degree of empty clarity and ease, the state of mind becomes naturally rounded and transparent, wondrous and unfathomable, bound by no fixed pattern, gathering and releasing at will, resonating in step with the transforming and nurturing of heaven and earth—how vast a realm that is!

In sum, the learner should, upon passing through each stage, casually let go of attachment to that stage—just like eating sugarcane: once the sweetness has been chewed out, spit out the pulp, and in the end return, clean and clear, to emptiness. This is "in pursuing the Way, one decreases daily; decrease and decrease again, until one arrives at non-action"; and only thus can one truly reach "through non-action, nothing is left undone."

Now look at those in the world who contend for supremacy and pacify the realm: they cannot avoid dispatching troops and generals and taking up arms on the field—how could they truly be "without affairs"? Yet the mark of mastery is that, while the body stands in the thick of countless entangled "affairs," the mind must return to a settled, clear state of "no affairs." Only then can one concentrate with single-minded purpose and lay strategy within the command tent, the will undisturbed by noise, so that good plans flow forth unceasingly.

So a great general who truly leads an expedition keeps his orders strict and clear: his ears do not listen to rumors passed around outside, his eyes do not dwell greedily on irrelevant matters, his thoughts do not drift outward at random; only thus can he respond flexibly to circumstances, and pacify the realm as surely as turning over his hand. If, on the contrary, everything is in uproar all day and one rushes about in confusion, then the more affairs there are the more panicked the mind, and the more panicked the mind the more clouded the wits; the moment the enemy shows its head one cannot hold one's ground, and the instant the two armies clash all order is lost—to hope for success in a single battle like this, is it not doubly difficult? Moreover, the situation is often that one wave has not subsided before another rises: no sooner does fire break out in the east than smoke rises in the west. Without knowing how to "use stillness to master motion, and rest to await the weary," few avoid collapse and flight. Since ancient times, every defeat and every city lost and state destroyed has had its root in the fact that one's own mind was first thrown into the disorder of "affairs."

Consider why the art of war prizes winning by surprise and setting up feints and ambushes: it is precisely to throw the enemy's mind into confusion—so that, gazing from afar, the enemy sees banners filling the eye and suspects every bush and tree of being a soldier, and even with a great host bearing down, they are so terrified that they flee before any engagement. Only one who can "treat affairs as though there were none," setting aside all entanglements and turning the single mind inward to illuminate within, is like Zhuge Liang, who, amid a confrontation of a million troops, still wore his silk kerchief and waved his feather fan, composed and at ease—and so, at a moment of peril and collapse, he was able to uphold the Western Shu and prolong the lifeline of the Han house. But once the mind is crammed with cares and anxiety, the Square Inch is thrown into disorder and the spirit's tower has no master—just as Xu Shu, for his mother's sake, had to leave Liu Bei and go over to Cao Cao, and from then on, his mind a tangle of hemp, could not offer even half a brilliant plan to help restore the great enterprise. With a mind so confused, how could one speak of settling the realm?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言修道之人，若见日益，不见日损，则心昏而道不凝矣。故曰：「德惟一，二三则昏。」惟随炼随忘，随忘随炼，始不为道障。若记忆不置，刺刺不休，实为吾道之忧也。故必渐消渐灭，至于一无所有，斯性尽矣。然后由无而生有，实为真有，所以能出没鬼神，变化莫测焉。经中「天下」喻道，「取天下」喻修道，「有事、无事」喻有为、无为。人能清净无为，纯是先天一气，道何难成？此即取天下之旨也。若搬运有为，全是后天用事，便堕旁门，此又不足取天下之意也。或曰：采药炼丹，进火退符，安得无为？须知，因其升而升之，非先有心于升也；随其降而降之，非先有心于降也，即至采取不穷，烹炼多端，亦是纯任自然，并无半点造作，虽有为也，而仍属无为矣。彼徒咽津、服气者，乌足以得丹而成道哉？

To sum up, this chapter says that if practitioners of the Way see only that they are "increasing daily" (learning more techniques, hoarding more concepts) and do not see themselves "decreasing daily" (dissolving stray thoughts, letting go of attachments), their mind and spirit will surely grow ever more clouded, and the Great Way cannot coalesce. Hence the ancient text says: "Virtue is precious for being single; once divided two or three ways, it grows dim."

Those who truly know how to apply themselves must "forget as they refine, and refine as they forget"—whenever they gain a bit of insight, they casually set it down and never carry it on their backs as a burden; only then will it not turn into an obstacle to cultivating the Way. If you keep memorizing how well you sat on such-and-such a day or what sensations you once had, and savor and show them off endlessly to everyone you meet, that is truly a grave affliction for cultivation! These posterior-heaven discriminations and attachments must be allowed to melt away bit by bit, until the mind is empty and holds nothing; only then is one's inner nature truly penetrated and complete. And then the living impulse that springs forth from this thorough "nothing" is a real "something," able to pass freely everywhere and respond without fixed method.

In this passage of the classic, "the realm" is a metaphor for the Great Way, "winning the realm" for cultivating the Way, and "having affairs" and "having no affairs" for posterior-heaven deliberate contrivance and prior-heaven pure, still non-action. As long as one can keep to purity, stillness, and non-action, so that the whole body runs naturally on nothing but the One Breath of Prior Heaven, what difficulty is there in accomplishing the Way? This is the deep meaning of "winning the realm always through having no affairs." On the other hand, if one is keen on deliberately conveying the movement of Breath and lets the posterior-heaven mind meddle everywhere, one slides into the side doors and crooked paths—this is the warning of "once they have affairs, they are not fit to win the realm."

Someone may ask: does internal alchemy not also speak of gathering the Medicine and refining the elixir, advancing the yang fire and withdrawing the yin tally (fire phasing)? How can that be called non-action? You must understand that the orthodox alchemical tradition teaches: "Follow the movement of Breath as it rises of itself and illuminate its rising with awareness—never harbor a private intent beforehand to yank it upward; follow it as it descends of itself and accompany its descent—never harbor a private intent beforehand to press it down." Even when it comes to all the subtle warming, nurturing, cooking, and refining of the later stages, everything is left wholly to nature, without the slightest trace of human fabrication—it may look like "action," but at the bone it remains thoroughly "non-action." As for those who rely solely on rigidly swallowing saliva and deliberately puffing out the belly to ingest breath, fussing blindly over the posterior-heaven bodily shell—how could they ever obtain the true elixir and accomplish the Great Way?

Key Terms

  • One Breath of Prior Heaven: The prior-heaven living impulse that naturally governs body and mind in a state of purity, stillness, and non-action; its opposite is the deliberate, posterior-heaven activity of conveying the breath, swallowing saliva, and ingesting breath.
  • Fire phasing (advancing the fire and withdrawing the tally): In this chapter Huang Yuanji offers a crucial corrective—the so-called rising and falling of the fire means "rising as it rises, descending as it descends," never stirring up an intention in advance to manipulate it by force.
  • Square Inch: The human heart-mind, the spirit's tower. Huang Yuanji contrasts Zhuge Liang, who "treated affairs as though there were none," with Xu Shu, whose "square inch was already in disorder," to show that success or failure in both cultivation and worldly affairs is decided by the stillness and composure of the single mind.
  • Forgetting as you refine: Like spitting out sugarcane pulp or peeling a plantain and unreeling a cocoon—once you have walked through a stretch, let go of attachment to the experiences and techniques of that stretch, so that old experience does not become a new obstacle on the Way.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Distinguish the two games of "building skills" and "cultivating mind and nature." Learning a foreign language, learning to program, or training a professional skill truly calls for "increasing daily"; but settling the heart and dissolving anxiety call precisely for "decreasing daily"—hoard fewer self-improvement lists that only make you more anxious, and cut away more of the stray thoughts, fixations, and pointless comparisons that drain your energy.
  2. Practice "busy hands, unhurried mind." When something big truly comes up, there will inevitably be "affairs"; the key is, like Zhuge Liang, first to steady your breathing and pace, handle only the one thing in front of you at a time, and not let anxiety turn the whole board into "every bush and tree a soldier."
  3. Forget yesterday's good state today. Whether you once had a wonderfully comfortable experience in quiet sitting or a standout performance at work, don't keep clinging to it, savoring it, or straining to reproduce it. "Forget as you refine," and begin each time anew with a fresh, ordinary mind.
  4. Go with the flow; don't wrestle with your body. Even in a simple breathing relaxation, just feel the natural rhythm of inhaling and exhaling as it already is, and never hold or push the breath—"rise as it rises, descend as it descends": comfortable and natural is what is right.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Swing the axe so that it whistles like the wind": From Zhuangzi, "Xu Wugui": Carpenter Shi swung his axe with a whoosh of wind and sliced the white plaster off the tip of a man of Ying's nose without harming the nose—a figure for consummate skill.
  2. "The Marquis Wu" and "Xu Yuanzhi": Both from the history and the popular romance of the Three Kingdoms. Zhuge Liang was enfeoffed as Marquis of Wuxiang and directed battles with calm assurance, in silk kerchief and with feather fan. Xu Shu, styled Yuanzhi, originally served Liu Bei; when his aged mother fell into Cao Cao's hands, "his square inch was already in disorder," and he took leave of Liu Bei to go over to Cao Cao, after which he never devised a single plan for Cao Cao for the rest of his life.
  3. "Virtue is single; divided two or three ways, it grows dim": Adapted from the Book of Documents, "Both Possessed Pure Virtue": "When virtue is single, every action is auspicious; when virtue is divided two or three ways, every action is inauspicious."
  4. "Swallowing saliva and ingesting breath": Methods in ancient health-preservation and guiding practices that emphasize posterior-heaven manipulation of the body and breath (such as deliberately swallowing saliva, or holding and gulping the breath). Huang Yuanji is not here rejecting the normal production of saliva in eating, but criticizing the side-door habit of replacing prior-heaven purity, stillness, and non-action with deliberate conveying and attachment to forms.
  5. Textual note: In the classic text, 太上日 has been corrected to 太上曰; in the commentary, 故日 to 故曰, 泪没无存 to 汩没无存, 听我经轮 to 听我经纶, the superfluous comma in 此波复，起 removed to read 此波复起, and 成道裁 corrected to 成道哉.
