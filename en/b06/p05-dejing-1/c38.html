Chapter 38 · Highest Virtue Does Not Strive for Virtue

Highest virtue has no self-conscious design on virtue and therefore attains wholeness, whereas elaborate ritual marks the thinning of loyalty and good faith; in internal alchemy, this points straight to the Mysterious Pass, True Intention and True Breath as matchmakers, and the true secret of subduing the breath for longevity

About This Chapter

Chapter 38 opens the Classic of Virtue (Dejing) with a thunderous insight: "Highest virtue does not take itself for virtue, and therefore truly has virtue; lower virtue never lets go of virtue, and therefore has no virtue." Step by step, Laozi peels back the historical descent from the Way and Virtue down to humaneness, righteousness, and ritual, exposing how purely external ritual arises from the thinning of loyalty and good faith and becomes the doorway to strife, while clever "foreknowledge" is only the flashy blossom of the Way. Hence the person of great resolve dwells in thickness and substance rather than thinness and show.

Huang Yuanji opens the Classic of Virtue with sweeping breadth and aims straight at the core of inner cultivation:

  1. Substance and function of Way and Virtue: First, he resolves the age-old rivalry between Confucianism and Daoism: Way and Virtue are the substance, while humaneness, righteousness, ritual, and wisdom are their function—originally an undivided whole with no antagonism or hierarchy. Only as later hearts grew crafty did people split Way from Virtue and turn ritual and cleverness into tools for outward veneer and suspicious calculation. True wisdom is clarity born from sincerity, never harsh, prying shrewdness.
  2. Direct pointer in internal alchemy: Lamenting that later methods of swallowing saliva, ingesting breath, harvesting medicine, and refining elixirs grew ever more tangled while losing the root, Huang makes an exception and lays bare the true meaning of the single opening of the Mysterious Pass: it is no fixed anatomical spot to stare at rigidly, but that instant of awakening—Reverting the Light and turning back to shore—right in the midst of worldly turmoil and swirling thoughts. He then clarifies with razor precision the partnership between True Intention (the guiding spirit of union) and True Breath (the instrument of union), closing with Qiu Chuji's famous dictum—"If the breath remains unsettled by even a hair's breadth, your life is not yet in your own hands"—and revealing the two senses of "subduing the breath" (fuqi).

Safety note: This chapter emphasizes "subduing the breath" and calming posterior ordinary breathing, yet Huang Yuanji explicitly specifies that the key lies in returning peacefully to the central palace in unmoving stillness and recalling wandering thoughts through lucid awareness—never in forcibly holding or blocking the breath. Beginners in quiet sitting should simply allow breathing to soften and relax naturally, and never deliberately stop the breath, which can cause chest tightness or dizziness.

Original (Chinese)

太上曰：上德不德，是以有德。下德不失德，是以无德。上德无为而无以为，下德为之而有以为。上仁为之而无以为，上义为之而有以为。上礼为之而莫之应，则攘臂而仍之。故失道而后德，失德而后仁，失仁而后义，失义而后礼。夫礼者，忠信之薄而乱之首也。前识者，道之华而愚之始也。是以大丈夫处其厚，不处其薄；居其实，不居其华。故去彼取此。

Lord Lao says: Those of highest virtue do not consider themselves virtuous, and for that very reason truly possess complete virtue; those of lower virtue anxiously cling to not losing their virtue, and for that very reason have no real virtue.

Highest virtue acts effortlessly without contrivance and harbors no ulterior motive; lower virtue acts deliberately and keeps a hidden agenda behind its deeds. Highest humaneness acts, yet does so out of spontaneous care without ulterior design; highest righteousness acts, yet with deliberate judgment and set intent. When it comes to highest ritual, one acts according to prescribed forms expecting an equal response; if others do not respond in kind, one rolls up one's sleeves, thrusts out an arm, and drags them into compliance.

Therefore, only when the Way is lost does Virtue appear; when Virtue is lost, humaneness appears; when humaneness is lost, righteousness appears; when righteousness is lost, ritual appears. As for elaborate ritual, it is the thinning out of loyalty and good faith and the very beginning of disorder. As for clever foreknowledge, it is merely the showy blossom on the surface of the Way and the beginning of folly. Therefore, a person of great resolve abides in thickness and never in thinness, dwells in solid fruit and never in showy blossoms—and thus leaves the latter behind to embrace the former.

Original (Chinese)

上古之风，浑浑噩噩，一任其天，浩浩渊渊，各安其性，上下无为，君民共乐，忠厚成风，讼争不起。何世道之敦庞若此乎？皆由安无为之天，率自然之性，一时各老其老、幼其幼、贤其贤、亲其亲，安耕乐业，食德饮和，不知道德之名，更不闻仁义、礼智之说，然而抱朴完贞，任气机之自动，与天地以同流，俨若不教而化，无为而成，自与道德为一，仁义礼智不相违焉。夫以道德并言，道为体，而德为用；以道德、仁义、礼智合论，则道德又为体，而仁义、礼智又为用。后世圣人，虽为化民起见，而立道德之名，分为仁义、礼智之说，其实道德中有仁义礼智，仁义礼智内有道德，无彼此，无歉缺也。降至后世，而道德分矣。等而下之，仁义礼智，亦多狃于一偏。此皆由气数之推迁，人心之变诈，故至于此。太上欲人返本还原，归根复命，乃为之叹曰：上德无为之人，惟率其性，不知有德，是以其德常存；下德有为之士，知德之美，因爱其名，好行其德，惟恐一失其德，顿丧其名，此两念纷驰，浑沦顿破，不似上德之一诚不二、片念无存，由有德而反为无德也。且上德无为，斯时天下之民，一道同风，群安无为之世；下德有为，际此繁华渐起，俗殊政异，共乐有为之常。岂非「忘机者息天下之机，好事者启天下之事」乎？然时穷则复，物穷则变，人穷则返。当此多事之秋，风俗浇漓，人心变乱，滔滔不返，天真梏没久矣，必有好仁之主，发政施仁，清源正本，易乱为治，转危为安，势不能不有为，然虽有为之迹，而因时制宜，顺理行去，有为仍属无为，所以垂衣裳而天下治也。更有好义之人，际此乱离之日，欲复承平，大兴扫除之功，欣欣自喜，悻悻称雄，不能一归淡定，虽或乂安宇宙，人物一新，而上行下效，民物之相争相夺者，不能已也。至于上礼之君，人心愈变矣，习往来之仪，论施报之道，或厚往而薄来，或施恩而报怨，则不能相安于无事。朝有因革，俗有损益，不能彼此相合，远近同情，稍有不应，而攘臂相争，干戈旋起，不能与居与处而相安。故曰：「失道而后德，失德而后仁，失仁而后义，失义而后礼。」迄于今，人愈变，事愈繁，而忠信之坏已极，不得不言礼以维持之。无如徒事外面之粉饰，不由中心之发皇，酬酢日多，是非愈众。彼缘礼以为维系人心之计者，殆未思应于外不由于中，必至凶终而隙末，欲安而反危。故曰：「忠信之薄，而乱之首也。」他如智非奇计异谋，预度先知之纠察，乃由诚而明，不思而得，不学而能，自然虚明如镜，岂逆诈臆不信所可比哉？然道之华，非道之实，且察察为明，必流于虚诬诈伪而不觉，在己或矜特识，其实愚之始也。是以大丈夫有真识定力，知敦厚以为礼，故取其厚，不取其薄；知虚华之非智，故取其实，不居其华。去取攸宜，而大道不难复矣。

The ways of high antiquity were whole, simple, and uncontrived, entrusting all things to Heaven—vast and deep, with everyone resting peacefully in their own nature. Above and below practiced non-action; rulers and people shared the same joy; honesty and kindness formed the custom of the age, and neither lawsuits nor quarrels arose. Why were the ways of the world so rich and solid? Entirely because people abode in effortless Heaven and followed their natural disposition. Each honored their elders, cared for the young, esteemed the worthy, and loved their kin, tilling the fields in peace, delighting in their work, and nourished by virtue and harmony. They did not even know the words "Way" and "Virtue," still less had they heard theories of "humaneness, righteousness, ritual, and wisdom." Yet because they embraced simplicity, kept their integrity intact, let the natural vital current move on its own, and flowed in unison with heaven and earth, it was as if transformation happened without teaching and completion came without striving—naturally one with Way and Virtue, and never at odds with humaneness, righteousness, ritual, or wisdom.

In truth, when Way and Virtue are spoken of together, the Way is substance and Virtue is function; when Way and Virtue are discussed alongside humaneness, righteousness, ritual, and wisdom, Way and Virtue are again the substance, while humaneness, righteousness, ritual, and wisdom are the function. Although later sages established the names of Way and Virtue and distinguished humaneness, righteousness, ritual, and wisdom in order to guide the people, in reality humaneness, righteousness, ritual, and wisdom dwell within Way and Virtue, and Way and Virtue pervade them in turn—there is no split between them and nothing lacking. Descending to later ages, however, Way and Virtue broke apart; descending further still, humaneness, righteousness, ritual, and wisdom each hardened into one-sided bias. All of this came from the turning of the times and the growing craftiness of human hearts.

Wishing humanity to return to the origin and restore life to its root, Lord Lao sighs: those of highest virtue and non-action simply follow their nature without knowing themselves to be "virtuous," so their virtue abides intact. Those of lower virtue and deliberate striving know how admirable virtue looks; loving the fine reputation, they eagerly perform virtuous deeds while constantly fearing that a single slip will ruin both virtue and name. Between craving praise and fearing loss, two restless thoughts race through the mind, shattering primordial wholeness at once. Unlike highest virtue—single-mindedly sincere, undivided, without a stray thought—their seeming virtue turns into the absence of virtue.

Moreover, in an age of highest virtue and non-action, all under heaven share one Way and one custom, resting together in peaceful ease; in an age of lower virtue and deliberate effort, outward luxury and cleverness spread, customs diverge, policies shift constantly, and everyone delights in busy contrivance. Is this not what the ancients meant by saying, "Those who forget scheming put the world's scheming to rest; those who love stirring up affairs provoke troubles everywhere"?

Yet when a trend reaches its extreme, it turns back; when things reach their limit, they change; when humanity hits bottom, it seeks return. In a turbulent age when customs have grown thin, hearts have turned chaotic beyond recall, and innate innocence has long been shackled and buried, there must arise a humane ruler who enacts benevolent governance, clears the source and straightens the root, turns chaos to order, and transforms danger into safety. Circumstances compel such a ruler to act; yet though traces of action appear, every measure fits the moment and follows natural principle, so action remains effortless non-action—like the sage kings of old who let their robes hang loose and the realm came to order.

Next come champions of righteousness in times of strife and division. Wishing to restore peace, they launch grand campaigns to sweep away wrongs—delighting proudly in their victories, bristling with heroic fervor, unable to return to quiet equanimity. Even if they pacify the realm and renew society for a time, those below imitate those above, and the spirit of contention and seizure among the people can no longer be stopped.

When rulers rely on ritual above all, human hearts have grown subtler and trickier still. Everyone measures the ceremonies of giving and receiving, weighing favors against returns: some give generously and receive coldness, others bestow kindness and reap resentment, and people can no longer dwell together in quiet ease. Courts keep revising statutes; regions add or subtract customs; hearts fail to meet and near and far grow estranged. At the slightest failure to reciprocate properly, people roll up their sleeves to fight and weapons soon clash—making peaceful coexistence impossible. Hence the text says: "When the Way is lost, there is Virtue; when Virtue is lost, there is humaneness; when humaneness is lost, there is righteousness; when righteousness is lost, there is ritual."

Down to our own day, minds have grown ever craftier and affairs ever more tangled; loyalty and good faith have decayed to the utmost, leaving people no choice but to invoke ritual to hold things together. Alas, they work only on outward polish without sincerity welling from the heart; the more social formalities multiply, the more gossip and conflict erupt. Those who hope to bind hearts together through outward forms forget that compliance on the outside without sincerity within inevitably ends badly and breaks apart in bitterness—seeking security, they invite peril. Thus Laozi cuts to the bone: "Ritual is the thinning of loyalty and good faith, and the head of disorder."

As for true wisdom, it is never clever stratagems or suspicious foreknowledge that polices others in advance; it is clarity born from utmost sincerity—knowing without straining to think, capable without contrived study, naturally vacuous and bright like a clear mirror. How could anticipating deceit and presuming bad faith ever compare to that? Such cleverness is only the showy flower of the Way, not its real fruit. Worse still, priding oneself on sharp, prying scrutiny slides imperceptibly into fabrication and deceit: one may congratulate oneself on rare foresight, when in fact it is the beginning of folly. Therefore, a person of genuine insight and steadiness knows that deep honesty is the true root of ritual and chooses thickness over thinness; knows that flashy cleverness is not true wisdom and abides in solid substance rather than showy blossoms. When what to discard and what to keep are rightly judged, the Great Way is not hard to recover.

Original (Chinese)

此言道德废而有仁义，仁义废而有礼智，愈趋愈下，亦人心风俗使然，无足怪者。至于修养一事，咽津服气出而道一变，采药炼丹出而道一变，迄于今纷纷左道，不堪言矣。谁复知玄关一窍，为修道之要务乎！吾今为人示之：人欲识此玄关，须于大尘劳、大休歇后，方能了彻得这个玄关。又曰「念起是病，不续即药」，又曰「放下屠刀，立地成佛」，总不外尘情杂虑纷纷扰扰时，从中一觉而出，即是玄关，所谓「回头是岸」，又曰「彼岸非遥，回光返照即是」。但恐于玄关未开之前，先加一番意思去寻度，于玄关既开之后，又加一番意思去守护，此念虑纷纷，犹天本无云翳，云翳一散即现太空妙景，而却于云翳已散之后，又复加一番烟尘，转令清明广大之天，因之而窄逼难容、昏暗莫辨矣。佛云：「应如是住，如是降伏其心。」此等玄机，总著不得一毫拟议，拟议即非；著不得半点思虑，思虑即错。惟于玄关未开时，我只顺其了照之意；于玄关既开候，我亦安其坐照之常。念若纷驰，我即收回，收回即是；神如昏罔，我即整顿，整顿即是。是何如之简而捷、便而易乎？特患人于床上安床，动中寻动，静里求静，就涉于穿凿，而玄关分明在前，却又因后天知虑遮蔽，而不在矣。吾今示一要诀，任他思念纷纭，莫可了却，我能一觉而动，即便扫除，此即是玄关。足见人之修炼，只此觉照之心，亦如天宫赤日，常须光明洞照，一毫昏黑不得，昏黑即落污暗地狱。苟能拨开云雾，青天白日，明明在前，如生他想，即落凡夫窠臼，非神仙根本。总之，仙家无他玄妙，惟明心见性，乃修炼要诀。若丹是何物，即吾丹田中絪縕元气是也。然此元气，与我本来不二元神，会合一处，即是返还太极无极、父母未生前一点天命。人能以性立命，以命了性，即可长生不死。但水府求玄，欲修成金液之丹，不得先天神息，采取烹炼、进退温养，则先天元性与先天元命，不能自家会合为一、攒五簇六而成金丹。虽然，既得元性、元命矣，若无真正胎息，犹人世男女，不得媒妁往来交通，亦不能结为夫妇。故丹经云：「真意为媒妁。」兹又云「真息为媒妁」，岂不与古经相悖乎？不知真意者，炼丹交合之神；真息者，炼丹交合之具，要皆以神气二者，合之为一而已矣。第无真息，则真气不能自升自降，会合温养，结成玄珠；既得真息，若无真意为之号令，摄持严密，则使真息亦不能往来进退，如如自如。故曰：真意者，炼丹之要。然真意不得真正元神，则真意从何而始？惟于玄关窍开之初，认取这点真意，于是返而持之，学颜子拳拳服膺，斯得之矣。况元神所流露，即是真意，即是「一善」，亦即「得一而万事毕」之道。学人认得分明，大丹之本立矣。昔邱祖云：「息有一毫之未定，命非己有。」吾示学人，欲求长生，先须伏气。然伏气有二义：一是伏藏此气，归于中宫，如如不动；一是管摄严密，降伏后天凡息，不许内外呼吸出入动摇吾固有之神气。久久降伏，自能洗心退藏于密。长生即在此伏气中，除此别无他道。修行人，须照此行持，乃不负吾一片苦衷耳。

This chapter explains how the decline of Way and Virtue gave rise to humaneness and righteousness, and their decline gave rise to ritual and cleverness—slipping ever lower as human hearts and customs shifted, which is scarcely surprising. Yet in the work of inner cultivation, the very same thing happened: first methods of swallowing saliva and ingesting breath appeared and the Way shifted; then elaborate techniques of harvesting medicine and refining elixirs appeared and the Way shifted again; down to our own day, countless side-paths swarm everywhere, too painful even to describe. Who still knows that the single opening of the Mysterious Pass is the true core of cultivating the Way?

Today I will lay this bare for all to see: to recognize this Mysterious Pass, one often comes to clear realization right after great worldly toil and great inner letting go. The ancients say, "When a thought arises, that is the illness; not continuing it is the medicine," and again, "Lay down the butcher's knife and become a Buddha on the spot." Put plainly, right when worldly emotions and tangled thoughts are swirling chaotically, waking up sharply out of them in a single flash of clarity—that very awakening is the Mysterious Pass! That is what is meant by "turning back is the shore," and "the other shore is not far: Reverting the Light in inward reflection is it."

What is most to be feared? Adding extra mental effort to hunt and calculate before the Mysterious Pass opens, and then adding another layer of tense mental effort to guard it after it opens. Piling thought upon thought like this is like a clear sky with no clouds: once the haze disperses, the vast wondrous sky stands revealed on its own; yet right after the clouds clear, you stir up fresh smoke and dust yourself, turning the bright, boundless sky cramped, narrow, and murky! As the Buddhist sutra says, "Thus should one abide, and thus subdue one's mind." This subtle pivot cannot bear a hair's breadth of conceptual conjecture—conjecture misses it at once; it cannot bear a speck of contrived thought—contrived thought goes wrong at once.

Before the Mysterious Pass opens, simply follow your quiet, lucid awareness; once it opens, continue resting easily in that same calm, steady awareness. If thoughts scatter and race, gently recall them—the very act of recalling is the Way. If spirit grows dull and foggy, gently rouse and steady it—the very act of steadying is the Way. How concise, direct, accessible, and easy is that! The real danger is "placing a bed on top of a bed"—seeking movement inside movement, chasing stillness inside stillness. The moment you force contrived effort, the Mysterious Pass stands right before your eyes yet vanishes behind the fog of the posterior cognitive spirit.

Let me hand you one direct, unmistakable formula: no matter how tangled and endless your thoughts seem, the instant you wake up sharply and sweep them clean, that clear, luminous instant is the Mysterious Pass! From this you can see that human cultivation comes down to nothing other than this lucid, illuminating heart—like the blazing sun in high heaven, forever shining brightly without a trace of gloom; the slightest gloom drops one straight into dark, turbid confusion. Once you part the clouds and mist, blue sky and bright sun shine plainly right before you; raise any extraneous fancy on top of that and you fall straight back into the ruts of an ordinary person, far from the root of the immortals.

In short, the school of the immortals holds no bizarre hocus-pocus: "clarifying the mind and seeing one's true nature" is the first essential of practice. And what is the "Elixir"? Simply the warm, swirling primordial Breath within my lower Cinnabar Field. When that primordial Breath unites in one place with my originally undivided Original Spirit, one returns to the Supreme Ultimate and the Boundless—back to that single spark of Heaven's mandate prior to one's parents' birth. Whoever establishes life through pure nature and fulfills nature through abundant life (dual cultivation of inner nature and life) attains enduring longevity.

Yet to seek the deeper mystery in the Palace of Water and bring forth the Golden Liquid Elixir (Golden Flower), without the prior-heaven spiritual breath to harvest, refine, advance, withdraw, and gently nourish, prior-heaven original nature and original life cannot unite of themselves, gather the five elements and six harmonies, and crystallize into the Golden Elixir. Going one step deeper: even after recognizing original nature and original life, without genuine embryonic breathing (True Breath) moving back and forth between them, they are like a man and woman in the world without a matchmaker to mediate between them—they cannot join as husband and wife.

Someone may ask: the ancient elixir classics clearly state that "True Intention is the matchmaker," yet here you say "True Breath is the matchmaker"—does that not contradict the classics? Not at all! True Intention is the governing spirit of alchemical union; True Breath is the concrete instrument of alchemical union. Both are indispensable, and both exist solely to bring Spirit and Breath together into one. Without True Breath, true Breath cannot rise and descend of itself to mingle, warm, and coalesce into the Mysterious Pearl; conversely, even once True Breath is present, without True Intention issuing quiet command and holding everything steadily together, even True Breath cannot come and go, advance and recede, in effortless ease.

That is why alchemists call True Intention the crux of refining the Elixir. Yet where does True Intention spring from if not from genuine Original Spirit? Precisely at the first opening of the Mysterious Pass, recognize that pure outflow of True Intention, then turn inward and hold fast to it—like Yan Hui, who "attained a single good and clasped it faithfully to his breast without ever losing it"—and you have truly succeeded. Indeed, what naturally flows from Original Spirit is True Intention itself, the Confucian "single good," and the Daoist Way of "attaining the One so all affairs are accomplished." Once the student recognizes this clearly, the foundation of the Great Elixir stands firm.

Patriarch Qiu Chuji once said: "If the breath remains unsettled by even a hair's breadth, your life is not yet in your own hands." I tell students plainly today: to seek longevity, you must first learn to "subdue the breath" (fuqi). Subduing the breath has two meanings: first, gently hiding and settling true Breath back into the central palace of Central Yellow, unmoving and at peace; second, steadily guiding and quieting coarse posterior ordinary breathing so that hurried, scattered inhalation and exhalation through nose and mouth never agitate our innate Spirit and Breath. Over long practice, once ordinary breathing is gently subdued, one naturally fulfills the Book of Changes saying: "washing the heart clean and withdrawing into the secret stillness." The entire path of longevity lies inside these two words—"subduing the breath"—and there is no other road beside it. Practitioners, be sure to walk faithfully by this counsel, and do not let my heartfelt plea go in vain!

Key Terms

  • Highest virtue and lower virtue: Highest virtue follows innate nature without taking itself to be virtuous, keeping virtue whole and unmarred; lower virtue clings to reputation and fears losing virtue, falling into contrived calculation and losing genuine virtue.
  • Foreknowledge (qianshi): Clever, presumptuous guessing and suspicious calculation, as opposed to true wisdom born of utmost sincerity; Laozi calls it "the showy blossom of the Way and the beginning of folly."
  • Mysterious Pass: In this chapter Huang Yuanji dismantles rigid attachment to physical body parts, declaring directly that waking up clearly amid tangled thoughts and Reverting the Light in inward reflection is itself the Mysterious Pass.
  • True Intention and True Breath: Both are called the "matchmakers" uniting Spirit and Breath in internal alchemy. True Intention is the lucid governing awareness flowing from Original Spirit (the spirit of union), while True Breath is soft, deep prior-heaven embryonic breathing (the instrument of union); each completes the other.
  • Subduing the breath (fuqi): The closing practical key of the chapter, with two aspects—inwardly settling true Breath in the central palace of Central Yellow in unmoving stillness, and outwardly calming hurried posterior ordinary breathing so it no longer disturbs Spirit and Breath.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Genuine character does not need constant proof. When helping others or keeping your principles, constantly worrying whether people notice or keeping score of favors and returns only drains the heart. Drop the urge for credit and tallying, and life becomes solid and light.
  2. Practice the everyday Mysterious Pass through "one awakening that sweeps clean." You do not need to retreat to a mountain cave: whenever anxiety, doom-scrolling, or rising anger carries you away, the split second you wake up—"My thoughts just swept me off again"—and gently gather your attention back is Huang Yuanji's "turning back is the shore."
  3. Do not "put a bed on top of a bed" in sitting practice. Once thoughts quiet down, simply rest there without interrogating yourself ("Am I in stillness yet? Did the Mysterious Pass open?"). When thoughts wander, bring them back; when drowsiness creeps in, gently straighten your spine and brighten awareness. Simpler is stronger.
  4. Experience "subduing the breath" by slowing down and settling. Notice how shallow and hurried your breathing becomes during stress or irritation. Spend a few quiet minutes each day letting your breath soften, slow, and settle gently toward the middle abdomen, and your mind will naturally steady alongside it.

Translator's Notes

  1. Roll up one's sleeves and drag them in (rangbi er reng zhi): Rangbi means rolling up the sleeves to bare the arms; reng means pulling or dragging by force—depicting formalists who resort to coercion when others fail to reciprocate their rituals.
  2. Solid and rich, thin and shallow, pacifying (dunpang, jiaoli, yi'an): Dunpang means simple, honest, and substantial; jiaoli describes frivolous, thinned-out social mores; yi'an means governing into peace and stability.
  3. Ending in ruin and rift (xiongzhong ximo): Alludes to the Biography of Wang Dan in the Book of the Later Han, referring to relationships that begin with warm outward show yet end in bitter rupture.
  4. Anticipating deceit and presuming bad faith (nizha yibuxin): From the Analects ("Xianwen"): "Not anticipating deceit, not presuming bad faith, yet perceiving clearly beforehand—is that not worthy?"
  5. "Thus should one abide, and thus subdue one's mind": From the Diamond Sutra, the Buddha's answer to Subhūti's question on how those who set out on the path should abide and calm their minds.
  6. "Clasped faithfully to his breast" (quanquan fuying): From the Doctrine of the Mean, praising Confucius's disciple Yan Hui: "Whenever he attained a single good, he clasped it earnestly to his breast and never let it go."
  7. Patriarch Qiu: Qiu Chuji (1148–1227), one of the Seven Perfected of the Quanzhen Northern Lineage and founder of the Longmen branch (written with the variant character 邱 during the Qing to observe the naming taboo for Confucius). "If the breath remains unsettled by even a hair's breadth, your life is not yet in your own hands" is one of his most celebrated alchemical dicta.
  8. "Washing the heart clean and withdrawing into the secret stillness": From the Appended Remarks of the Book of Changes, denoting cleansing away mental dust and storing Spirit and Breath in silent depth.
  9. Textual note: Several printing and OCR errors in the base edition—including 敦魇 → 敦庞, 俨着 → 俨若, 风爷 → 风俗, 牿没 → 梏没, 义安字宙 → 乂安宇宙, 臆『信 → 臆不信, 莫辦 → 莫辨, 特惠 → 特患, 案臼 → 窠臼, 具息者 → 真息者, and 优藏 → 伏藏—have been collated against standard texts and context, and stray footnote fragments interpolated into the main text have been removed.
