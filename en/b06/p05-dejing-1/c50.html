Chapter 50 · The Abundance of Life Begetting Life

The waxing and waning of yin and yang is itself the great virtue of life begetting life, yet worldly people indulge their desires and throw themselves into the ground of death; those good at nurturing life keep to the natural course and apply reversal, establishing their life upon formless utmost sincerity, so that naturally there is no ground of death for anything to exploit

About This Chapter

The lines of Chapter 50—"going out into life, entering into death," "the companions of life are three in ten, the companions of death are three in ten, and those among the people who live but move into the ground of death are also three in ten"—have always drawn widely differing interpretations (some read them as ten out of thirteen, some as the four limbs and nine openings, some as three out of ten). Drawing broadly on the principles of the Book of Changes and on the alchemical Way, Huang Yuanji offers an original two-layered exposition:

  1. The waxing and waning of yin and yang, and "throwing oneself into the ground of death" (§2 to the first half of §3): Heaven, earth, and all things are by nature ceaselessly begetting life amid the filling and emptying of yin and yang and the cycle of day and night—those born on receiving yang breath make up three in ten (endowed with the three yang lines of Qian), and those who end on meeting yin breath make up three in ten (encountering the three yin lines of Kun); this is the "abundance of life begetting life" of great natural transformation. But there is one other kind of person who, though born endowed with yang like everyone else, opens up posterior-heaven cleverness and stirs up desires, indulges feelings and gives free rein to cravings, exhausts essence and depletes spirit, and forcibly pushes themselves into the ground of death—this is "those who move into the ground of death are also three in ten."
  2. The "no ground of death" of those good at nurturing life (second half of §2, middle of §3): Why are those good at nurturing life said to "travel on land without meeting rhinoceros or tiger, and enter an army without being touched by armor and weapons"? Huang Yuanji unravels this along two dimensions: in terms of mind, nature, and virtue, the virtue of utmost sincerity and pure generosity moves all things and naturally dissolves hostility and danger; in terms of cultivating the body and establishing one's life, only a body with form has a ground of death, and once one has cultivated oneself to clarity of mind and spirit and returned to the formless principle of supreme harmony, outer blades and wild beasts have nowhere to inflict their harm.
  3. Not letting the survival or loss of the outward form shake the original mind (end of §3): The final stroke lifts the whole chapter to a very high plane—the physical frame has birth and death, but that is merely the outward "trace." The sages and worthies of old gave their lives for benevolence and regarded the cauldron as an everyday thing precisely because, having attained the Way and being full within, they had no fear whatsoever of life and death in the outer world—this is the true "no ground of death."

Safety note: This chapter involves advanced internal-alchemy metaphors such as "the method of inverting yin and yang," "swiftly pushing the Handle of the Dipper," "rising, descending, warming, and nurturing," and "the elixir completed in nine cycles," and cites ancient legends and symbolic expressions such as "tigers and rhinoceroses tamed," "blades doing no harm," and "gathering, it takes form; dispersing, it becomes breath." Never mistake these classical rhetorical figures for claims that practice can make one "invulnerable to blade and spear," "avert disasters and escape dangers," or confer supernatural powers, and still less imitate, without guidance, any forcible use of intention to drive the movement of breath up and down. This chapter's most practical warning for modern people is to give up the "self-thrown-into-the-ground-of-death" habits of indulging desire, staying up late, and exhausting essence and depleting spirit, and to nurture life steadily in accord with natural rhythms.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：出生入死。生之徒十有三，死之徒十有三，民之生，动之死地亦十有三。夫何故？以其生生之厚。盖闻善摄生者，陆行不遇兕虎，入军不被甲兵；兕无所投其角，虎无所措其爪，兵无所容其刃。夫何故？以其无死地。

Lord Lao says: When people leave the impulse of life, they step into death. Those who accord with the impulse of life and live long and at peace make up three in ten; those who accord with natural decline and move toward death make up three in ten; and there is another kind of person who was living perfectly well, yet through reckless action and indulgence of desire sends themselves into the ground of death—they too make up three in ten. Why is this? Because they over-indulge their cravings and, greedily seeking the abundance of life, actually harm life (or: because the principle of life begetting life in the transforming and nurturing of heaven and earth is by nature abundant and cyclical).

I have heard that one who is truly good at nurturing life travels on land without being harmed by rhinoceros or fierce tiger, and enters the ranks of an army without being wounded by blade, weapon, or armor; the rhinoceros finds no place to thrust its horn, the fierce tiger finds no place to set its claws, and weapons find no place to drive in their blades. And why is this? Because such a person simply has no "ground of death" on them through which death could slip in.

§2
Original (Chinese)

天地之生物也，虽千变万化，无有穷极，而其道不外一阴一阳，盈虚消长，进退存亡而已，其间亦无非一太极之理气流行而已。夫生死犹昼夜也，昼夜循环，运行不息，亦如生死之循环，迭嬗不已。但其中屈伸往来，原属对待两呈，无有差忒。自出生入死者言之，则遇阳气而生者十中有三，逢阴气而死者亦十中有三。其有不顺天地阴阳之常，得阳而生，犹是与人一样，自有生后，知识开而好恶起，物欲扰而事为多，因之竭精耗神，促龄丧命，所谓动之死地者亦十中有三。是生之数不敌其死之数，阴之机更多于阳之机，造化生生之理气，不虞其竭乎？然而太极之元，无声无臭，动而生阳，静而生阴，发为五行，散为万物，极奇尽变，莫可名言，亦无欠缺。所以顺而出之，源源不绝，逆而用之，滴滴归宗。生者既灭，死者又添，死者既静，生者又动，此造化相因之道、鬼神至诚之德，寓乎其间，自元始以至于今，未有易也。不然，万物有生而无死，将芸芸者充满乾坤，天地不惟无安置之处，亦且难蓄生育之机。此消者息之，盈者虚之，正所以存生生之理也。人能知天地生生之厚，即在此消息盈虚，于是观天之道，执天之行，于杀中觅生机，死里求生气，行春夏秋冬之令，合生长收藏之功，顺守逆施。彼天地生化众类，而成万年不敝之天，以此人身返本还原，以作千古非常之圣，亦莫不由此。此岂靡靡者所能任哉？惟善于摄生之人，用阴阳颠倒之法，造化逆行之方，下而上之，往而返之，静观自在，动候阳生，急推斗柄，漫守药炉，返乎太极，复乎至诚，出有入无，亘古历今，同乎日月，合乎乾坤，以之遗大投艰，亦无入不得，即猛如虎兕，亦且化为同俦，利若甲兵，亦皆销为乌有，又何畏兕角之投、虎爪之措、兵刃之加，而计生死存亡于一旦耶？此何以故？以其无死地也。况圣人炼性立命有年，聚则成形，散则成气，日月随吾斡旋，风雷任其驱使，虎兕纵烈，兵刃虽雄，只可以及有形，安能施于无形？天下惟无形者，能制有形，岂有有形者而能迫无形乎？噫！万物有形则有生死，圣人无形则无生死，且主宰乎生生死死之原，万物视之以为生死，有何人灾物害而漫以相加者哉？

Though heaven and earth's production of things changes in a thousand ways and transforms in ten thousand, without end, the principle behind it never escapes the filling and emptying, waxing and waning, advancing and retreating, surviving and perishing of one yin and one yang; and throughout, it is nothing but the principle and breath of a single Supreme Ultimate flowing and circulating.

Human life and death are like day and night: day and night cycle and turn without stopping, and life and death likewise cycle and succeed one another without end. In this contracting and extending, going and coming, yin and yang originally correspond to each other without the slightest discrepancy. Speaking in terms of the common principle of "going out into life and entering into death," those who are born and grow by riding yang breath make up three in ten, and those who age and return to storage on meeting yin breath also make up three in ten. But there is one more kind of person who does not accord with the constant norms of heaven and earth's yin and yang: at birth they clearly received yang breath just like everyone else, yet after they grow up, once posterior-heaven scheming opens and loves, hates, likes, and dislikes multiply, they are harassed all day by material desires, rushing about and calculating without end, until they have exhausted and hollowed out all their Essence, breath, and Spirit, cutting short their own lifespan and throwing away their own lives—these are the so-called "those who move into the ground of death," and they too make up as many as three in ten!

Reckoned this way, the share of life cannot match the share of death, and the occasions of yin depletion are even more numerous than those of yang generation—would not the primordial breath of creation's ceaseless life-begetting then run dry? It would not! For the source of the Supreme Ultimate is by nature soundless and scentless: moving, it gives rise to yang; resting, it gives rise to yin; unfolding, it becomes the Five Phases; dispersing, it becomes the myriad things. However extraordinarily intricate and changeable it may be, beyond the power of words to exhaust, in itself it lacks nothing at all. So, flowing forward and outward from it, it streams on without end; turning back against the current to return to the origin in cultivation (reversal), every drop returns to the source. When an old life ends, it enters the count of dead stillness; when stillness reaches its extreme, a new impulse of life stirs in turn. This is precisely the great Way by which creation's phases give rise to one another, and the abundant virtue of heaven and earth's ceaseless utmost sincerity; from the primordial beginning of the cosmos until today, it has never changed. Just imagine: if the myriad things were only born and never died, the whole of heaven and earth would long since have been packed full—not only would there be nowhere to put them, but no new impulse of generation could accumulate either! It is precisely because life is gestated within waning and fullness returns to emptiness that the fundamental great virtue of the cosmos's ceaseless life-begetting is preserved.

As long as people see through to the fact that heaven and earth's "abundance of life begetting life" is hidden precisely within this waxing and waning, filling and emptying, they can "observe the Way of Heaven and hold to the workings of Heaven"—glimpsing the impulse of life in austere withdrawal, recognizing the breath of life in the depths of stillness; emulating the seasons of spring, summer, autumn, and winter, and according with the pattern of spring's birth, summer's growth, autumn's harvest, and winter's storage; ordinarily keeping to the constant Way along the natural course, and in cultivation reversing back to the origin. Heaven and earth rely on this pattern to transform and nurture the myriad things and establish a firmament that does not decay in ten thousand years; people rely on this pattern to return to the root and restore the origin and to cultivate the character of one who transcends the ordinary and enters sagehood—in no case does it come from anywhere but this Way. How could this be borne by the mediocre who merely drift with the current?

Only those truly good at nurturing life understand the reversal by which yin and yang return to the root: they make the essence and breath that would otherwise dissipate outward go from below to above, from going out to returning; in stillness they observe at ease, in movement they become aware of the arising of the single yang (the Living Zi Hour), following the pivot to grasp the rhythm (which alchemical texts figure as "swiftly pushing the Handle of the Dipper"), while calmly and steadily warming and nurturing the central palace ("leisurely guarding the medicine furnace"), thereby returning to the original source of the Supreme Ultimate and to the state of utmost sincerity without falsehood. When one reaches the point where movement and stillness are unobstructed, one goes out of being and enters non-being, spans past and present, and shares the virtue of the sun and moon, of Qian and Kun, then even when meeting the gravest and most arduous dangers one is at ease everywhere: before such a deep, clear, selfless, and fearless presence, even fierce rhinoceroses and tigers lose their ferocity, and sharp blades, spears, and armor lose any opening to exploit. Why then live in constant dread of the rhinoceros's horn goring, the tiger's claws striking, or blades falling upon the body, panicking and calculating over life and death in a single instant? Why are they able to be like this? Because they have long since had no "ground of death" through which calamity could slip in!

What is more, the sage who has attained the Way, having cultivated inner nature and life for years on end, has long since transcended narrow attachment to the posterior-heaven bodily shell—in the symbolism of the alchemical classics, the original Breath gathers to take form and disperses to return to emptiness, flowing together with the transformations of heaven and earth. However ferocious the tiger and however sharp the blade, they can only harm "things with form" that cling to the physical frame; how could they possibly harm the transparent, unobstructed "formless Way"? Under heaven, only the formless Great Way can govern the myriad things that have form; how could it be that things with form turn around and overpower the formless Great Way? Ah! The myriad things are confined within bodily shells with form, and so they are held fast by life and death; the sage accords with the formless Great Way, and so stands serenely beyond life and death, and moreover penetrates to the very source of all living and dying—what human calamity or harm from things could then lightly befall him?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言十为天地之全数，三为三阳三阴。人禀乾三阳而生，遇坤三阴而死。此原是天地一阴一阳，屈伸往来，循环相因之理，非阴无以成阳，非死无以为生，故休息退藏，无非裕生生之厚德于无疆也。其在纵情肆欲，灭理丧心，不顺阴阳，自戕身命，所谓动之死地，非耶？其生虽与人同，其死却与人异。盖顺阴阳而生死者，固太极之浑然在抱，俱两仪之真气流行；若逆造化而生死者，皆本来之元气无存，因后起之阴邪太甚，故皆曰「十有三」也。十者全数，即道之包罗天地；三者，天一生水，地二生火，一天二地，合水火而为三。且天一生水，金生水也，地二生火，木生火也，四象具焉；土无定位，游行于四象之中，即太极之「纯粹以精」者，主宰阴阳之气，运行造化之机，在天地则为无极，而太极之原在人身，静则无声无臭、不二之元神，动为良知良能、时措之真意，合之即五行也。此天地人物公共生生之厚德，有物则在物，无物则还太虚，不以人物之生死而有加减也。是以善摄生者，入室静修，观我一阳来复，摄之而上升，摄之而下降，摄之而归炉温养，丹成九转，火候十分，所谓「道高龙虎伏，德重鬼神钦」者是，有何虎兕兵刃之害哉？试观古人深山僻处，虎兕为群，豺狼与伍，甘心驯伏，自乐驰驱者不少。又有单骑突出，群酋倾心，弃甲抛枪，敬如神明，爱若父母者。他如孝心感格，贼寇输诚，节烈森严，奸回恻念，皆由至诚之德，有以动之也。观此而「兕无所投其角，虎无所措其爪，兵无所容其刃」，洵不诬也。要之，一元之理气，非造化有形之阴阳，我能穆穆缉熙至于光明，又何生死之有？彼有生死者，其迹也，我能泯其迹，一归浑沦之命、太和之天，虽迹有存亡，而理则长存而不敝，又何生之足乐、死之堪忧乎？古圣人舍身取义，杀身成仁，视刀锯为寻常，烹鼎镬为末事，此何以故？良以有得于中，无畏于外焉耳。故曰「无死地」。

他注：水之成数六，火之成数七，合为十三，亦是。

In this chapter, "ten" represents the complete number of heaven and earth, and "three" represents the three yang lines of the Qian trigram and the three yin lines of the Kun trigram. People are born by receiving the three yang lines of Qian, and at the end they die on meeting the three yin lines of Kun. This is simply the natural, constant principle in heaven and earth of one yin and one yang, contracting and extending, going and coming, cycling and giving rise to one another—without the storing of yin, the generating of yang cannot come about; without the curtain falling on the old, there is no new birth. So the resting and withdrawal of life is nothing other than the endless nurturing of heaven and earth's great virtue of the "abundance of life begetting life."

As for those who indulge their passions and desires, go against heavenly principle and conscience, do not accord with the rhythms of yin and yang, and ruin their own bodies and lives—are they not precisely what the classic calls "those who move into the ground of death"? At birth they were clearly just like ordinary people, yet at death they are entirely different: those who are born, age, fall ill, and die naturally in accordance with the constant principle of yin and yang still embrace the undivided Supreme Ultimate and brim with the upright breath of yin and yang; whereas those who go against creation and ruin themselves through indulgence have long since squandered all their original Breath, their whole being depleted and taken over by posterior-heaven yin evils. That all three kinds of people are described as "three in ten" carries a further layer of numerological meaning: "ten" is the complete number, symbolizing that the Great Way embraces heaven and earth; "three" comes from "Heaven's one gives birth to water, Earth's two gives birth to fire"—one and two combine to make three. Moreover, with Heaven's one giving birth to water (metal produces water) and Earth's two giving birth to fire (wood produces fire), the four images of metal, wood, water, and fire are all complete; earth has no fixed position of its own but circulates at the very center of the four images—it is precisely the purest and most subtle pivot of the Supreme Ultimate, governing the breath of yin and yang and driving the pivot of creation. In heaven and earth it appears as the source of the Ultimate of Non-being and the Supreme Ultimate; in the human body, in stillness it is the soundless, scentless, single and undivided Original Spirit, and in movement it is the True Intention that naturally expresses innate knowing and innate ability, always fitting the moment—taken together, these are precisely the complete Five Phases.

This is precisely the "abundant virtue of life begetting life" held and shared in common by heaven, earth, and all things: when there is a form, it dwells in that thing; when the form disperses, it returns to the Great Void; and it never increases or decreases by the slightest amount because of the birth or death of an individual life.

For this very reason, those who are truly good at regulating and nurturing body and mind practice in quiet, become aware of the impulse of life as the single yang returns within the body, go along with it to nurture its circulation, and bring it back to the central palace to warm and nurture it, until their practice becomes mature and fully rounded (which alchemical texts figure as "the elixir completed in nine cycles, the fire phasing fully attained"). This fulfills the old saying, "When the Way is lofty, dragon and tiger submit; when virtue is weighty, ghosts and spirits revere"—how could there be any harm from fierce tigers or blades? Look at history and the ancient records: many lofty recluses living in remote mountains and wildernesses had tigers, leopards, and packs of wolves as neighbors, and the beasts willingly became tame and did them no harm. There are also those like Guo Ziyi, who rode alone straight into the enemy camp, whereupon every enemy chieftain was won over heart and soul, threw down helmet and armor, and revered and loved him as they would gods or their own parents. Other cases—such as utmost filial devotion moving bandits to surrender, or awe-inspiring righteousness making the treacherous feel shame and be unable to bear doing harm—all came about because a virtue of utmost sincerity and pure generosity within truly moved the hearts of all beings! Judging from these true events, Laozi's saying that "the rhinoceros finds no place to thrust its horn, the fierce tiger finds no place to set its claws, and weapons find no place to drive in their blades" is not false in the least!

In the final analysis, this single breath of prior-heaven supreme-harmony principle transcends the gathering and dispersing of posterior-heaven yin and yang with form. As long as I can long nurture this reverent, clear, open, and upright mind ("profound and deep, continuously bright"), what fear of life and death could there be? So-called having life and having death is merely the "trace" of the physical frame; if I can let go of narrow attachment to outward traces and return to the undivided, imperishable mandate of Heaven and the Great Way of supreme harmony, then though the traces must in the end survive or perish, the inner heavenly principle and true nature endure forever imperishable—what then is there worth greedily delighting in life or fearfully grieving over death? Since ancient times the sages and worthies have given up life to uphold righteousness and sacrificed themselves to fulfill benevolence, regarding knife and saw as ordinary and the boiling cauldron as a trifling matter—what did they rely on? Precisely this: their hearts had already realized the true, imperishable Great Way, and so they had no fear whatsoever of any outer danger. This is "no ground of death" at its highest level!

(Appended note by Huang Yuanji: Other commentators say that in the Five Phases the completing number of water is six and that of fire is seven, and that six and seven added together make thirteen, as an explanation of "ten-and-three"; this too is reasonable.)

Key Terms

  • Reversal (the method of inverting yin and yang): In contrast to the "forward flow" of indulgence and dissipation, the way of nurturing life that gathers in Essence, Breath, and Spirit and returns to the root.
  • Handle of the Dipper ("swiftly pushing the Handle of the Dipper"): The handle of the seven stars of the Northern Dipper rotates with the four seasons; alchemists borrow it as a figure for using the True Intention to grasp the pivotal rhythm of the Living Zi Hour when yang arises.
  • Qian and Kun (the three yang lines of Qian, the three yin lines of Kun): Huang Yuanji explains "the companions of life are three in ten, the companions of death are three in ten" through "born of Qian's three yang, dying at Kun's three yin," showing that life and death belong in essence to the natural waxing and waning of yin and yang.
  • Original Spirit and True Intention: This chapter makes clear that the two correspond to the central earth of the Supreme Ultimate—in stillness, the soundless, scentless, undivided Original Spirit; in movement, the True Intention of innate knowing and innate ability, always fitting the moment.
  • Fire phasing ("the elixir completed in nine cycles, the fire phasing fully attained"): A figure for practice deepening from shallow to profound, refined nine times over until it returns to mature, centered harmony.
  • No ground of death: On the surface level, not indulging desire or acting rashly and not throwing oneself into danger; on the deeper level, possessing within a generous virtue of utmost sincerity and an imperishable true nature, so that one does not give rise to fear over the survival or loss of the bodily shell with form.

Putting It into Practice

  1. First stop the self-depleting habits of "moving into the ground of death." What truly wears down modern people's vitality is usually not natural disasters or wild beasts but "cleverness opening and likes and dislikes arising, material desires harassing and affairs multiplying"—overdrawing yourself by staying up late, emotional self-attrition, binge eating and drinking, and excessive indulgence. Simply digging fewer pits for yourself already beats the great majority of folk health remedies.
  2. Follow the seasonal and daily rhythm of "birth, growth, harvest, and storage." In the daytime, when your energy is high, concentrate on your work (generation); at night, be still when it is time to be still and go to sleep on time (withdrawal and storage). Treat rest as serious work that accumulates the "abundance of life begetting life," not as wasted time.
  3. Use "utmost sincerity and selflessness" to defuse hostility between people. Why is it said that "when virtue is weighty, ghosts and spirits revere"? Many conflicts and dangers are provoked by arrogance, scheming, and confrontation; an open, upright heart with candor and goodwill can naturally dissolve many potential "rhinoceroses, tigers, armor, and weapons" before they ever take shape.
  4. Turn fear into inner steadiness. The more tightly you clutch at outward fame, gain, and vanity, the more weak spots (grounds of death) your life has; the more open, full, and integrated in knowing and acting you are within, the more steadily you can hold your ground in the face of upheaval and impermanence.

Translator's Notes

  1. "The companions of life are three in ten…": Commentators through the ages have explained shi you san (十有三) in different ways, falling mainly into two camps: "three out of ten" (Wang Bi, part of Heshang Gong's interpretation, and the main thrust of Huang Yuanji's commentary here) and "thirteen" (the four limbs and nine openings, the seven emotions and six desires, etc.; Han Feizi and some Daoist commentators). At the end of §3 Huang Yuanji also appends the remark "the completing number of water is six and that of fire is seven, which together make thirteen; this too is reasonable," preserving the alternative reading as well.
  2. "Observe the Way of Heaven and hold to the workings of Heaven": From the opening line of the Daoist classic Yellow Emperor's Scripture of the Hidden Talisman (Huangdi yinfu jing).
  3. "Bearing the great and taking on the arduous": From the Book of Documents, "The Great Announcement": "I am charged with Heaven's service; the great and arduous has been laid upon my person," meaning to shoulder an extremely weighty and difficult mission.
  4. "Riding alone straight out, the chieftains were won over": Refers to the famous Tang general Guo Ziyi's meeting with the Uighurs alone on horseback (recorded in the New Book of Tang, "Biography of Guo Ziyi"). When an allied Tibetan and Uighur army invaded, Guo Ziyi removed his helmet and armor and rode straight into the Uighur camp with only a few dozen horsemen, winning over the Uighur leaders through utmost sincerity and great righteousness; the Uighur officers and men all dismounted and bowed to him in a ring, laid down their arms, and withdrew their troops.
  5. "Profound and deep, continuously bright": From the Classic of Poetry, "Major Odes," "King Wen": "Profound and deep was King Wen—oh, continuously bright and reverent!" It describes the bearing of a sage's virtue: deep, reverent, and luminously unceasing.
  6. Textual note: In the classic text, 太上日 has been corrected to 太上曰; in the commentary, 干变万化 to 千变万化, 出生人死 to 出生入死, 生者既火 to 生者既灭 (the 灭 having lost a stroke), 谩守药炉 to 漫守药炉, 出有人无，耳古历今 to 出有入无，亘古历今, 无人不得 to 无入不得, 祝圣人 to 况圣人, 人票乾三阳 to 人禀乾三阳, 故皆日 to 故皆曰, and 烹鼎镬为未事 to 烹鼎镬为末事.
