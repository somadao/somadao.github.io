Chapter 44 · Hoarding Much Means Losing Much

In the world, craving fame and wealth ends in ruin and heavy loss; in the alchemical Way, knowing contentment and knowing when to stop lays bare the life-or-death crux of "stopping the fire to nourish the elixir" and "seeing through illusory scenes"

About This Chapter

On the surface, Chapter 44 is a perfectly plain warning to the world: which is dearer, fame or your own self? Which matters more, wealth or your life? Whoever craves more and will not let go is bound to suffer grievous loss. Huang Yuanji first follows the grain of human affairs to explain "the contented suffer no disgrace; those who know when to stop meet no danger," and then, in his second section, turns his brush to use the same lines to expose the most treacherous pass in actual internal-alchemy practice—stopping the fire and seeing through illusion:

  1. The worldly lesson of losing oneself for fame and goods (§2): People seek fame and wealth for the sake of their own well-being, yet end up giving their lives for empty renown and heavy riches—is that not putting the branch before the root? Fan Li retired once his work was done and Zhang Liang declined his fief to abstain from grains, both because they knew to pull back before reaching the brim.
  2. "Fame" as a figure for illusory scenes, "goods" as a figure for the true Medicine (§3): In quiet sitting, the greatest dangers are clinging to the alluring beauties, treasures, palaces, and immortals that surface before the mind's eye, and failing to let go once the Medicine has returned to the furnace, stubbornly pressing on with circulation. If the fire is sufficient yet you do not know how to stop the fire, far from preserving the Elixir you will injure the body and scatter the spirit.
  3. Striking out every illusory name and image (end of §3): Whether it is the cognitive spirit stirring up mischief or outer scenes luring you, strike it all out with a single stroke and pay it no heed; only by knowing when to stop, being content, and nourishing steadily does one truly "rest in the highest good."

Safety note: This chapter contains advanced internal-alchemy warnings about "stopping the fire to nourish the elixir," "circulation," and the illusions and mischief of the cognitive spirit that may arise in quiet sitting. During sitting or meditation, never deliberately chase lights, sounds, visions of palaces or figures, or unusual bodily sensations such as "a body beyond the body"; still less should you keep forcing breath along pathways when body and mind already feel hot and tense. If persistent hallucinations, emotional over-excitement, insomnia, or physical discomfort appear, stop practicing at once, return to normal meals, social contact, and daily routines, and seek professional medical or psychological help when needed.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：名与身孰亲？身与货孰多？得与亡孰病？是故甚爱必大费，多藏必厚亡。知足不辱，知止不殆，可以长久。

Lord Lao says: Empty fame or your own life—which is nearer to you? Your life or your goods—which is worth more? Gaining fame and profit or losing your life—which is the true calamity? For this reason, excessive love of outer things is bound to exact a heavy cost, and hoarding much is bound to end in losing much. Whoever knows contentment suffers no disgrace; whoever knows when to stop runs into no danger. Thus one can endure long and stand secure.

§2
Original (Chinese)

夫人之好名、好货者，莫不以名能显扬我身，货足肥润我身。身若无名，则湮没不彰矣；身若无货，则困苦难堪矣。是以贪名者，舍身而不顾；黩货者，丧身而不幸。贾子曰：「贪夫徇财，烈士徇名。」人情类然，古今同慨。然亦思名与身孰亲耶？以名较之，名，外也；身，内也。人只为身而求名，何以因名而丧身？岂名反亲而身反疏乎？货与身孰多耶？以身拟之，身，贵也；货，贱也。人皆求货以为身，何以亡身而因货？岂身反少而货反多乎？亦未思之甚也。夫有名而性不存，与有身而名不显，孰得焉？孰失焉？舍生而货虚具，与失货而命常凝，孰存耶？孰亡耶？以是思之，与其得名货而失身，不如得身而失名货之为愈。况好名位者，损精神，伤生命，甚爱所以大费也；厚储蓄者，用机谋，戕身心，多藏所以厚亡也。望重为国家所忌，积厚为造物所尤，古来势大而罹祸，财多而受诛者，不知凡几？皆由不知敛抑，不自退藏，贪多不止，以致结怨于民，获罪于天也。惟知足知止者，一路平常，安稳到底，无辱无殆，不危不倾，而长保其身家，并及其子孙。范蠡所以无勾践之患，张良所以有赤松之游也，诚知几之士哉！后起者，将有鉴于斯文。

Those in the world who chase fame and crave goods all assume that a good name will bring them honor and luster, and plenty of goods will keep them comfortable and well fed. Without a name, a person sinks into obscurity; without goods, life becomes hard and wretched. So those who covet fame disregard even their lives for empty renown, and those who crave goods ruin themselves by hoarding and meet disaster. Jia Yi of the Han lamented, "The greedy man dies for wealth; the man of ardent spirit dies for fame." Such is human nature on the whole, and it has drawn sighs in every age.

But calm your mind and think it over: fame or your own life—which is nearer to you? Set fame beside your life: fame lies outside you, while your life is the root closest to yourself. People seek fame in the first place to give themselves standing—so how can they end up laying down their lives for it? Could outer renown really be nearer to them than their own lives? And goods or your life—which is worth more? Weigh it by your life: life is precious beyond measure, money as cheap as dirt. People seek wealth in the first place to support themselves—so how can they end up losing their lives over it? Could their own lives really weigh less and their goods weigh more? They simply have not thought it through!

Suppose you win a great name but lose your own inner nature and life, compared with keeping yourself whole but remaining unknown—which is gain and which is loss? Suppose you leave behind a houseful of idle goods but lose your life, compared with letting the money go while your life stays secure and gathered—which is preservation and which is ruin? Thinking it through along these lines, rather than trading your life for fame and profit, it is far wiser to keep your life and give up empty renown and fleeting wealth.

What is more, those devoted to fame and rank rack their brains day after day, gravely draining their Essence and Spirit and maiming their lives—this is "excessive love is bound to exact a heavy cost." Those who hoard family fortunes with all their might scheme and plot at every turn, wearing body and mind full of holes—this is "hoarding much is bound to end in losing much." Too high a reputation easily arouses the suspicion of the court; too great a fortune easily invites the leveling of Heaven's Way. Since antiquity, who can count those whose overwhelming power brought them violent death, or whose vast wealth brought confiscation and ruin? All of it came from not knowing how to rein themselves in, not knowing how to step back at the height of the flood, greedily grasping without ever stopping—until they had earned the resentment of the people and offended against Heaven's principle.

Only those who truly know contentment and know when to stop travel an ordinary road, safe and steady to the very end: they meet no disgrace, fall into no peril, have no fear of collapse or overthrow, keep their persons and households secure for long, and even pass blessings down to their descendants. Fan Li of the Spring and Autumn period escaped the murderous hand of King Goujian of Yue, and Zhang Liang of the early Han retired once his work was done to follow Master Red Pine in pure cultivation and distant wandering—all because they were clear-sighted people who perceived the subtle first signs of things! Those who come after and read this passage should take it as a warning.

§3
Original (Chinese)

此借知足知止者，喻止火养丹，以名喻景、货喻药。贪幻景者，多被魔缠；好搬运者，难免凶咎。药未归炉，宜进火以运之；药既入鼎，宜止火以养之。火足不知止火，非但倾丹倒鼎，致惹病殃，亦且丧命焚身，大遭危殆。又况大道虚无，并无大异人处。或贪美酒美味、艳色艳身、金玉珠玑、楼台宫殿，又或天魔地魔、鬼魔神魔，种种前来试道，或充为神仙，夸作真人，自谓实登凌霄宝殿，因此一念外驰，以致精神丧败，大道无成者不少。又或识神作祟，三尸为殃，自以为身外有身，而金丹至宝遂戕于顷刻者亦多。若此等等，总由火足不止火，丹回不养丹，所以志纷而神散，外扰而中亡。修炼之士，幻名幻象、幻景幻形，须一笔勾销，毫不介意，如此知止知足，常养灵丹，则止于至善，永无倾颓焉。

From the standpoint of actual internal-alchemy practice, this chapter in fact borrows the worldly ideal of "knowing contentment and knowing when to stop" as a figure for the crucial step in cultivation of "stopping the fire to nourish the Elixir": "fame" stands for the various illusory scenes that surface in quiet sitting, and "goods" stand for the true Medicine within the body. Those who crave strange illusory scenes are mostly entangled by inner demons; those who are infatuated with circulating the vital current and never know when to let go can hardly avoid danger and misfortune.

In the measured rhythm of the alchemical method, it is only when the vital current first stirs and has not yet returned to the furnace of the central palace that one speaks of advancing the fire in moderation to move it. But once the true Medicine has entered the cauldron of the central palace, one must stop the fire at once and turn to pure, still, warm nourishment. If the fire phasing is already sufficient and one stubbornly refuses to stop the fire, not only will the hard-won vital current be scattered completely and bring illness and misfortune upon body and mind—one may even be scorched by dry fire and fall into grave danger.

Besides, the true Great Way is by nature empty and plain; there is nothing mystifying or flamboyantly different from ordinary people about it. Yet some practitioners have a taste for the sensational: in the quiet state they crave fine wine and rich food, beautiful figures and seductive bodies, gold, silver, pearls, and jewels, jade towers and marble halls; or they meet so-called demons of heaven and demons of earth, ghosts, spirits, and all manner of uncanny visions that come to test their Way. Some even grow giddy, posing as immortals and boasting of being Perfected Ones, truly believing they have ascended to the Hall of Lingxiao in the heavens. Because of this single thought rushing outward, no small number end up with their vital spirit shattered and the Great Way unaccomplished. Others are troubled by the mischief of the cognitive spirit and the disturbance of selfish desires within (which alchemical texts call the "Three Corpses"): what is plainly an illusion woven by the mind they mistake for having refined "a body beyond the body" (see spontaneous Dharma Body), and many of them, too, destroy in an instant the originally pure foundation of the Golden Elixir.

All these kinds of deviation come down, at root, to "the fire is sufficient yet one does not stop the fire; the Elixir has returned yet one does not nourish the Elixir." So the will becomes confused and the Original Spirit dissipates; outwardly one is led by the nose by illusory scenes, while inwardly the genuine vitality is completely lost. Therefore, true cultivators of the Way must strike out with a single stroke every illusory name, light, scene, and form, without giving them a second glance. Able thus to know when to stop and to be content, nourishing body and mind plainly, calmly, and steadily, one comes to rest in the most complete, pure, and still state, forever free from the danger of collapse and ruin.

Key Terms

  • Stopping the fire: The crucial point of fire phasing at which, once the fire is sufficient and the vital current has returned to the center, one immediately halts all deliberate refining and added mental effort and turns to pure, still, warm nourishment.
  • Fire phasing and Medicine: Alchemists use "Medicine" as a figure for prior-heaven original Breath, and "fire phasing" for the intensity and the advancing-and-withdrawing rhythm of one's effort; Huang Yuanji stresses that "once the Medicine has entered the cauldron, one should stop the fire to nourish it."
  • Cognitive spirit: The posterior mind of discrimination and deluded thought; the hidden hand behind the strange illusory scenes in quiet sitting that make people imagine they have "ascended as immortals" or "sent out the spirit" is mostly the mischief of the cognitive spirit.
  • Spontaneous Dharma Body ("a body beyond the body"): The holy state of consummation at the highest level of internal alchemy; Huang Yuanji uses it to warn practitioners never to mistake illusions of the cognitive spirit for a body beyond the body.
  • Illusory scenes and the Three Corpses: Alchemists call all the sensory hallucinations—beautiful figures, towers, gods, and ghosts—that pop up in stillness "illusory scenes," and use the "Three Corpses" as a figure for the covetous habits lurking in the human heart; all must be struck out with a single stroke and ignored.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Regularly rank "self versus goods." When overtime, late nights, anxious rat-race competition, or clinging to face leave you plainly sleepless, exhausted, or quick to blow up, stop and ask yourself "fame or my own life—which is nearer?" and resolutely cut out the vanity that overdraws your body.
  2. In any practice, know to quit while you are ahead. Whether in quiet sitting, breathing exercises, or intense workouts, stop at the right point once body and mind feel at ease, slightly warm, or clear and settled; never grind on adding more just to "earn a bit more sensation"—that is the everyday version of "stopping the fire."
  3. "Strike out with a single stroke" every sight and sound that appears in sitting. When points of light, images, odd sounds, or a floating feeling occasionally surface as you sit with eyes closed, they are mostly random signals of a relaxing nervous system or fabrications of the cognitive spirit. Do not panic, do not cling, and do not give them mystical meaning, and they will disperse on their own.
  4. At the high point of good fortune, learn to leave space on purpose. The brilliance of Fan Li and Zhang Liang lay not in charging forward but in knowing when to exit. Doing things to eight-tenths and leaving three-tenths unsaid keeps you safe longer than pushing everything to the extreme.

Translator's Notes

  1. "The greedy man dies for wealth; the man of ardent spirit dies for fame": From Jia Yi's "Rhapsody on the Owl" of the Western Han (also in the Records of the Grand Historian, "Biographies of Qu Yuan and Master Jia"); xun 徇 means to give one's life for, to chase at any cost.
  2. "Fan Li escaped the danger of Goujian; Zhang Liang went wandering with Master Red Pine": Fan Li, a minister of Yue in the Spring and Autumn period, helped King Goujian destroy Wu, then, knowing that the king "could share hardship but not share ease," resigned his post and sailed off across the Five Lakes, dying a natural death. Zhang Liang, after helping Liu Bang win the empire in the early Han, declared that he wished to leave worldly affairs behind and wander with Master Red Pine (abstaining from grains in pure cultivation), and so escaped the fate that befell the dynasty's meritorious ministers.
  3. "Three Corpses" (sanshi): A traditional Daoist term, also called the "Three Peng," referring to three dark, yin influences dwelling in the human body that love to lure people into craving pleasure; internal alchemists often use it for the selfish desires and deluded thoughts of the cognitive spirit lurking in the heart.
  4. Textual note: A stray dot "•" at the head of the commentary has been deleted; in 黩货者，丧身而不幸 ("those who crave goods ruin themselves and meet disaster"), the original may have read 不恤 or 不惜 ("without regard"), but lacking firm graphic evidence the base text is retained as is; 凶答 has been corrected to 凶咎, the traditional form 又況 standardized to 又况, 凌宵宝殿 corrected to 凌霄宝殿, and 识神作票 corrected to 识神作祟.
