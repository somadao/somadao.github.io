VI · Classic of Virtue (Ch. 51–60) · 德经

Chapter 56 · Most Honored in the World

One who truly knows the Great Way shuts the mouth once the work takes hold—blocking the openings and closing the gates to nourish Breath, blunting sharpness and untangling knots to settle Spirit, softening the light and joining the dust to hide one's edge—and so never falls into nearness or distance, benefit or harm, honor or disgrace

About This Chapter

Chapter 56 of the Daodejing opens with the timeless line "those who know do not speak; those who speak do not know," and goes on to set out the state of mysterious sameness: "blocking the openings, closing the gates, blunting sharpness, untangling knots, softening the light, joining the dust." Holding closely to both actual practice and the art of keeping a low profile in the world, Huang Yuanji analyzes it with particular sharpness:

  1. Why "those who know do not speak" (§2): Those who have truly realized the Great Way scarcely have time enough, day and night, to quiet the mind and safeguard what they have gained—where would they find the leisure to show off and hold forth everywhere? Moreover, whatever can be put into words is only husk and dregs; the true taste is like drinking water, where only the drinker knows whether it is cold or warm. Once the tongue lets it slip, one not only scatters one's original Breath and disturbs one's Spirit, but also readily invites the world's envy and resentment.
  2. Three stages of the work of mysterious sameness (§2): "Blocking the openings and closing the gates" is gathering in the senses and nourishing the original Breath; "blunting sharpness and untangling knots" is wearing down one's edge, dissolving stray thoughts and settling the mind and Spirit; "softening the light and joining the dust" is blending with ordinary life and hiding one's edge, not striving to stand out—at odds neither with oneself nor with others.
  3. Escaping the dualistic trap of "nearness and distance, benefit and harm, honor and disgrace" (§3): People who love to hold forth mostly want, deep down, to win others' closeness, favors and esteem. They do not realize that in the world, where there is nearness there is distance, where there is benefit there is harm, where there is honor there is disgrace. Only by seeking within oneself rather than from others, and nourishing oneself in clarity and stillness, can one transcend all outer clinging, praise and blame, and attain the true honor of being "most honored in the world."

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：知者不言，言者不知。塞其兑，闭其门，挫其锐，解其纷，和其光，同其尘，是谓玄同。故不可得而亲，不可得而疏，不可得而利，不可得而害，不可得而贵，不可得而贱，故为天下贵。

Lord Lao says: Those who have truly realized the Great Way do not lightly hold forth, and those who talk grandly all day have not truly realized the Way. Block the leaking openings of the mouth and senses, close the gates that run out after things; blunt the sharp edge, untangle the knotted confusion; soften the dazzling light, merge with the rolling dust of the world—this is called the deep, unobstructed "mysterious sameness."

Those who reach this state can neither be deliberately courted and drawn close by the world, nor pushed away and kept at a distance; they can neither be won over with benefits nor harmed with misfortune; they can neither be raised up and honored nor brought low and disgraced—and precisely because of this, they become the most honored in the world.

§2
Original (Chinese)

大凡无德之人，当其闻一善言，见一善行，辄欣欣然高谈阔论，以动众人之耳，取容悦于一时，不知革面洗心，返观内证。孔子曰：「道听途说，德之弃」，洵不诬矣。若真知大道之人，方其偶有所知，朝夕乾惕之不暇，安有余力以资口说，徒耸外人之听闻耶？即令温故知新，悠然有会意处，亦自有之而自得之，犹饮食餍饫，既醉既饱，惟有自知其趣味，难为外人道也。彼好与人言者，殆有不足于己者焉。而况德为己德，修为己修，知之既真，藏之愈固，窃恐一言轻出，即一息偶离，斯道之失于吾心者多矣。此知者所以不言也。若言焉者，其无得于己，实不知乎道。使果有所知，又孰肯轻泄如斯乎？是言者不知，益审矣。又况不可言者精华，可言者皆糟粕。知者非不言，实难言也。言者非不知，盖徒见其皮肤耳。所谓「得了手，闭了口」者，诚知得道匪易，讵容以语言耗其气，杂妄损其神，矜才炫能标其异，徒取恶于流俗哉？以故有道高人，塞兑闭门，养其气也；挫锐解纷，定其神也；和光同尘，则随时俯仰，与俗浮沉，如愚如醉，若讷若痴，众人昏昏，我亦昏昏，不矜奇，不立异，与己无乖，于世无忤也。苟有一毫粉饰之心、驰骛之意，即不免放言高论，以取快于一堂。如此者，非为名，即为利。岂不闻太上告孔子之言乎：「可食以酒肉者，我得而鞭扑；可宠以爵禄者，我得而戮辱。」惟闭户潜修，抱元守一，神默默，气冥冥，沉静无言，恬淡无欲，无为为为，无事为事，则人不可得而亲，亦不可得而疏，不可得而利，亦不可得而害，不可得而贵，亦不可得而贱。此求诸己，不求诸人，尽其性，复尽其命，故为天下之所最贵。三界内外，惟道独尊。我修我道，即我贵我道，天下无有加于此者。太上曰：「知我者希，则我贵焉。」学者亦知之否？

As a rule, people lacking in true cultivation of Virtue, whenever they happen to hear a fine saying or see a good deed, immediately hold forth with great delight, to stir the ears of the crowd and win a moment's praise and favor, never knowing to wash their hearts and change their ways within themselves, to turn the light around and look within, and to verify it in their own experience. Confucius said, "To pass on along the road what one has heard along the way is to cast away Virtue!" This is truly no exaggeration.

Those who have truly realized the Great Way are entirely different. When they have just gained some insight, they scarcely have time enough, morning and evening, to stay vigilant and safeguard it—where would they find spare energy to make it a topic of talk and needlessly startle the ears of others? Even when the work deepens and, reviewing the old and learning the new, they come to a profound understanding at their ease, it is something they truly possess themselves and quietly take in for themselves—like having feasted on fine wine and food until drunk and full, where only they themselves know the sweetness of it clearly, and there is no way to explain it to an outsider. Those who cannot help telling everyone they meet are mostly empty inside and lacking in substance.

What is more, Virtue is the Virtue cultivated in one's own person, and the work is the work one has refined oneself through real effort; the more genuinely one grasps it, the more closely one guards it. One fears that if a single word slips out carelessly, in that instant the mind and Spirit will leak outward by a breath, and far too much of the Great Way will slip away from one's heart! This is the reason "those who know do not speak." Conversely, those who are always talking grandly have gained nothing real in themselves and in fact do not understand the Great Way at all; if they truly understood even a little, who would be willing to let the secrets of heaven slip out so carelessly? Seen in this light, the principle that "those who speak do not know" is all the more evident beyond doubt.

Besides, what truly cannot be spoken is the essence of the Way; whatever can be poured out of the mouth is all dregs. Those who have realized the Way are not deliberately putting on airs and refusing to speak; it is that its mysterious subtleties are truly hard to convey in words. Those who talk emptily are not wholly ignorant; they have only stayed at the surface, at the skin. Alchemists often say, "Once you've got it in hand, shut your mouth," precisely because they know deeply how hard it is to attain the Way—how could they bear to spend their original Breath on idle words, injure their mind and Spirit with scattered deluded thoughts, and show off their talents and set themselves apart, only to draw the world's resentment and dislike?

Therefore, truly accomplished masters "block the openings and close the gates" in order to nourish their original Breath; they "blunt sharpness and untangle knots" in order to settle the mind and Spirit; and "softening the light and joining the dust" means bowing and rising with the times, sinking and floating along with the world, looking foolish, looking slightly tipsy, seeming slow and dull—when the crowd is muddled, I too appear muddled; not flaunting the marvelous, not proclaiming oneself different, going against neither one's own original heart nor anything in the world.

So long as the heart still harbors a trace of self-embellishing vanity or a hint of outward-running clinging, one cannot help holding forth with grand pronouncements, for the pleasure of applause from a full room. Those who do this, if not for fame, are for profit. Have you not heard the words Lord Lao addressed to Confucius? "Whoever can be won over by feeding him wine and meat, I have a hold on him and can whip him; whoever can be bought with the favor of rank and salary, I have control over him and can humiliate him."

Only by closing the door and cultivating in secret, embracing the Origin and guarding the One, letting the Original Spirit sink deep in silence and the original Breath lie hidden in darkness, quiet and sparing of words, serene and without desire, taking non-action as action and non-affairs as one's affair—only in such a state can the world neither deliberately draw close and court such a person, nor push them away and keep them at a distance; neither tempt them with profit nor harm them with misfortune; neither lift them up by power nor bring them low by humiliation. All of this comes from seeking verification within oneself rather than begging from others, fulfilling one's inner nature completely and making one's true life whole; hence one becomes the most honored in the world. Within and beyond the Three Realms, the Great Way alone is supreme; to cultivate my Way is to accomplish my own honor, and nothing in the world can rise above it. In Chapter 70 Lord Lao also says: "The fewer who understand me, the more precious I am." You who study the Great Way, do you grasp this deeper meaning?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言有道之人，必不轻言，以世上知道者少，苟好腾口说，不惟内损于己，亦且外侮于人。《易》曰：「机事不密则害成。」古来修士，因轻宣机密，以致惹祸招灾者不少，是以君子慎密而不出也。即使可与言者，亦兢兢业业，其难其慎，试之又试，然后盟天质地，登坛说法，亦不敢过高过远，刺刺不休。足见古人韬光养晦之功，即见古人重道敬天之意。彼轻易其言者，皆无得于己，不知道者也。若果知之，自修自证之不遑，又安有余闲以为谈论地耶？彼放言无忌者，非欲人亲之、利之、贵之乎？不知有亲即有疏，有利即有害，有贵即有贱，何如缄默不言，清静自养，使人无从亲疏、利害、贵贱之为得欤？夫以我贵我道，自一世可至万世，天下孰有加于此者？学者修其在己，刻刻内观，勿徒议论之风生可也。

This chapter says that people of the Way never speak lightly. Since there are very few in the world who truly understand the Way, if one insists on showing off with one's tongue, within one depletes one's own Essence, Breath and Spirit, and without one invites others' slights and resentment. The Book of Changes says, "If vital secrets are not kept closely, great affairs are ruined." Since ancient times, not a few practitioners have brought disaster and misfortune on themselves by carelessly divulging the secrets of the work; therefore the noble person is always careful and discreet and does not let things out.

Even on meeting a vessel truly worthy of receiving the transmission, the enlightened teachers of old were diligent and cautious, careful and careful again, testing the student's conduct and resolve over and over, and only then solemnly swore an oath before heaven and opened the altar to expound the teaching; and even when they did speak, they dared not speak in lofty, abstruse, far-fetched terms or chatter on without end. From this one can see both the deep cultivation of the ancients in veiling their light and keeping a low profile, and their devout reverence for the true Way and awe of heaven's mandate.

By contrast, those who open their mouths only to talk grandly have no real attainment in themselves and do not understand the true Way at all. If they truly understood it, they would be too busy practicing and verifying it themselves—where would they find the leisure to sit there all day holding forth? Those who speak without restraint and make grand pronouncements—do they not, deep down, hope that others will draw close to them, give them benefits, and raise them up? Yet they fail to understand that in the world, as soon as there is "nearness," "distance" follows; wherever there is "benefit," "harm" lurks behind it; wherever there is "honor," sooner or later it falls into "disgrace." How can that compare with closing one's mouth in silence, nourishing oneself in clarity and stillness, and leaving others no way to cling to one as near or far, or to manipulate one through benefit and harm, honor and disgrace—which is far more solid and at ease?

The honor of the Great Way accomplished through one's own real cultivation can extend from this one life to a thousand ages and ten thousand generations—what in the world could surpass it? Students need only bring the work down into their own person, turning the light around and looking within at every moment, and must never stir up an empty wind of grand talk!

Key Terms

  • Blocking the openings and closing the gates: The Daoist work of gathering in. "Openings" (dui) are the mouth and the apertures of the senses; "gates" are the doors through which the mind and intention go in and out. Huang Yuanji explains it as restraining sights, sounds and speech and nourishing the original Breath.
  • Blunting sharpness and untangling knots: Wearing down the posterior-heaven competitive edge and dissolving the tangles of thought; Huang Yuanji explains it as the work of settling the mind and Spirit.
  • Softening the light and joining the dust (mysterious sameness): Restraining the radiance of one's inner wisdom, not striving to stand out, blending plainly into ordinary human life without drifting with the current, and reaching a deep, unobstructed accord between self and things.
  • "Once you've got it in hand, shut your mouth": An old alchemists' saying, meaning that once one's practice has truly taken hold and one first glimpses the true moment, one must keep strict silence and nourish it quietly, never boasting and letting the Breath leak away.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Break the urge to "teach others as soon as you understand a little." If, after reading a couple of books or having a few experiences in sitting meditation, you can't help holding forth everywhere, you will quickly spend the bit of clarity and steadiness you have just gathered. "Once you've got it in hand, shut your mouth" is the best iron rule for protecting the Way.
  2. Use "softening the light and joining the dust" to test your own cultivation. A true practitioner looks quite ordinary and is easy to get along with, neither putting on mystical airs nor wounding those around them with a sense of being "at a higher level than you."
  3. See through the two-sidedness of "nearness and distance, benefit and harm, honor and disgrace." Those who praise you for the sake of benefit or reputation today may trample you tomorrow for the same reasons. Bring your anchor of value back to the inner growth of "seeking within oneself," and outer flattery and neglect will no longer be able to hurt you.
  4. Talk less and save your true Breath. In daily work and social life, consciously cut down on pointless complaining, arguing and gossip, and you will soon feel for yourself the return of energy that comes from "blocking the openings and closing the gates to nourish Breath."

Translator's Notes

  1. "To pass on along the road what one has heard along the way is to cast away Virtue": From the Analects ("Yang Huo"), where Confucius' original wording criticizes passing on fine sayings one has heard without putting them into practice, merely showing them off in casual talk.
  2. "Whoever can be won over with wine and meat… whoever can be bought with rank and salary…": The sense adapts old Daoist tradition and the Zhuangzi's theme of "accepting no benefit from others" and "not being caught in the world's net," showing that once a person craves wine and meat, fame and profit, they have given others a handle by which to control them.
  3. "The fewer who understand me, the more precious I am": From Chapter 70 of the Daodejing, which reads 知我者希，则我者贵; Huang Yuanji quotes it here as 则我贵焉, stressing that the true Way is rare and self-honoring.
  4. "If vital secrets are not kept closely, great affairs are ruined": From the Book of Changes, "Appended Statements I": "If the ruler is not discreet he loses his ministers; if a minister is not discreet he loses his life; if vital secrets are not kept closely, great affairs are ruined. Therefore the noble person is careful and discreet and does not let things out."
  5. "Chatter on without end" (cici buxiu): A classical idiom in which cici describes endless chattering, as in Han Yu's "Preface Seeing Off Vice Director Yin": "His talk ran on and on without stopping."
  6. Textual note: 日 in 太上日 in the classic and in 《易》日 in the commentary has been corrected to 曰; 已 in 不足于已, 已德, 已修 and 无得于已 has been corrected throughout to 己; 而祝德为已德 has been corrected, by sense and character shape, to 而况德为己德; 杂安损其神 → 杂妄损其神; 手世无忤也 → 于世无忤也; 荷有一毫 → 苟有一毫; 求请己，不求请人 → 求诸己，不求诸人; and 不惟内损于，亦且外侮于人 has been supplied with the missing character, following the parallel structure, to read 内损于己.
