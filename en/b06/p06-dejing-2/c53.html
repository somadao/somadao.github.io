Chapter 53 · Walking the Great Way

The Great Way is as plain as everyday conduct, and innate knowing, the instant it stirs, is most at risk of slipping into posterior-heaven cunning; governing the body is like governing a court—rather than flaunting embroidered robes and sharp swords outside, fill the Three Treasures within and steal back the original Breath of Prior Heaven

About This Chapter

On its surface, Chapter 53 is a scathing attack on a neglected government and an extravagant nobility, the culture of the "robber chief" (daogan). Huang Yuanji, however, turns it over layer by layer and reads it as a razor-sharp manual for "guarding against deviation and correcting error":

  1. The Great Way is utterly ordinary, yet the human heart loves shortcuts: The sudden flash of understanding in "if I had even a little knowledge" is exactly the innate, Prior-Heaven true conscience Mencius describes when someone "suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well." When true conscience first stirs it is plain and direct; the danger is that it slides at once into posterior-heaven calculation ("only acting on it is to be feared"), and worse still, that impatient seekers of quick merit find the Middle Way too bland and go hunting down the side paths of the strange and the occult.
  2. The state as a figure for the body—empty within and showy without is the greatest danger: The court's laws are slack, the fields overgrown, the granaries empty, yet outside there are fine robes, sharp swords, rich foods and hoarded goods. This is like the practitioner who will not restrain desire and preserve sincerity in the heart, nor fill out the Three Treasures of Essence, Breath and Spirit within, but only wants a glowing complexion and an impressive reputation, or is even infatuated with strange magical arts.
  3. Telling the "true stealing of the pivot" from "stealing an empty name": Truly superior cultivation, like the "knowing how to steal" spoken of in the Book of Changes, steals back inwardly the numinous yang Breath of heaven and earth. A lustrous complexion, and all kinds of extraordinary feats and marvels, can never be taken as proof of having attained the Way.

Safety note: The final section of this chapter mentions that when the ancients refined Essence and Breath, old illnesses might turn into "sores, ulcers, pus and blood, expelled through urine and stool." This is a traditional saying from an age without modern medical knowledge. In modern life, any bleeding, suppuration, skin ulceration or abnormality of urine or stool is a health warning sign that calls for prompt medical examination. Never treat it as a "purging reaction" or as "yin dissolving and yang growing" and so delay diagnosis and treatment!

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：使我介然有知，行于大道，惟施是畏。大道甚夷，而民好径。朝甚除，田甚芜，仓甚虚；服文彩，带利剑，厌饮食，财货有余，是谓盗竿。非道也哉！（他经不书「盗竿」，书「盗夸」，言以夸大为道也。）

Lord Lao says: Were I to have even a little true knowledge, walking on this Great Way, what I would fear most is straying, going crooked, acting rashly. The Great Way is very level and broad, yet people love to squeeze down byways and take shortcuts. At court, the laws have all been abolished and left in ruin; the fields are choked with weeds and the granaries stand empty. Yet those on top wear gorgeous embroidered robes, carry sharp swords at their belts, are sated with every delicacy, and pile up more goods and wealth than they could ever use—this is called the "robber chief" (daogan, the ringleader of brigands). How could this be the Great Way! (Huang Yuanji's original note: other editions do not write "robber chief" but "robber's boast" (daokua), meaning that boastful show is taken for the Way.)

§2
Original (Chinese)

君子之道，造端夫妇；圣人之道，不外阴阳。苟能顺天而动，率性以行，成己为仁，成物为智，合内外而一致，故时措而咸宜，有何设施之不当，是令人可畏乎哉？无如道本平常，并无隐怪，末世厌中庸而喜奇异，遂趋于旁蹊曲径而不知。有如朝廷之上，法度纪纲，实为化民之具，而彼昏不觉，概为改除，且喜新进而恶老臣，好纷更而变国政，先代典型尽为除去，犹人身之元气伤矣。朝无善政，野少观型，于是惰农自安，田土荒芜，草莱不治，财之源穷矣。靡费日甚，仓廪虚耗，菽粟无存，财之储罄矣，非犹人身之精气概消磨，而无复有存焉者乎？不图内实，只壮外观，由是衣服必极光华，刀剑务求精彩，饮食须备珍馐，财货更期充足，不思根本之多匮，惟期枝叶之争荣，如此而欲取之无尽，用之不竭，在在施为俱无碍也，不亦难乎？是皆由不顺自然之天，日用常行之道，有以致之也。犹盗者窃物，藏头露尾，如竿之立，见影而不见形，喻修道者之以假乱真也。大道云乎哉！

The Doctrine of the Mean says, "The Way of the noble person begins in the relation of husband and wife": the most ordinary family bonds are the starting point of the Way. And the Way of the sages, when all is said, never goes beyond the principle of yin and yang. If a person can truly act in accord with heavenly principle and conduct affairs by following their inner nature—completing oneself, which is humaneness, and completing others, which is wisdom—uniting inner cultivation and outer action into one, then whatever they do at any time and place will be fitting. Where then would there be any improper action to make one anxious and fearful?

Alas, the Great Way is utterly plain and has no mysterious or bizarre tricks about it; yet in this latter age people find the Middle Way too commonplace, love to chase after the novel and the strange, and without even noticing slip into crooked doors and rugged byways.

It is just like the court. Laws, institutions and the cords of order are the basic tools for transforming the people, yet a muddled ruler, wholly unaware, changes and abolishes them all. He favors the newly arrived climbers and dislikes steady old ministers, loves to reverse orders from morning to evening and toss the affairs of state about, and sweeps away every fine model left by earlier generations—this is like a person utterly wounding their own original Breath! When the court has no good government, the people lose their models too; lazy farmers think only of idle ease, the fields are overgrown and untended, and the very source of wealth runs dry. Add to this extravagance and waste growing worse by the day: the granaries are emptied, not a grain of beans or millet remains, and the family fortune is squandered—is this not just like a person consuming the Essence and Breath of the body until nothing at all is left?

Empty within, yet unwilling to find a way to fill out the root, such people only know how to prop up outer display: their clothes must be the most dazzling and splendid, their swords forged as finely and keenly as possible, their meals stuffed with rare delicacies to the point of surfeit, and their wealth hoarded ever higher. The root has long since withered, yet they never think of nourishing it, hoping only that the branches and leaves outside will vie in splendor. To expect, while doing this, that what is taken will never run out, what is used will never be exhausted, and that everything will go smoothly—is that not far too hard? All of this is brought on by failing to follow the natural Way of heaven and by turning away from the true path of everyday conduct. It is like a thief stealing things, hiding his head but showing his tail; or like a pole set upright, whose shadow can be seen while its substance cannot be grasped. It is a figure for false practitioners who pass off the fake as the real—how could this deserve to be called the "Great Way"!

§3
Original (Chinese)

此介然有知，是忽然而知，不待安排，无事穿凿，鸿鸿濛濛，天地初开之一气，先天元始之祖气是，是即孟子「乍见孺子入井」，皆有怵惕恻隐之一念，吾道云「从无知时，忽然有知」，真良知也。此等良知之动，知之非艰，而措之事为，持之永久，则非易耳。当其动时，眼前即是，转瞬而智诱物化，欲起情生，不知不觉流于后天知识之私，此「顺而施之」，所以可畏也。惟眼有智珠，胸藏慧剑，照破妖魔，斩断情丝，自采药以至还丹，俱是良知发为良能，一路坦平，并无奇怪，此大道所以甚夷也。无奈大道平常，而欲躁进以图功者，往往康庄不由，走入旁蹊小径，反自以为得道，竟至终身不悟，良可慨也夫！朝喻身也。身欲修饬，不至覆灭，必须闲邪存诚，而后人欲始得尽净，天理乃克完全。久久灵光焕发，心田何至荒芜之有？精神团结，仓廪何至空虚之有？不文绣而自荣，匪膏粱而克饱，又何服文彩、厌饮食之有？且慧剑锋锐，身外之利刃无屑；三宝克全，身内之货财不竭。若此者，真能盗天地灵阳之气以为丹者也。胡今之人，不由中庸，日趋邪径，一身垢尘，除不胜除，而且妄作招凶，元阳尽失，于是纷来沓往，并鲜空洞之神，荒芜已极，关窍非尽塞乎？力倦神疲，毫无充盈之象，空乏堪嗟，精气非尽耗乎？徒外观之有耀而文彩是将，徒利剑之锋芒而腰带是尚，亦已末矣，乃犹厌饮食以快珍馐，好货财以期丰裕，何不思学道之巧用机关，盗回元气，固求在内而不在外者也。《易》曰：「作易者，其知盗乎？」正此之谓也。若舍此而他图，支离已甚，敢云大道？

The "little knowledge" (jieran you zhi) here refers to an awareness that wells up suddenly in a single instant—needing no prior arrangement and no strained cleverness, just like the first breath of origin when heaven and earth first opened out of primal chaos, the ancestral Breath of the Prior-Heaven beginning. It is just as Mencius says: when someone suddenly sees a small child about to fall into a well, everyone instantly feels a surge of alarm and compassion. When our alchemical Way speaks of "suddenly knowing out of not-knowing," it means exactly this "true conscience," unmixed with selfish motives.

When this true conscience first stirs, recognizing it is not difficult; what is difficult is to carry it into action and keep hold of it for a long time. In the instant it first appears, the Great Way is right before one's eyes; but in the blink of an eye, outer temptations arrive and cleverness stirs, desires and private feelings rise up at once, and without noticing one slides into the private calculations of the posterior-heaven cognitive spirit—this is why "acting on it by following outer circumstances" is so much to be feared. Only one who has a pearl of wisdom in the eye and a sword of insight hidden in the breast, able to see through illusory demonic hindrances at a glance and cut cleanly through superfluous ties of feeling and desire, will find that from the first gathering of the medicine all the way to the returned elixir, everything flows naturally from true conscience as true ability—level and smooth all the way, without a trace of the bizarre. This is why the Great Way is so very level.

What a pity: the more ordinary the Great Way is, the more those who crave quick results and want to reach heaven in one step refuse to take the broad highway, love to burrow into side doors and byways, smugly believe they have attained the Way, and never wake up even unto death. It is truly lamentable!

Applying the classic to self-cultivation: the "court" is a figure for our body. If the body is to be well ordered and stable, never falling into decline and ruin, one must guard against wayward thoughts and preserve sincerity, clearing out posterior-heaven selfish desires completely; only then can one's Prior-Heaven original nature be whole and intact. Over time the inner numinous light naturally shines forth—how could the field of the heart be overgrown? When Essence, Breath and Spirit gather and hold firm, how could the granaries within the body be empty? Without silk brocade, one's complexion and bearing are naturally warm and lustrous; without rich meats, the inner Breath is full and one is naturally sated and at peace—so what need is there to "wear embroidered robes and be sated with food"? Moreover, with a keen sword of insight in the breast, one has no regard at all for the common steel hanging at the waist; with the Three Treasures of Essence, Breath and Spirit complete in the body, the wealth within is inexhaustible. Only one who can do this is a true master who knows how to steal the numinous yang Breath of heaven and earth to refine the elixir!

Why is it that people today will not walk the true path of the Middle Way, but rush down crooked roads every day? Their whole being is so caked with dust and afflictions that they cannot clear it away, and yet they act recklessly and invite disaster, spending their original yang until none is left. Then stray thoughts fly about in confusion, and not a trace of empty, bright, deep and quiet Spirit remains in the heart; the field of the heart is overgrown to the extreme—are not all the passes and openings of the body blocked? Utterly exhausted and listless, without the least sign of fullness, pitifully empty—has not all their Essence and Breath been spent? To care only for surface glitter by putting on splendid robes, to seek only a dazzling edge by wearing a sharp sword, is already to have fallen to the lowest grade; and yet they still crave rare delicacies and cling to wealth outside themselves! How can they fail to understand that learning the Way is about skillfully seizing the pivot of creation and stealing back the original Breath that has leaked away—something that is sought within oneself, never outside? The Book of Changes says, "Did the makers of the Changes know how to steal?" (the sages who drew the trigrams and developed the Changes surely knew how to steal the life-giving pivot of heaven and earth)—and that is exactly this principle. If one abandons the root and flails about outside, fragmented and scattered like this, how dare one call it the "Great Way"?

§4
Original (Chinese)

他注云，「介然」数句，是倏忽间有一线之明，何尝非知？但验诸实行，每多穷于措施，故云可畏，此明大道之不易也。下一节，言学者不探本原而徒矜粉饰，不求真迹而徒务虚名，是犹立竿见影，得其似，不得其真，故谓之「盗竿」。此讲亦是。古来凡有道者，肌肤润泽，毛发晶莹，等等效验，要皆凡人所共有，然未可以为定论也。又况炼精、炼气，阳光一临，阴霾难固，犹霜雪见日而化，故神火一煅，陈年老病，悉化为疮疡脓血，从大小二便而出，不但初学有之，即至大丹还时，亦有变化三尸六贼，流血流脓，臭不堪闻者。惟有心安意定，于道理上信得过，于经典中参得真，足矣。须知遏欲存诚，去浊留清，层层皆有阴气消除，阳气潜长，学道人不可不知。以外之事，莫说身体光荣，行步爽快，不可执以为凭，即飞空走电，出鬼没神，霎时千变，俄顷万里，亦不可信以为道。盖奇奇怪怪，异端邪教，剑客游侠之类，皆能炼之，未可以为真。若认外饰为真，必惑奇途，造成异类，可惜一生精力，竟入左道旁门，欲出世而涉于三途六道，不亦大可痛哉！太上此章大意，教人从良知体认，方无差误。无奈今之学道者，只求容颜细腻，身体康强，岂知外役心劳，而良田荒芜，宝仓空旷，先天之精气，为所伤者多矣。后天虽具，又何益乎？果然三宝团聚，外貌自然有光。彼驰之于外，而矜言衣食者，何若求之于内而先裕货财也。内财既足，外财自赅，岂同为盗者，不盗天地灵阳之气，而徒盗圣人修炼之名也哉？

Other commentators explain that the lines on "a little knowledge" mean that a person suddenly gains a thread of clear awakening—how is that not "knowledge"? It is only that when one tries to put it into daily practice, one often does not know where to begin and comes up short; hence "only acting on it is to be feared," which shows that walking the Great Way is not easy. The next passage, they say, criticizes students of the Way who do not probe the root but only show off a surface veneer, who do not seek genuine attainment but only covet an empty name—like setting up a pole and chasing its shadow, they learn a superficial semblance without ever receiving the true transmission; hence it is called the "robber chief" (daogan, read here as "robbing the pole"). This explanation is also quite sound.

Since ancient times, people who possess the Way have often had lustrous skin and glossy hair, but such outward signs can also be found in ordinary people who take good care of themselves, and must never be taken as a firm standard for judging attainment of the Way. Moreover, according to the experience of the ancients in refining Essence and Breath, once yang Breath rises, the turbid yin within the body cannot hold its ground, just as frost and snow naturally melt when they meet the sun; so it used to be said that once the spirit-fire refines the body, long-standing hidden ailments are transformed into turbid matter and expelled. (Note: if anyone today experiences bleeding, pus, ulcers or similar symptoms, modern medical diagnosis and treatment must come first; never mistake them for signs of progress in practice.) In traditional practice, the key is a settled heart and a steady intention, truly understanding the principles and truly penetrating the classics—that is enough. Know that in restraining selfish desire, preserving sincerity, and removing the turbid while keeping the clear, every layer of the work involves a process of turbid yin receding and yang Breath quietly growing; those who study the Way must not fail to know this.

As for all the other outward appearances, not only must a radiant complexion and a light step not be taken as real proof—even the legendary marvels of flying through the air and riding lightning, appearing and vanishing like ghosts and spirits, a thousand transformations in an instant, ten thousand miles in a moment, must absolutely never be believed to be the "Way"! For all sorts of strange and bizarre arts can be practiced to some effect by heretical sects, cults and wandering swordsmen and street magicians alike; none of it can be taken as genuine. If one takes such showy outer trappings for the true Way, one will surely be lost on the side roads of novelty-seeking and turn oneself into some nondescript oddity—wasting a whole lifetime's strength only to fall into the crooked doors and side gates. Having hoped to transcend the world, one sinks ever deeper instead. Is that not deeply painful?

The main purpose of this chapter of Lord Lao is to teach people to recognize the Way in their own true conscience, so that they will not go astray. Alas, students of the Way today fix their eyes only on outward signs such as a smooth complexion and a strong body, not knowing that by toiling and rushing about outside every day, the field of their heart has long since grown over with weeds, the treasury within the body has long since been emptied, and their Prior-Heaven Essence and Breath have been gravely harmed! What good, then, can posterior-heaven eating, drinking and tonics really do? If only the Three Treasures of Essence, Breath and Spirit are truly gathered within, the outward appearance will naturally have its radiance. How can those who chase outward all day and boast of their food and clothing compare with those who work inward and first fill the treasury within the body? When true wealth within is sufficient, outward radiance follows of itself, like a channel formed where water flows. Both involve the word "steal"—so why not steal the endlessly life-giving numinous yang Breath of heaven and earth, rather than merely stealing the empty name of the sages' cultivation?

Key Terms

  • "A little knowledge" (jieran you zhi): Huang Yuanji reads jieran as a single moment of clarity that suddenly appears, equivalent to the Prior-Heaven true conscience in Mencius' "suddenly seeing a child about to fall into a well," and opposed to the selfish calculation of the posterior-heaven cognitive spirit.
  • Three Treasures: The Essence, Breath and Spirit of the human body, which this chapter likens to the inexhaustible "granaries" and "goods and wealth" within the body.
  • Stealing the pivot (daoji): From the Book of Changes, "Did the makers of the Changes know how to steal?", and the Yinfu jing, "Heaven and earth are the thieves of the myriad things; the myriad things are the thieves of humankind; humankind is the thief of the myriad things." In internal alchemy it denotes the practical wisdom of following natural rhythms and inwardly gathering the One Breath of Prior Heaven—not plundering external things.
  • Robber chief / robber's boast (daogan / daokua): Daokua is the reading of the received editions, meaning brigands flaunting luxury; daogan means the ringleader of brigands (or, in Huang Yuanji's figure, a pole set up to cast a shadow, yielding only an empty image without substance).
  • Restraining desire and preserving sincerity (e yu cun cheng): Curbing superfluous posterior-heaven selfish desires and deluded thoughts, and guarding the sincere, clear original heart within—the basic work of refining the self that Huang Yuanji stresses again and again.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Treasure that first clean, intuitive thought. Very often a thought that is upright, kind and clear-headed arises, and two seconds later it is diluted by calculations of interest and worries about face. "Only acting on it is to be feared" reminds us that holding on to one's original intention is harder—and more valuable—than learning elaborate techniques.
  2. Be wary of "shortcuts" and the allure of the extraordinary. Any "secret method" that promises quick results, claims mysterious special powers, or bypasses honest living and a steady daily routine is, nine times out of ten, one of the side gates of "people love byways." A truly reliable path of self-cultivation is always plain, holds up over time, and stands the test of ordinary daily life.
  3. Don't let outer polish cover an inner hollowing-out. Piling up consumer goods, flaunting titles, or chasing only a good-looking complexion and labels while running down your sleep and fraying your nerves is exactly "the fields are overgrown and the granaries empty" while "wearing embroidered robes, with goods and wealth to spare." Put your energy back first into the "inner wealth" of rest, diet, emotional balance and steadiness of mind.
  4. Look at bodily changes rationally. Do not think yourself superior because of a few pleasant bodily sensations, and above all never reinterpret clear pathological signals such as bleeding, swelling or pain as "detoxing" or "dissolving yin." Seek medical attention promptly for any physical abnormality.

Translator's Notes

  1. Robber chief / robber's boast (daogan / daokua): The Heshang Gong and Wang Bi editions mostly read 盗夸 "robber's boast"; the Han Feizi ("Explaining Laozi") reads 盗竽 "robber's pipe" (the yu pipe leads the ensemble, hence a figure for the ringleader of evildoers); later printings also have 盗竿. Huang Yuanji's base text follows 盗竿 in the classic and notes in his own gloss that other editions read 盗夸.
  2. "The Way of the noble person begins in husband and wife": From the Book of Rites, "Doctrine of the Mean": "The Way of the noble person begins in the relation of husband and wife; at its utmost, it is manifest throughout heaven and earth." The later phrases "completing oneself is humaneness, completing others is wisdom" and "hence whatever is done at any time is fitting" also adapt wording from the Doctrine of the Mean.
  3. "Suddenly seeing a child about to fall into a well": From Mencius ("Gongsun Chou I"), where Mencius uses this to show that all people have a heart that cannot bear the suffering of others (the heart of compassion), requiring no outside search or calculation.
  4. "Did the makers of the Changes know how to steal?": From the Book of Changes, "Appended Statements I": "Carrying a burden while riding in a carriage invites robbers… Did the makers of the Changes know about robbers?" The original concerns guarding against robbers; later alchemists often adapted it to speak of "stealing the pivot of heaven and earth."
  5. Three Corpses and Six Thieves: Figurative terms shared by Daoism and Buddhism. The Three Corpses are three baneful tendencies lurking in the body that lure one into craving and aging; the Six Thieves are the deluded activity of the six sense faculties—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind—chasing outer objects and robbing one of one's true nature.
  6. Textual note: In the original printing, 日 in 太上日 and 《易》日 has been corrected to 曰; 未世 → 末世; 朝延 → 朝廷; 鸿鸿濛濠 → 鸿鸿濛濛; 持之水久 → 永久; 匪臂粱 → 膏粱; 关窍非尽塞手 → 乎; 過欲存诚 → 遏欲存诚; 飞空走第 → 飞空走电 (following Chapter 51); 篓时千变 → 霎时千变; and 未可以为具 → 真.
