Chapter 63 · Never Striving to Be Great

Tackle difficult things where they are easy and build great things from the minute; a careful distinction between ordinary lead and mercury and true lead and mercury reveals the right road of elixir cultivation—"move the Spirit, not the breath"

About This Chapter

Chapter 63 is one of the most down-to-earth chapters of the Daodejing: "The difficult things of the world must be done while they are easy; the great things of the world must be done while they are small." Whether in work or in cultivating the Way, many people fix their eyes on a grand goal from the start and want to reach heaven in a single step; the bigger their ambition and the more hurried their pace, the more likely they are to give up halfway.

Huang Yuanji explains this chapter with great thoroughness, in two main parts:

  1. The wisdom of cultivating the heart step by step: It is like washing a garment caked with old grime—you cannot scrub it with brute force, for scrubbing too hard not only fails to get it clean but also tears the fabric. Cultivating the Way to clear away accumulated habits is the same: you must patiently rinse them out bit by bit, starting from the easiest and smallest everyday matters. If you never make promises lightly, never treat tasks carelessly, and always remain reverent and cautious, then in the end things go along like a boat pushed downstream—"in the end there is no difficulty."
  2. Clearly distinguishing "true lead and true mercury" from "ordinary lead and ordinary mercury": In this chapter Huang offers a rare, thorough dissection of alchemical terminology. He states clearly that the heart-fluid and kidney-breath originally present in the body are only "ordinary lead and ordinary mercury." Only when the heart-spirit within Li gently descends into the palace of Kan, steaming out the true yang of the Cinnabar Field (true lead) the way charcoal warms a cold pot, and then the Original Spirit lightly presides over and guides it so that it rises to the Muddy Pellet, becomes clear dew, and descends back to the central palace (true mercury)—only when water and fire circulate in this way, stripping away all yin breath, is this the genuine, correct work of the returned elixir. The final phrase, "move the Spirit, not the breath," is a priceless master key for avoiding pitfalls.

Safety note: The latter part of this chapter describes in detail the fire phasing of internal alchemy—the conjunction of Kan and Li, the stirring breath in the Yinqiao, and true breath rising to the Muddy Pellet. These are physiological shifts that occur naturally only after long practice of purity and stillness. In everyday practice, never forcibly hold your breath, fix your mind on the perineum, or forcibly drive the breath up through the channels to the crown of the head; Huang explicitly warns, "move the Spirit, not the breath." Simply keep the heart quiet and natural.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：为无为，事无事，味无味。大小多少，报怨以德。图难于其易，为大于其细。天下难事必作于易，天下大事必作于细，是以圣人终不为大，故能成其大。夫轻诺必寡信，多易必多难，是以圣人犹难之，故终无难。

Laozi says: Act in the spirit of non-action, handle affairs with a heart free of affairs, and savor in a state of plain flavorlessness. Regard the great as small and the many as few, and repay resentment with virtue. Plan the difficult starting from where it is easy; accomplish the great starting from where it is small. The difficult things of the world invariably develop step by step from what is easy; the great things of the world invariably accumulate bit by bit from what is small. Therefore the sage never, from beginning to end, seeks grandeur or glory, and precisely because of this is able to achieve true greatness.

One who makes promises lightly will surely be short on trustworthiness; one who regards everything as too easy will surely meet many difficulties later on. Therefore the sage always approaches matters with reverence, treating them as difficult and handling them with care, and as a result never falls into real hardship from beginning to end.

§2
Original (Chinese)

道本中庸，人人可学，各各可成，只因物蔽气拘，不力剪除，安能洞见本来面目？如浣衣然，既为尘垢久污，非一蹴能去，必须慢慢洗涤，轻轻拔除，始能整敝为新。若用力太猛，不惟无以去尘，且有破衣之患。修士欲洞彻本原，又可不循序渐进哉？始而勉强操持，无容卤莽之力，久则从容中道，自见本来之天。功至炼虚合道，为无为也；顺应自然，事无事也；平淡无奇，何味之有？既无其味，何厌之有？他如大往小来，裒多益少，以至报复者不以怨而以德，此皆极奇尽变，备致因应之常，然而称物平施，无厚薄也，以德报怨，无异情也。且德为人所共有之良，以德报之，即以自然清净之神施之。因物付物，以人治人，即以大小多少投报，亦皆动与天随，头头是道，处处无差，而于己无乖，于人无忤焉。噫！此道之至难而至易，至大而至细者也。无如世之修士，计近功，期速效，往往好为其难，喜务其大，不知图难于易，为大于细，鲜有不蹶者。夫易为难之基，故天下难事必作于易，细为大之本，故天下大事必作于细，况道为万事万物之根，可不由易而难、自细而大乎？不然，进之锐者，退必速矣，又安望几于神化之域哉？是以古之圣人，知道有由阶，学有由进，不思远大之图，惟期切近之旨，淘汰渣滓，涵养本源，如水之浸灌草木，自然日变月化，不见其长而日长，所以自微之著，由粗之精，从有为有事中，而至于无为无事，愈澹愈浓，弥近弥远，而至于美大之诣。圣人终不为大，故能成其大也。今之学者，初起下手，便望成仙，心愈大，事愈难，竟至半途而废者多矣。惟有坚固耐烦，矢以恒久不息之心，庶几易者易而难者亦易，细者细而大者亦细耳。愿学者，图难于易，为大于细，出以持重老成，不至躁暴浅率得矣。不然，非但斯道之大，务以敦厚居心，始克有得，即此一应诺间，轻于唯者必寡信，后悔弥深；一进取内，好为易者每多难，退缩在即，其事有必然者。故圣人修炼之始，虽从易从细以为基，而惟日孜孜其难其慎，此心终未已也，所以先为其难，而其后顺水推舟，行所无事，故曰「终无难」焉。

The Great Way is by nature level, upright, and ordinary—anyone can learn it and everyone can accomplish it. It is only because we are clouded by external things and confined by a narrow, lopsided temperament that we cannot see; if we do not put in the effort to clear away these coverings little by little, how can we see our original face? It is like washing clothes: when a garment has been stained with dust and grime for a long time, it cannot be washed clean all at once. You must soak it slowly and wash it slowly, gently rubbing away the stains, before the old garment can look new again. If you are impatient and scrub too hard, you not only fail to remove the grime but may even tear the garment. If practitioners wish to awaken thoroughly to the source of life, how can they do otherwise than proceed step by step?

At the start, one relies on steadying oneself bit by bit as one works, leaving no room for the slightest rashness or brute force; after long effort, one naturally accords with the Way at ease and sees one's original heavenly nature. When cultivation reaches the stage of "refining emptiness and uniting with the Way," one does everything yet leaves no trace of deliberate action—this is "acting in non-action." Everything follows nature, and no entanglement or attachment arises in the heart—this is "handling affairs free of affairs." Life is plain and unremarkable; what strong, stimulating flavor could there be? And precisely because there is no rich flavor to crave, weariness never arises—this is "savoring the flavorless."

As for turning the great into the small and the small into the great, reducing what is excessive to supplement what is lacking, and even, when others offend us, responding not with resentful retaliation but with generous virtue—these may seem surprising and endlessly varied, but in fact they are nothing more than responding in accordance with the inherent pattern of things. Giving fairly according to the actual measure of each thing, without favoritism; repaying resentment with virtue, without any admixture of personal feeling. Moreover, "virtue" is the innate goodness that all people share; to repay others with virtue is to treat them with one's own natural, pure mind-spirit. Handling things according to their own nature and treating others according to ordinary human feeling, in every exchange, great or small, many or few, each movement accords with heavenly principle, everything is the Great Way, and there is not the slightest error—no inner knot in oneself, and no friction with others.

Ah! This is where the Great Way is at once hardest and easiest, grandest and most minute! Unfortunately, practitioners in the world are always calculating petty immediate gains and longing to see quick results; they like to show off by taking on the hard and to pursue grandeur, not understanding the principle of "planning the difficult where it is easy and doing the great where it is small," and so few of them avoid stumbling. Understand that the easy is the foundation of the difficult, so the difficult things of the world must be done from where they are easy; the small is the root of the great, so the great things of the world must be accumulated from where they are small. And since the Great Way is the root of all affairs and all things, how could it not proceed from easy to difficult and from small to great? Otherwise, the more fiercely one charges ahead, the faster one is sure to retreat—how could one then hope to enter the realm of spiritual transformation?

Therefore the sages of antiquity understood that cultivating the Way has steps to follow and learning has an order of progress. They did not daydream about lofty, far-fetched blueprints but simply made solid the most immediate practice before them, rinsing away the dregs in the heart bit by bit and nurturing the source of life—just as clear water slowly soaks and irrigates plants, accumulating day by day and month by month, changing quietly; you do not see them growing, yet they grow every day. Thus practice becomes manifest from the hidden and subtle, refined from the coarse, and passes naturally from the earnest practice of "action and affairs" to the free and easy realm of "non-action and no affairs"; the plainer the flavor, the richer it becomes; the closer one's footing, the farther one travels, until one reaches the attainment of beauty and greatness. Throughout their lives the sages never proclaim that they are doing great things, and precisely because of this they accomplish truly great things.

By contrast, today's students want to become immortals the moment they begin; the greater their ambition, the harder things become, and very many end up giving up halfway. Only by summoning the steadiness to stay calm and the patience to bear tedium, and by vowing an aspiration that endures without ceasing, will easy things remain easy and difficult things gradually become easy too; small things will remain small, and great things, broken down, will also become small and manageable. I hope all who study the Way will "plan the difficult where it is easy and do the great where it is small," adopting a steady, mature attitude rather than being restless and shallow.

Otherwise, it is not only a great matter like cultivating the Way that requires a sincere, solid heart in order to gain anything; even in everyday life, when agreeing to something offhandedly, those who glibly say "Sure!" will mostly fail to deliver and lose others' trust, regretting it bitterly afterward. And in the course of working and striving, those who always think "This is too simple" will often run into walls everywhere once they actually start, and will soon beat a retreat—this is an inevitable result. Therefore, from the very first day of cultivation, although the sage lays the foundation from the easiest and smallest places, in the heart they remain diligent and cautious every day, never daring to be careless. Precisely because they think through the difficulties in advance and prepare thoroughly, later on everything goes along naturally like a boat pushed downstream, without the slightest strain—and so Laozi says, "in the end there is no difficulty."

§3
Original (Chinese)

此「为无为」三句，是纯任自然工夫。以下「图难于易」一节，是欲造精深，必由浅近之意。至于丹道言铅言汞，究是何物？不妨明辨之。要知此个物事，不外阴阳两端。以汞配铅，即如以女配男，交媾之后，化生元气出来，又将元气合阴气入中宫，然后成丹。在先天，离是纯阳之乾，坎是纯阴之坤，因气机一动，乾之中爻走入坤宫，坤之中爻走入乾窍，乾遂虚而为离，坤遂实而为坎，故乾虽阳而有阴，坤虽阴而有阳，即非先天纯阴、纯阳，太极浑沦之旧，然犹不失其正也。久之，神则生精，气则化血，而气质之性、气数之命，从此出矣，盖以有思虑知觉之心、气血形体之身，不似乾坤原物。至人以法追摄，离中一点己汞，汞为心液，液虽属阴，却从离火中出，带有火性，下入坎宫，熏坎宫一点阴血，血为坎水，水虽属阳，却从坎水中生，实为寒体，古人谓「火入水乡，神入气里」，犹冰凝之遇火，如炭火之热釜，自然温暖，生出阴蹻一脉动气来。虽然火入水中，犹釜底加炭，热气熏蒸，蓬勃上腾，即真铅生也。自此以神运之，而上升泥丸（主宰之而已），犹烤酒甑中，热气被火而升于天锅，则成露珠，滴入瓮中，此即吾教曰「真汞」，又曰「忙将北海初潮水，灌济东山老树根」，其实气化为液而已。复行归炉温养，液又化气，循环不已，一升一降，直将气血之躯，阴气剥尽，凡身化为金身，浊体变为乾体，仍还我太极虚无，不生不灭之法身焉。昔朱元育云：「对坎离言，身中离精坎气，皆属凡铅。直到坎离交媾，真阴真阳会合，生出一点真阳出来，才算先天真铅种子。」然未得明师口诀，纵使勉强把持，也只可以固色身，到得下元充壮，久必倾泄矣。学人得此阳生，只算一边工夫，安望结胎成圣？惟将此阳气，引之上升，复合周身之阴精，更与泥丸绛宫之神髓灵液，交合为一，此正谓：「东家女（木汞也），西舍郎（金铅也），夫妻配合入洞房。黄婆劝饮醍醐酒，每日熏蒸醉一场。」此乾坤交而结丹，前只是坎离交而产药。有此真铅、真汞一合，才可还丹，铅即水中所生之金，汞即火中所生之木。前只算凡铅、凡汞，到此才算真铅、真汞。学人照此用工，运神不运气，庶不至误事焉。

The three phrases at the beginning of the classic text, beginning with "act in non-action," describe the work of non-action that relies purely on naturalness; the following section, "plan the difficult where it is easy," means that to reach profound attainment one must begin from what is shallow and near. While we are at it, what exactly are the "lead" and "mercury" (see Lead and Mercury) that alchemy talks about every day? It is worth clarifying them thoroughly here.

Understand that these two things, lead and mercury, are when it comes down to it nothing other than the two poles of yin and yang. Pairing mercury with lead is like a man and woman marrying and uniting; after they merge, prior-heaven original breath is generated, and this original breath is then fused with the yin breath of the body and gathered into the central palace, and only then is the Golden Elixir formed.

In terms of the evolution from Prior Heaven to Posterior Heaven: in the original prior-heaven state, the position of Li was originally the pure-yang Qian trigram, and the position of Kan was originally the pure-yin Kun trigram (see Qian and Kun). Later, when the dynamic of breath stirred, the yang line in the middle of Qian fell into the palace of Kun, and the yin line in the middle of Kun rose into the opening of Qian; thus Qian became empty in the middle and turned into the Li trigram (the heart), and Kun became full in the middle and turned into the Kan trigram (the kidneys). From then on, yin was hidden within Qian's yang and yang within Kun's yin; although this is no longer the old state of prior-heaven pure yin and pure yang, the Supreme Ultimate in undivided wholeness, at this stage the original upright breath is still not lost. As time went on, Spirit leaked downward and transformed into tangible essence, and breath stagnated and transformed into posterior-heaven blood; and from this arose the lopsided nature of temperament and the limited destiny of allotted fate—because by now there was a posterior-heaven heart that thinks and discriminates and a posterior-heaven body of flesh and bone, no longer the original face of Qian and Kun.

The accomplished person uses the method of cultivation to retrieve and gather it back: the single point of "own mercury" in the Li trigram (the heart)—that is, the true spirit and true fluid that arise out of Li's fire, yin fluid on the surface but carrying a warm, fiery nature within—is made to sink down into the palace of Kan (the kidney storehouse), there to warm and steam the single point of originally cold yin blood, the water of Kan, within Kan's palace. The ancients called this "fire entering the land of water, Spirit entering into breath." It is like hard ice meeting a warm fire, or like adding charcoal under a pot to heat the water: the Cinnabar Field naturally becomes pleasantly warm, and lively stirring breath arises below in the "Yinqiao" channel. When the warmth of the fire keeps nurturing beneath the water, it is like continually adding charcoal under the stove: the heat steams upward, rising vigorously—and this vigorous true yang breath, just born, is what is called the arising of "true lead"!

From here on, use the Original Spirit to guide it gently (note: only quietly presiding over and watching it, never forcing or straining), letting this true breath rise naturally to the Muddy Pellet at the crown of the head. This is just like a still for making liquor: the heat below is driven up by the fire to the cooling pot on top, where, meeting the cold, it condenses into clear, sweet dewdrops that fall drop by drop back into the jar. This clear dew that falls is what our Daoist school calls "true mercury"; an old formula vividly calls it "hastily take the first tide-water of the Northern Sea to irrigate the roots of the old tree on the Eastern Mountain"—in plain terms, it is simply true breath rising and transforming into fluid. Next it descends back to the elixir furnace to be nurtured, and the fluid in turn transforms into true breath. Thus with one ascent and one descent, circulating without cease, one strips away completely the posterior-heaven yin and turbid breath in the body of flesh and blood; the mortal body is exchanged for a golden body, the heavy turbid body turns back into the pure-yang body of Qian, and one returns once more to the original Supreme Ultimate, empty and void, the unborn and undying spontaneous Dharma Body.

The Ming-dynasty alchemist Zhu Yuanyu once said: "Speaking solely of the two organs Kan and Li, the essence of Li and the breath of Kan originally present in the body are only 'ordinary lead.' Only when the conjunction of Kan and Li takes place, and true yin and true yang genuinely meet, generating that single point of prior-heaven true yang, does one have the true seed of prior-heaven true lead." However, without the oral instructions of an enlightened teacher, merely forcing oneself to hold on and guard the lower Cinnabar Field can at most temporarily secure the physical body; once the breath and blood of the lower origin grow full and strong, over time they will inevitably overflow and leak away. When a practitioner first obtains this arising of yang, they have done only half the work—how could they expect this already to form the Holy Embryo and realize sagehood?

One must let this newly born true yang breath be guided naturally upward, then merge it with the yin essence of the whole body, and further unite it as one with the spirit-marrow and numinous fluid of the Muddy Pellet at the crown and the Crimson Palace in the chest. This is exactly what the old song says: "The girl of the eastern house (Wood-mercury), the young man of the western lodge (Metal-lead), husband and wife unite and enter the bridal chamber. The Yellow Matron (True Intention) urges them to drink the wine of ghee; every day they are steamed and get drunk once more." Only at this point is it called "the conjunction of Qian and Kun" that forms the elixir; the earlier step was only "the conjunction of Kan and Li" that produces the Medicine. Only when true lead and true mercury have united does one have the genuine returned elixir.

To sum up in one sentence: true lead is the True Metal born within the water of Kan; true mercury is the True Wood born within the fire of Li. Before conjunction and transformation, they can only be counted as ordinary lead and ordinary mercury; only at this step are they true lead and true mercury. Let students of the Way apply themselves according to this principle, and remember well: "move the Spirit, not the breath"—simply concentrate the Spirit in purity and preside naturally, and never deliberately push the physical breath around; only then will you avoid going astray and spoiling the work.

Key Terms

  • Lead and Mercury: Here Huang strictly distinguishes "ordinary lead and mercury" (the heart-fluid and kidney-breath originally present in the body) from "true lead and mercury" (the prior-heaven true yang generated after the conjunction of Kan and Li, and the numinous fluid that is sublimated and descends).
  • Qian and Kun and conjunction of Kan and Li: Prior-heaven pure yang and pure yin are Qian and Kun; falling into posterior heaven, they exchange their middle lines and become Kan and Li. Alchemy first relies on the intercourse of Kan and Li to produce the medicine, then returns to the pure bodies of Qian and Kun to form the elixir.
  • Muddy Pellet and Crimson Palace: The Muddy Pellet is the upper Cinnabar Field at the crown of the head, and the Crimson Palace the middle Cinnabar Field below the heart; they are where true breath rises and transforms into fluid and where spirit-marrow and numinous fluid merge.
  • Spontaneous Dharma Body: The original true body, empty and void as the Supreme Ultimate, unborn and undying, restored once the posterior-heaven yin and turbid breath have been stripped away.
  • Yinqiao: In traditional internal alchemy, a key point at the bottom of the lower Cinnabar Field, around the perineum (Perineum); it also belongs to the Eight Extraordinary Vessels, and is often where true yang first stirs.
  • Yellow Matron: The alchemists' personified figure for True Intention; intention belongs to Earth, and Earth's color is yellow. Like a matchmaker, she brings lead and mercury (Metal and Wood, water and fire) together to merge.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Change habits the way you wash an old garment. Bad habits and an impatient temper built up over many years cannot be thoroughly corrected by a single day of fierce resolve; using too much force tends to bring relapse or giving up. Correcting a little each day, gently and steadily, is what truly makes you new.
  2. Break big goals down into the most unremarkable small steps. Whether in study, work, or quiet sitting and self-cultivation, do not fixate on "great accomplishment" from the start; do the smallest step in front of you solidly each day—"never striving to be great, one is able to achieve greatness."
  3. Promise less, and leave more room. "Promises made lightly inspire little trust; treating things as too easy brings much difficulty." Weigh things before agreeing to others, and think through the difficulties a little more before acting; a bit more caution early on makes the way easier and smoother later.
  4. In quiet sitting, remember "move the Spirit, not the breath." Do not puff out your belly, hold your breath, or use your mind to force the breath around in circles inside the body; simply bring the mind-spirit back and let it rest quietly, and the breath will settle evenly of itself as the heart grows still.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Reducing the many to supplement the few" (pou duo yi shao): The original woodblock edition reads 衰多益少. Based on the Book of Changes, "Qian" (Modesty) hexagram—"reduce the many to supplement the few, weigh things and distribute evenly"—pou (裒, to reduce, to gather) was miswritten as shuai (衰) because of their similar forms; it has been corrected here.
  2. "Those who glibly say yes will surely be short on trust" (qing yu wei zhe bi gua xin): The original woodblock edition reads 轻于惟者. Since 唯 (wěi, to agree offhandedly) and 惟 are similar in form, it has been corrected here.
  3. "Hastily take the first tide-water of the Northern Sea to irrigate the roots of the old tree on the Eastern Mountain": An oral formula transmitted in Daoist alchemical classics. The Northern Sea stands for the kidneys (Kan water), the Eastern Mountain for liver-Wood and the heart-spirit; it describes the image of true breath rising and transforming into cool, sweet dew that moistens body and mind.
  4. Zhu Yuanyu: A famous internal alchemist of the late Ming and early Qing, styled the Daoist of Yunyang, author of Elucidating the Hidden in the Cantong qi and Elucidating the Hidden in the Awakening to Reality. He advocated the alchemical method of purity and stillness and had a profound influence on the Daoist community of the Qing dynasty.
  5. "The girl of the eastern house, the young man of the western lodge…": An allegorical verse transmitted among alchemists since the Song and Yuan dynasties. The girl of the eastern house stands for Wood-mercury (the heart-spirit), the young man of the western lodge for Metal-lead (the true yang in the kidneys), the Yellow Matron for True Intention in the central palace, and the wine of ghee for the sweet-dew numinous fluid generated when Spirit and breath merge.
