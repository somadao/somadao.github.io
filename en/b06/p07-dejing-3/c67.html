Chapter 67 · I Have Three Treasures

The Great Way is supremely vast yet arises from the supremely minute; only by constantly holding the three treasures—compassion, frugality, and not daring to be first in the world—taking benevolent compassion as substance, keeping to essentials as method, and humble harmony as function, can one refine the elixir and preserve the true

About This Chapter

Chapter 67 sets forth the warmest and most moving teaching of the Daodejing, the "three treasures"—the first is compassion, the second is frugality, the third is not daring to be first in the world. In Huang Yuanji's eyes, these three treasures are by no means mere moralizing; they are the master outline of elixir cultivation in which Confucianism and Daoism meet:

  1. The Way is vast and resembles nothing; its true source lies in the supremely minute (§2–§3): The Great Way is so vast that no concrete form can compare with it, yet its source is hidden precisely in the subtlest place of the human heart—like the bit of unwillingness King Xuan of Qi felt on seeing an ox trembling, or the bit of compassion a passer-by feels on seeing a child about to fall into a well. This bit of natural compassion is the root and sprout of the Golden Elixir.
  2. The three treasures interlock link by link (§2–§3): "Compassion" is the substance of benevolent love; only with true compassion is there the "great courage" to shoulder all things. "Frugality" is restraint and keeping to essentials—gathering the mind into the Square Inch and the scattered Breath back into the lower Cinnabar Field; the more refined and concise, the further it can extend ("breadth"). "Not daring to be first in the world" is humble harmony and gentle compliance, which prevents an agitated, violent temperament from stirring up fire and injuring the elixir, so that one can grow into a great vessel. Conversely, abandoning compassion to seek courage inevitably becomes cruelty, abandoning frugality to seek breadth inevitably breeds greed, and abandoning the rear to contend for the front inevitably invites a deadly calamity.
  3. The alchemical metaphor of "certain to win in battle, certain to hold firm in defence" (§3): A compassionate, gentle heart is not only "without enemies" in the human world as the benevolent are; placed in elixir refining, it is right both in advancing and in withdrawing. When approaching the furnace and advancing the fire, it can burn away stray thoughts and selfish desires ("certain to win in battle"); when guarding the city during ablution, it can steadily protect the Holy Embryo and the Original Spirit ("certain to hold firm in defence").

Safety note: §3 of this chapter uses alchemical metaphors such as "approaching the furnace and advancing the fire," "guarding the city and ablution," and "stirring up fire and injuring the elixir" to remind practitioners to beware of an agitated, violent temperament and of rash, impatient advance. In quiet sitting and self-cultivation, if you forcibly "gather the breath" or "advance the fire" with a competitive, eager-for-results attitude, you can easily provoke rising liver-fire, irritability and insomnia, or a sense of oppression in the chest. Gentleness, restraint, and freedom from haste are themselves the best safety rail.

Original (Chinese)

太上曰：天下皆谓我大，似不肖。夫惟大，故似不肖；若肖，久矣其细也夫。我有三宝，持而宝之：一曰慈，二曰俭，三曰不敢为天下先。夫慈故能勇，俭故能广，不敢为天下先，故能成器长。今舍慈且勇，舍俭且广，舍其后且先，死矣。夫慈，以战则胜，以守则固。天将救之，以慈卫之。

Lord Lao says: Everyone in the world says that the "Way" I speak of is exceedingly vast—so vast that it seems to resemble no concrete thing at all. It is precisely because it is vast and boundless that it seems to resemble nothing; if it truly resembled some concrete thing in the world, it would long ago have become petty and commonplace!

I have three heirloom treasures, which I always hold firmly and cherish: the first is called "compassion," the second "frugality and keeping to essentials," and the third "not daring to push ahead of the people of the world." Precisely because there is compassion, true great courage can arise; precisely because one can be frugal and restrained, one can extend to vast reaches; precisely because one does not dare to push forward and put oneself first, one can become a great vessel and the model for all.

People today, however, do the opposite: they abandon compassion and flaunt brute courage, abandon frugality and greedily seek breadth, abandon staying behind and contend for the front everywhere—all of this is seeking one's own death! Know that this treasure, "compassion," if used in battle will surely win, and if used in defence will surely hold firm. When Heaven is about to save someone, it endows him with this compassion and guards him with it.

夫道，本无极而太极者也，无大无细，非大非细，即大即细，固有言思拟议所不能罄者。若强以大名之，则「浩然之气，至大至刚，充塞乎天地之间」是。如欲以细状之，则「无名之璞，至隐至微，藏于太空之际」是。其在人也，得之则生，失之则死。要皆自无而有，由微而著，盖以微者其原，而大者其委。与其言大以明道，不如言细以显道也。所以太上曰：「天下皆谓我大。」夫「我」，即道也。道本无方无体，今以大称，是道有方体可拟，似不相肖。夫惟大莫名其大，故不肖人之所谓大。若欲形天之道，肖我之身，自开天以至于今，体天立极，阐道明教之圣人久矣乎，皆以无极之极，不神之神，至细至微，而为道也。顾道如此无声无臭，恍惚杳冥，学者又从何下手哉？太上曰：我有三宝，持而守之，拳拳弗失，宝而珍之，念念不忘，则可返本还原，以复维皇之诞降。三宝者何？一曰慈，慈即仁也。仁慈蔼蔼，为天下元，君子体仁，足以长人，且统乎四端，兼乎万善，仁在其中，即道在其中，充之至极，可以包罗天地，贯注古今，此为金丹之本，修士所宜珍念也。顾其道及乎至大，其几起于至微，若不知万念俱忘，一灵内照，徒务广而荒，求博而泛，于仁无得，于道无有焉。惟反求诸己，笃于寸心，欲立立人，欲达达人，守约施博，古所谓「得其一，万事毕」，非此俭欤？夫俭为求仁之方，修道之要，学者既知其慈，尤当养以俭，始可与道同归。虽然，使自高自大，不有谦和之度，则在内只知一己，在外渺视诸人，自诩聪明，矜言智慧，居然以先知先觉自命，往往视天下人无有能处己先者，究之性不恬静，气不和平，而欲丹成九转，道极九天也，难矣。古云：「修丹要诀，以灵觉为道之体，冲和为道之用」，庶在在处处，不敢为天下先也。且夫慈也者，人心之良能也。尽一己之心，以立万物之命，誓愿何其宏也？养寸衷之性，以求万物之安，精力何其壮也？是守慈之人，即养勇之人。曾子谓子襄曰：「自反而不缩，虽褐宽博，吾不惴焉；自反而缩，虽千万人，吾往矣。」非一片仁慈，毫无私屈者，能有如此之大勇乎？必所守者约，而后所施者博，是非约无以为博也。惟能慎举动，省思虑，致一心于方寸，收百体于丹田，绵绵密密，不贰不息，继继绳绳，无急无荒，自然修其身而天下平，非俭何由广乎？至若不敢为天下先，正「谦尊而光，安贞之吉」。其能柔顺乎天下，而天下莫与之争，即能顺承乎天道，而天道默与以成。非有冲和之德，不敢为天下先，焉能大器晚成如是乎？是知慈也、俭也、后也，皆求道之本始也；勇也、广也、先也，皆奉道之末效也。今之学者不然，舍慈且勇，必生忍心；舍俭且广，必怀贪念；舍后且先，必有争竞，皆取死之道。即或幸存，亦行尸走肉，滥厕人群，其与死又何异哉？总之，慈为人之生理，性所同然，惟能守之以约，出之以和，则慈惠恻怛，自出真诚，天下未有不心折而屈伏者。惠足使人，仁者无敌，焉有何战之不胜、守之不固，而贻羞于天下耶？《书》曰：「惟天阴骘下民，相协厥居。」俾之以生以遂，永享无事之天，所谓「天将救之」者此也。《诗》曰：「维天之命，於穆不已。」足见清空一气，流行不息，发育无疆，夫亦曰「以慈卫之」而已矣。

The Great Way is originally "the Ultimateless that is also the Supreme Ultimate." It can be called neither great nor small; it is neither great nor small, and at the same time it is both great and small. It is certainly not something that ordinary words and thought can exhaust. If one forces the name "great" upon it, then it is what Mencius called "the flood-like Breath, supremely great and supremely firm, filling all the space between heaven and earth." If one forces the description "small" upon it, then it is "the nameless uncarved block, supremely hidden and supremely subtle, concealed within the boundless void." As it applies to human beings: those who obtain it are full of life, and those who lose it head toward death. In the end, it always goes from nothing to something, from the subtle to the manifest—the "subtle and minute" is its source, and the "vast" is only its outflow once it has unfolded. Therefore, rather than speaking broadly of "greatness" to explain the Way, it is better to point out the Way at the "subtle and minute."

That is why Lord Lao says: "Everyone in the world says I am great." This "I" refers to the Great Way itself. The Way originally has no fixed location or form; now that people describe it as "great," it is as if the Way really had some big bulk that could be compared—which on the contrary makes it seem unlike. Precisely because it is so great that no name can frame it, it does not at all resemble the "greatness" in people's eyes. If one really wishes to embody the Way of Heaven and realize it in one's own person, then all the sages, from the opening of heaven and earth until today, who embodied the Way of Heaven, established the human ultimate, and expounded the Great Way, understood this long ago—they all entered into the Way from the ultimate of the Ultimateless, the true spirit that displays no marvels, the most subtle and hidden place!

Yet since the Great Way is so soundless and scentless, dim and obscure, where exactly should a student of the Way begin? Lord Lao immediately gives a clear handhold: I have three treasures; hold them firmly in both hands without losing them, cherish them in your heart without forgetting them for a single moment, and act accordingly—then you can return to the root and restore the origin, recovering the pure and good nature that Heaven bestowed on human beings.

What are the three treasures? The first is called "compassion," and compassion is what the Confucians call "benevolence" (ren). This kindly, benevolent vital impulse is the source of all goodness under heaven; the noble person who embodies this benevolent virtue is fit to lead others. It governs the four sprouts—commiseration, shame, deference, and the sense of right and wrong—and encompasses all goodness under heaven. Where benevolence is, there the Way is; nourish it to the utmost, and it can embrace heaven and earth and run through past and present. This is precisely the root of the Golden Elixir, which practitioners of the Way should most cherish and keep in mind.

However, though the Way of benevolence can unfold to the most vast extent, its first pivot lies in the most minute place. If you do not know how to set down the myriad stray thoughts and turn a single point of spiritual clarity back to shine inward, but only busy yourself seeking breadth and abundance outside, the result will surely be sprawling and neglectful: you will attain neither benevolence nor the Way. Only by turning back to search within your own heart, working hard on the sincerity of the square inch, helping others stand as you wish to stand and helping others succeed as you wish to succeed, and holding to the simplest, most essential root, can you unfold vast and broad functions. The ancients said, "Attain the One, and the ten thousand affairs are done"—is this not the second treasure, "frugality" (restraint and keeping to essentials)? "Frugality" is precisely the method of seeking benevolence and the key to cultivating the Way. Having understood "compassion," the student of the Way must all the more nourish it with "frugality"; only then can he return together with the Great Way.

Even so, if one is self-important and puffed up, lacking a humble and harmonious bearing—seeing only oneself within, despising others without, constantly boasting of one's cleverness and wisdom, posing as one who knows first and awakens first, always feeling that no one in the world is fit to walk ahead of oneself—then in the end one's nature will be restless and uneasy and one's breath not peaceful, and to hope to refine the ninefold Golden Elixir and ascend the Way to the ninth heaven will be very hard indeed! An old formula says: "The key secret of elixir cultivation is to take clear, spiritual awareness as the substance of the Way, and harmonious, humble gentleness as the wondrous function of the Way." Only thus can one, at all times and in all places, "not dare to be first in the world."

To go a step further, this "compassion" is the innate good capacity of the human heart. To vow to use one's whole heart to safeguard the lives of all things—how vast is such a vow! To nourish the heavenly nature in one's own square inch of heart in order to seek the peace of all things—how mighty is such spirit and vigour! So the one who truly keeps "compassion" is at the same time the one who cultivates true "courage." Long ago Zengzi said to Zixiang the famous words that Mencius quotes: "If, on examining myself, I find I am not upright, then even facing a common man in coarse, loose clothing, would I not feel afraid? If, on examining myself, I find I am upright and in accord with righteousness, then even if thousands and tens of thousands stand in my way, I will go forward!" Were it not for a breast full of open, upright compassion, without a trace of selfish grievance, how could such earth-shaking great courage arise?

By the same principle, one must first keep to the refined and essential, and only afterward can one extend broadly—without "the essential" there can never be true "breadth." Only by being cautious in every movement, reducing wild thoughts, gathering the heart and settling it within the Square Inch, drawing the scattered Breath of the hundred members back into the lower Cinnabar Field—continuous and close-knit, single-minded and unceasing, linked on without break, neither hasty nor neglectful—can one naturally extend step by step from cultivating oneself to bringing peace to all under heaven. Without "frugality," how could "breadth" ever be achieved?

As for "not daring to be first in the world," it accords exactly with the principle of the Book of Changes: "Humility, being honoured, shines forth," and "the good fortune of resting in steadfastness." If you can stay gentle, compliant and humble toward the people of the world, no one in the world will contend with you; if you can comply with and receive the Way of Heaven, the Way of Heaven will naturally help you succeed in the unseen. Without this rich virtue of harmonious, humble retreat, without daring to stay behind others willingly, how could one "mature late as a great vessel" and become the chief of all vessels?

From this we see that the three—"compassion," "frugality," and "staying behind"—are all the fundamental starting points of seeking the Way; while the three—"courage," "breadth," and "becoming chief of vessels"—are merely the fruits that naturally ripen after one serves the Way. Many learners in the world today turn root and branch upside down: discarding compassion to flaunt bravado, they will surely give birth to a cruel and vicious heart; discarding restraint to greedily seek breadth, they will surely harbour endless greed; discarding yielding to rush to the front, they will surely sink into endless strife—all of these are roads to self-destruction! Even if by luck they still draw breath, they are no more than walking corpses mingling in the crowd—how are they any different from the dead?

In sum, compassion is the fundamental principle of heaven by which human beings live and keep on living, and it is common to every person's nature. If only one can nourish it with "keeping to essentials" and express it with "humble harmony," then that compassion and kindness will come wholly from sincerity, and there will be no one in the world who is not won over and transformed by you. Kindness is enough to win people's hearts, and the benevolent have no enemies in the world—what battle could you then fail to win, what city fail to hold firm, and how could you bring shame upon yourself before the world? The Book of Documents says: "Heaven silently settles and protects the people below, harmonizing them in their dwellings." To let all living beings be born, nourished and prosper, enjoying forever peaceful days without trouble—this is what the scripture means by "When Heaven is about to save someone." The Classic of Poetry says: "The mandate of Heaven—how profound and unceasing!" This shows that the clear, empty, boundless primordial Breath of the universe flows without rest and nurtures without limit; in the final analysis, it comes down to nothing more than the words "it guards him with compassion"!

Original (Chinese)

道曰大道，其实无极而太极也。然非从无极之始，混混沌沌中，觉出津涯，又安知太极之根，能测其起止乎？学者须先明道原，于不睹不闻之中，寻出至隐至微之体，即所谓「虚而灵」者是。顾其细已甚，曰黍珠一粒，又若有可象者。总之，无形之形，无状之状，迎之不见其首，随之不见其后，即人心中蔼然一片仁慈是也。虽至颓至劣之夫，亦不泯仁慈之性。孔子曰：「我欲仁，斯仁至矣。」修丹岂有他哉？不过守此仁慈而已。何谓仁慈？如齐王见牛之觳觫而不忍，乡人见孺子之坠井而恻然，此皆仁心发端，天心来复。由此思之，此个动机动念，无时不有，第恐人不及觉耳。学者从天真发动处，扩充行去，自然炼丹有基。但不可务博而荒，只须守约而微，一心皈命，五体投诚，古云：「心要在腔子里，念不出总持门。」由此愈约愈博，愈微愈彰，其约之弥精者，其拓之愈广也。学者可不以俭为本乎？虽然，俭德为怀，固以约鲜失之良法，苟不出以谦和，又恐躁暴之性，起火伤丹，故守约尤须致和，在在自卑自小，不居人先，始为虚己下人，仁心常存，道气常存矣。若不尚慈而尚勇，不务俭而务广，不居后而居先，如此则心是凡心，气是凡气，人身虽存，天性已灭，其不死亡者，未之有也，安望我有三宝持而不失乎？且人有仁慈，尤足得人之欢心，以之出战战必胜，以之守城城必固，此即喻临炉进火，烧退六贼三尸，守城沐浴，则保固胎婴元神。是柔和之心，为炼丹养道之要。况天之生人，予之以生，无不予以仁慈。能克念归仁，长生永命之丹，即在是矣。

Though the Great Way is called "the Great Way," it is in fact the process of passing from the Ultimateless to the Supreme Ultimate. But unless, in the earliest, undifferentiated stillness of the Ultimateless, you personally perceive that first faint shore and edge, how could you recognize the sprout from which the Supreme Ultimate emerges and grasp the rhythm of its beginning and end? Therefore the practitioner must first clearly recognize the source of the Great Way, and in utmost stillness, where nothing is seen and nothing heard, find again that supremely hidden and subtle substance—that is, the point of spiritual clarity the ancients called "empty yet numinous." It is subtle to the extreme; the alchemists reluctantly liken it to "a single millet pearl," as if there were some trace to follow after all. To put it plainly, that form without form, that shape without shape, that true mechanism whose head cannot be seen when you meet it and whose back cannot be seen when you follow it—in every person's heart, it is that kindly, warm, gentle compassionate heart!

Even the most degenerate and base person never completely extinguishes this point of compassionate nature in his bones. Confucius said: "If I truly desire benevolence, benevolence arrives at once." What lofty, unreachable trick is there to refining the elixir? It is nothing more than guarding this compassionate nature. What is compassion? It is as the Mencius describes: King Xuan of Qi saw an ox, about to be led off to consecrate a bell with its blood, trembling all over with fear, and on the spot felt unable to bear it; a passer-by suddenly sees a little child about to fall into a well and, without willing it, feels a pang in his heart and is moved to commiseration. None of these is calculated; they are the natural first stirrings of the benevolent heart, the return of the Heaven-given heart of the Way.

Considering it along these lines, such innocent stirrings are in fact present in us at every moment; the only fear is that people are usually too restless to notice them in time. If the practitioner can follow the very place where this point of innocence has just begun to stir, and expand and nourish it, the foundation of elixir refining will naturally be firmly laid.

However, when beginning, one must never be greedy for grand achievements and pursue breadth, only to fall into neglect; one need only keep to "refined, essential, subtle, minute"—settling the whole heart on the root of inner nature and life and sincerely gathering in the hundred members of the body. The ancients put it well: "The heart should stay inside the chest (not run about outside); thoughts should not cross the threshold of the gate of total retention." Cultivating like this, the more refined and essential the restraint, the broader the later unfolding; the subtler the discernment, the more brightly the original heart shines forth. The purer one's keeping to essentials, the wider one's vision opens. How could the practitioner not take "frugality" as the foundation of his life?

But one more safeguard must be added. Cherishing the virtue of frugality is indeed an excellent method, as in "those who err through restraint are few"; yet if it is not accompanied by a humble, gentle bearing, there is the fear that the hasty, violent habits within will flare up and, in quiet practice, forcibly "stir up fire and injure the elixir"! Therefore, while keeping to essentials, one must above all "achieve harmony"—in every matter and every place, making oneself low and small and not pushing ahead of others. Only by emptying oneself and placing oneself below others can the benevolent heart long endure and the Breath of the Way long abide.

Conversely, if one esteems brute courage rather than compassion, covets breadth rather than keeping to essentials, and contends for the front everywhere rather than willingly staying behind, then what moves is the ordinary heart and what is used is ordinary breath; though the body is still alive, the heavenly nature has long since withered and died—how could one not head toward ruin? And how could one still hope that "I have three treasures and hold them without losing them"?

Moreover, a person who holds a compassionate heart most easily wins the goodwill of all; use it to go to battle and you will surely win, use it to guard a city and it will surely hold firm. Brought into the actual practice of the alchemical Way, these two sentences make a superb metaphor. "Certain to win in battle" refers to approaching the furnace and advancing the fire: when upright Breath is abundant, it naturally burns away the deluded thoughts brought on by the six thieves of eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind, and the turbid breaths of the three corpses within the body. "Certain to hold firm in defence" refers to the stage of warm nourishment, when the fire is stopped and the city guarded, and during peaceful ablution one can firmly safeguard the Holy Embryo and the Original Spirit so that they are not startled and scattered.

Thus this warm, soft, compassionate heart is the foremost secret of refining the elixir and nourishing the Way. Moreover, when Heaven gave birth to all things and bestowed life on human beings, it never failed to bestow this compassionate nature on us at the same time. If only you can restrain stray thoughts and return to your original heart of compassion, the Golden Elixir of long life and enduring destiny is in fact right here, right now!

Key Terms

  • Three treasures (compassion, frugality, not daring to be first in the world): Laozi's three master principles for conducting oneself, governing the world, and cultivating the elixir. Huang Yuanji interprets them as "compassion is benevolence (the substance of the Way); frugality is keeping to essentials and gathering the heart (the method of cultivation); not daring to be first in the world is harmonious, humble gentleness (the function of fire phasing)."
  • A single millet pearl: A common inner-alchemy image for the point of prior-heaven spiritual vitality and the true seed of the Golden Elixir, supremely hidden and subtle in utmost stillness, round, bright and condensed like a grain of millet.
  • Square Inch and lower Cinnabar Field: The Square Inch is the heart and breast, the spiritual terrace where the heart-spirit dwells; the lower Cinnabar Field is the Cavity of Breath below the navel, where the original Breath gathers. "Gathering the heart into the Square Inch and drawing the hundred members into the Cinnabar Field" is the frugal work of collecting the mind and condensing the original Breath.
  • Approaching the furnace and advancing the fire; guarding the city and ablution: Metaphors for stages of alchemical fire phasing. "Advancing the fire" means raising upright Breath to sweep away stray thoughts and deluded passions (the six thieves and three corpses); "ablution" means that, when the fire has reached the right point, one temporarily rests from advancing and withdrawing and holds on with a calm, gentle heart, so as to protect and nourish the Holy Embryo and the Original Spirit.
  • Six thieves and three corpses: Terms used in traditional Daoism and the alchemical classics as metaphors for the selfish desires and deluded thoughts of the human heart. The "six thieves" are the six roots—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind—which chase their objects, give rise to desires, and rob one of one's own Essence, Breath and Spirit; the "three corpses" traditionally refers to the turbid breaths and hidden yin impulses in the body that entice one to greed. Huang Yuanji adapts both at the level of cultivating the heart and refining the nature.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Take "being unable to bear it" as your daily starting point for cultivating the Way. There is no need to seek some mysterious Way far away: the flicker of commiseration when you see others suffer, the gentle thought that stops you from hurting someone before you speak—these are the most ready-made "return of the heart of Heaven." Protect it, follow it, do one small thing with it, and you are already nourishing the elixir.
  2. Trade "simplicity" for true "breadth." Energy scattered across ten anxieties accomplishes nothing; bring your attention back to the one most important thing right now and to your own heart (the heart inside the chest). Once your focus is gathered, your horizons will open naturally.
  3. Beware of "competitiveness in spiritual practice." Many people study Buddhism or the Way, train and discipline themselves, while secretly comparing their progress with others, looking down on people, even struggling against themselves and flaring up in anger—this is exactly what Huang Yuanji warns of as "a violent temper stirring up fire and injuring the elixir." Practise gentleness first; talk of diligent progress after.
  4. Distinguish true courage from bravado. Bravado comes from fear of losing face and pushes back on a surge of hostility; true courage comes from a clear conscience, from protecting and taking responsibility ("if I am upright, even against thousands and tens of thousands, I will go forward"). The latter grows steadier the further it goes; the former grows more brittle the harder it charges.

Translator's Notes

  1. "The flood-like Breath, supremely great and supremely firm, filling all the space between heaven and earth": From Mencius, "Gongsun Chou I." The later passage, "Zengzi said to Zixiang: If, on examining myself, I am not upright… even against thousands and tens of thousands, I will go forward," comes from the same chapter; 缩 means straight, in accord with righteousness; 褐宽博 refers to a commoner in coarse, loose clothing; 惴 means to fear.
  2. "Help others stand as you wish to stand, help others succeed as you wish to succeed" and "If I desire benevolence, benevolence arrives at once": From the Analects, "Yong Ye" and "Shu Er" respectively.
  3. "Humility, being honoured, shines forth" and "the good fortune of resting in steadfastness": From the Book of Changes, the Judgment commentary on the hexagram Qian (Humility), "Humility, being honoured, shines forth; being lowly, it cannot be overstepped," and the Judgment commentary on the hexagram Kun, "The good fortune of resting in steadfastness corresponds to the boundlessness of earth."
  4. "Heaven silently settles and protects the people below, harmonizing them in their dwellings": From the Book of Documents, "Great Plan" (Hongfan), meaning that Heaven silently settles the people below and harmonizes their dwellings and livelihoods. 阴骘 is read yīn zhì and means silent protection and settling.
  5. "King Xuan of Qi saw the ox trembling and could not bear it"; "the villager saw a child about to fall into a well and was moved": Adapted respectively from Mencius, "King Hui of Liang I" (King Xuan of Qi, seeing the ox trembling as though an innocent man being led to execution, had it replaced with a sheep), and "Gongsun Chou I" (anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and commiseration). 觳觫 is read hú sù and describes trembling with fear.
  6. "The heart should stay inside the chest; thoughts should not cross the gate of total retention": "The heart should stay inside the chest" is a famous saying of Song Neo-Confucianism (see the Classified Sayings of Master Zhu and elsewhere), meaning that the mind should be gathered within its proper bounds and not run outward. "Total retention" (zongchi) is the Buddhist dhāraṇī, used here for the gate of the mind that holds together all good thoughts and gives rise to no stray delusions.
  7. Textual note: The base edition of the classic text contained the stray page number "340）," which has been deleted. In the classic and the original commentary, "太上曰," "一曰," "二曰," "三曰," "《书》曰," "《诗》曰," "道曰大道," "夫亦曰," and "曰黍珠一粒" were often misprinted with 日; 一己 and 反求诸己 were misprinted as 已; 笃于寸心 was misprinted in the base edition as 笃于于心 (emended to 寸心 on the basis of the four-character parallel phrasing and the later 寸衷 and 方寸); 性不恬静 was misprinted as 性不悟静; 末效 as 未效; 争竞 as 争竟; 黍珠 as 泰珠; 孺子 as 懦子; the 微 of 守约而微 and 愈微愈彰 as 徽; 苟不出以谦和 as 苛不出; a superfluous single quotation mark "'" after 躁暴 has been deleted; 况天之生人 was misprinted as 沈天之生人; and 胎婴 has been standardized in simplified characters. All have been corrected one by one.
