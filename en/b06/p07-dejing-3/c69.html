Chapter 69 · The One Who Grieves Prevails

The strategist would rather be the guest than the host, wary of the enemy and never advancing lightly; so too the elixir method must quietly wait for the prior-heaven Breath of Kan to stir of itself, treating the cognitive spirit as guest, and above all avoid advancing the fire rashly, which instead sets the body ablaze with deviant fire

About This Chapter

On the surface, Chapter 69 is entirely about the maxims of ancient strategists—"I dare not be the host but rather the guest; I dare not advance an inch but rather retreat a foot," "No calamity is greater than underestimating the enemy," "When opposing armies meet, the one who grieves prevails." Huang Yuanji first expounds to the full the strategists' principle of caution in war and love for the people, and then states outright the chapter's great inner-alchemical secret of host and guest:

  1. Distinguishing host and guest: the prior-heaven heart of the Way is host, the posterior-heaven human heart is guest (§2–§3): The greatest error people make in cultivating the Way is to let the posterior cognitive spirit overstep its authority and act as "host," using selfish intention to forcibly direct the rising and falling of the breath. The true elixir method must willingly take the "guest's seat," quietly waiting for the true yang within Kan in the lower Cinnabar Field (the original Breath of Prior Heaven) to stir of itself; only then does the spirit-fire join it along the momentum. Advancing the fire presumptuously ahead of time will surely bring on "deviant fire burning the body."
  2. Better to advance steadily step by step than to "advance an inch and retreat a foot" (§2–§3): Whether in warfare or in cultivation, the greatest fear is one day of sun followed by ten days of cold—fishing for three days and drying the nets for two. You progress only an inch, then slacken and fall back a foot; gaining little and losing much, you will never succeed in a lifetime.
  3. Compassionate grief, wariness of the enemy as if standing at the edge of an abyss (§2–§3): Underestimating the enemy inevitably means losing one's treasure and one's life. Only by constantly maintaining a reverent caution as if treading on thin ice, together with a heartfelt compassion and grief that cannot bear killing, expressed with gentleness, compliance and peace, can one remain undefeated in the battles of life and of cultivating the mind.

Safety note: §3 of this chapter warns explicitly: "If one does not quietly wait for the Breath of lead to stir, but carelessly raises and lowers, advances and withdraws the spirit-fire, circulating it round and round, there is none who does not set the body ablaze with deviant fire and suffer great distress and humiliation"; it also says that rashly advancing the fire will surely cause "the fire to rise and injure the elixir, the furnace to be wrecked and the cauldron ruined." Remember never to use subjective imagination to forcibly guide the breath to "rise and fall around the Celestial Circuit" in the body when body and mind are not yet still and there is no foundation of natural bodily sensation; otherwise it is very easy to bring on deviations such as a swollen head and rising heat, chest tightness and shortness of breath, insomnia and palpitations. In quiet nourishment, what is valued is "acting where there is nothing to be done"—simply follow what is natural.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：用兵有言：「吾不敢为主而为客，不敢进寸而退尺。」是谓行无行，攘无臂，仍无敌，执无兵。祸莫大于轻敌，轻敌则几丧吾宝。故抗兵相加，哀者胜矣。

Lord Lao says: Among the strategists of old there is a saying: "I would rather be the 'guest' who responds to battle after the other has moved than dare to be the 'host' who moves first to provoke it; I would rather cautiously retreat and hold a foot than dare to rashly advance an inch." (In his commentary Huang Yuanji reads it differently: better to hold the inch one has gained than ever dare to advance an inch only to retreat a foot.)

Truly reaching this realm of caution is called: seeming to march in formation, yet showing no formation seething with killing spirit; seeming to roll up one's sleeves and strike, yet showing no menacing arm; seeming to meet the enemy face to face, yet showing no hostility that seeks to fight; seeming to grip weapons, yet as though no weapon had been taken up. Of all calamities under heaven, none is greater than underestimating the enemy; once you underestimate the enemy, you will very nearly lose your most precious treasure of compassion entirely. Therefore, when two evenly matched armies cross blades, the side that bears a heart of sorrowful compassion, unable to bear the slaughter of living beings, will surely win the victory!

§2
Original (Chinese)

古人用兵，著为战策，其有言曰：「吾不敢为主而为客。」主犹君也，君主出令，得专其政；客犹臣也，臣主奉令，一听之君，所谓「饶他为主我为宾」是。是以吾为主，即以后天人心作主，而先天道心反退听焉。吾岂敢以后天人心为主，而先天道心反退听于后天人心也哉？其必以先天道心为主，而以后天人心为客，在在依之以为命也可。「不敢进寸而退尺」者，盖谓战胜而进，即一寸也宜固守之。如败而退，即跬步也不可让之。若进有寸功而退以尺计，是得少失多，难成易败，在用兵，为不才之将，在修道，为无功之人，吾岂敢哉？亦惟让彼为主，逊我为宾，则彼有可乘之机，我无可抵之隙，所谓「制人而不为人所制」，庶无挫辱之虞矣。见可而进，知难而退。其进也，必鼓其迈往之神；其退也，不予以可攻之势。如此小心，其难其慎，无非凡事让人以先，而己独处于后焉。故其行军也，若人能行而己似不能行者然。及其挺身而往，攘臂而前，又若人有臂而己无臂者然。迨至对垒交锋，两军相仍而战，又若人能敌而我无能敌者然。虽伐鼓渊渊，振旅阗阗，彼有所执，我岂独无兵者哉？然而善用兵者，有如涉春冰、履虎尾，一似人有兵而己无兵者焉。如此进不轻进，退不轻退，诚知社稷存亡、国家成败，系于一战，敌其可轻视乎哉？试观古来慎敌者，往往成功，轻敌者，常常败绩，如管子之伐山戎，子玉之战城濮，可见矣。况朝廷之兴衰，视将帅之得失，如不临事审慎，逞己才，恃己智，而谓人莫己若，似孟明之超乘以过，高固之出贾余勇，未有不败国亡家、覆宗灭祀者。圣人之大宝曰信，轻敌者必丧人君之信。惟两敌相抗，两兵相加，而自弱自柔，至慈至惠，常以杀伐之气，有干天地之和为忧，不以兵革之威，得辟土地之利为乐，有时用兵疆场，亦出于万不得已，虽未哭泣徇师，而仁慈恻怛之心、哀痛迫切之情，早已流露于陈师鞠旅之间，而三军共沐其生成，万姓咸相为感激也。所以君子有不战，战必胜矣，非哀痛之心，有以及人身而入人心也哉？

When the ancients used troops, they wrote down treatises on strategy, and among them is a maxim: "I dare not be the 'host' who moves first, but willingly become the 'guest' who responds." What is host and what is guest? The "host" is like the sovereign, who issues commands and holds the reins of government; the "guest" is like the minister, who only carries out orders and obeys the sovereign in everything—that is, what Zhang Boduan's Awakening to Reality calls "Let him be host, and I the guest."

If in every matter this selfish intention called "I" is made host, that is the same as putting the human heart of Posterior Heaven on the throne while chasing the heart of the Way of Prior Heaven off to the side to take orders! How could I dare let the posterior human heart be host while the prior-heaven heart of the Way, on the contrary, takes orders from the posterior human heart? One must always honour the prior-heaven heart of the Way as host and let the posterior human heart willingly withdraw to the guest's seat, relying at every moment and in every place on the prior-heaven heart of the Way to settle one's life and destiny.

Next, look at "I dare not advance an inch but rather retreat a foot." This means that when you win a battle and advance, even if you have pushed forward only an inch, you should hold it firmly; and if you meet a setback and must yield, you should not lightly give up even half a step. If, when advancing, you gain only an inch of small merit, yet on retreating lose a whole foot, that is gaining little and losing much, hard to accomplish and easy to ruin—in warfare it makes a worthless, mediocre general; in cultivating the Way it makes a person who labours in vain and achieves nothing. How could I dare do that?

So simply yield the "host's" position and modestly withdraw to the "guest's" seat: when the other side moves first rashly, it exposes an opening, while I hold steady and reveal no crack. This is what the art of war calls "controlling others without being controlled by them," and naturally one will suffer no setback or humiliation. Advance steadily only when you see the time is ripe; withdraw decisively when you know the situation is difficult. When advancing, be sure to summon your full vigour and spirit to press boldly forward; when withdrawing, never leave the other side an opening to attack. Being so careful and cautious upon cautious comes down to nothing more than being willing in every matter to let others go first and staying behind oneself.

Precisely because he is so modest, careful and restrained to the bone, when he leads the troops on the march it looks to outsiders as if everyone else marches with great fanfare while he alone is as quiet as though he could not walk at all ("marching without marching"). When he steps forward and swings his arm to meet the enemy, it seems as if everyone else is baring teeth and claws while he alone is so composed that he has not even shown his arm ("rolling up the sleeve without an arm"). When the two armies actually face each other and engage head-on, it seems as if everyone else is fierce and able to fight while he alone shows no killing spirit or desire to contend ("meeting without an enemy"). Although the war drums thunder to the clouds and the marshalled army is vast in its momentum, and the enemy holds weapons in hand—does my side really have no weapons? Yet the great general who is truly good at using troops is inwardly always as alert and wary as one walking on the thin ice of spring that is about to melt, or one who has carelessly stepped on a tiger's tail; so he looks as if others have weapons while he has none at all ("grasping without weapons").

He advances without rushing forward and retreats without retreating lightly in this way because he knows perfectly well that the survival or ruin of the state's altars of soil and grain, the success or failure of the empire's foundations, all hang on this one battle—how could the enemy be lightly underestimated? Open the history books and look: since antiquity those who were cautious toward the enemy often achieved great merit, and those who were arrogant and underestimated the enemy often suffered utter defeat. Look at how Guan Zhong, cautious and thorough, campaigned north against the Shanrong and won complete victory; then look at how the Chu general Ziyu, stubborn and contemptuous of the enemy, suffered a crushing defeat at the Battle of Chengpu and took his own life—how vivid the lessons are!

Moreover, the rise and fall of a court depend entirely on the successes and failures of its commanders. If one cannot be cautious when facing battle, but only shows off one's bit of talent, relies on one's bit of cleverness, and thinks no one in the world can match oneself—like the Qin general Mengming Shi, whose soldiers, passing the north gate of the Zhou royal city, frivolously showed off by leaping onto their chariots, only for the whole army to be wiped out at Xiao; or like the Qi grandee Gao Gu, who at the Battle of An showed off his bravery with a single chariot and cried out, "Who wants courage? Buy my surplus courage!", only for the Qi army to suffer a great defeat—who has ever failed to ruin the state and the family and cut off the ancestral temples and the altars?

The great treasure the sage values most is "good faith" (together with the three treasures of compassion and frugality from the previous chapter); one who blindly underestimates the enemy and rushes forward will surely throw away entirely the most precious foundation on which the sovereign stands! Only one who, when two armies are evenly matched and their blades cross, still willingly remains soft and weak, holding the utmost compassion and kindness, constantly worrying that the killing spirit of arms will harm the harmony of heaven and earth, and never taking pleasure in expanding territory by force of arms; who, even when he cannot avoid using force on the battlefield, does so only out of utter necessity—though he has not gone weeping to inspect the camps, his benevolent and compassionate heart, his urgent and deep grief for living beings, has long since naturally shown itself in every movement as he marshals the army and sets out. So the officers and soldiers of the three armies are all deeply bathed in this warmth that cherishes life, and the common people of the world are all moved from the heart to remember and support him. Therefore the truly virtuous noble person either does not fight, or, when compelled to fight, is sure to defeat the enemy! Is this not precisely because that sincerely grieving, compassionate heart both warms the bodies and minds of the soldiers and reaches straight into the hearts of all the world?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此喻真阳发生，气机充壮，方可进火行工。如不静候铅气之动，而漫以神火升降进退，循环运转，未有不邪火焚身、大遭困辱者。当其四候之际，必候坎气之自动，而离不得以专主，故曰「吾不敢为主而为客」。修炼之道，进行则常，退后则灾，如天之运行不息、水之流行不停，始克蒸蒸日上。若时作时辍，一暴十寒，则是进寸而退尺，功少而过多，终身必无成功矣。若此者，由不知归根复命之道，乃日用常行之道，不可以智计取，不可以作为得。惟逆修丹道，顺运自然，学如不学，功而无功，相因而造，顺势而前，无毫沮滞，无一把持，若禹之治水，行所无事而已。倘进火行符，轻于进退，犹行兵者之轻视敌人，未有不火起伤丹，炉残鼎败，以致铅汞一齐飞散者。噫！纯任自然，敬慎不败，固缉熙于光明。若妄作聪明，长生之宝，必因此后天尸贼为之戕害无存，又安望其成丹而可大可久哉？惟仁慈一片，哀痛十分，而复出之以和平，行之以柔顺，自然所向披靡，战无不胜。学人慎毋以后天识神为主，而先天元气皆退听焉，庶几其不差矣。

Brought into the cultivation of inner alchemy, this passage of military strategy speaks an even more iron-clad true transmission: one may advance the fire and carry out the work along the momentum only when the true yang of Prior Heaven in the body has stirred naturally and the Breath-mechanism is full and vigorous! If one is not willing to wait calmly and quietly for the true lead of Prior Heaven (the Breath of true yang) to stir of itself, but instead casually follows subjective thoughts, using the posterior spirit-fire to forcibly raise and lower, advance and withdraw, and contrive a circulation, there is none who does not provoke "deviant fire burning the body" and suffer great hardship, distress and humiliation.

Whenever one reaches the critical juncture of the four periods of the Celestial Circuit's fire phasing, one must wait for the prior-heaven true Breath within the Kan-water of the lower Cinnabar Field to stir naturally; the Li-fire heart-spirit above must never overstep its authority and take matters into its own hands to push and lead by force—this is "I dare not be the host but rather the guest" in the elixir method (in the conjunction of Kan and Li, Kan's Breath is host and Li's spirit is guest).

Next, on perseverance in cultivation: to keep advancing steadily is the constant Way, while losing heart and shrinking back halfway invites calamity. Only like the heavens turning without rest and great rivers flowing without interruption can the work rise higher day by day. If you practise for two days and rest for ten—one day of sun, ten days of cold—that is "advancing an inch and retreating a foot": progressing a little and falling back a great deal, with very little merit and very many faults; in this life you will certainly not succeed.

The reason this happens is entirely that people do not understand: the great Way of returning to the root and restoring destiny is by its nature an ordinary Way melted into daily life. It simply cannot be seized by cleverness or scheming, nor obtained by straining and forcing artificial effort. Only by following the reversal of returning to the root and source in direction, while in method entirely complying with natural rhythms—learning as naturally as though not learning, applying effort as easily as though making no deliberate effort, guiding along the momentum and advancing with it, without the slightest sluggishness or obstruction and without the slightest rigidity or stubbornness—is it like Yu the Great controlling the floods: "guiding along the course of the water and doing nothing that goes against its nature" ("acting where there is nothing to be done"), that is all.

Conversely, if one is careless in the fire phasing of advancing the yang fire and withdrawing the yin tally, advancing and withdrawing rashly, it is exactly like a general who underestimates a strong enemy—there is none in whom deviant fire does not flare up and burn the elixir-embryo, wrecking the furnace and ruining the cauldron, until the Essence, Breath and Spirit (lead and mercury) so laboriously nourished all fly apart together!

Alas, leaving everything purely to nature, reverent and cautious and never acting rashly, could have let the mind-substance shine on unbroken ("continuing in brightness"); but once one becomes opinionated and plays at cleverness, the supreme treasure of long life will surely be ruined and exhausted by the greedy, deluded stray thoughts of Posterior Heaven (the "corpse-thieves")—so how could one hope to form the Golden Elixir and accomplish a vast and enduring work of the Way? Only if one's breast always holds a heart full of compassion and a deep, grieving vigilance toward life and its downfall, and one turns this heart into a daily practice of peace, gentleness and compliance, will one naturally sweep all before one and win every battle on the long march of clearing away deluded thoughts and nourishing the true origin.

Students of the Way must remember one sentence: never let the posterior cognitive spirit usurp the host's place and push the One Breath of Prior Heaven aside to wait on its orders! As long as you hold to this bottom line, your cultivation will most likely not go astray.

Key Terms

  • Host and guest (host and guest in the conjunction of Kan and Li): The strategists' "I dare not be the host but rather the guest" is directly transmuted by Huang Yuanji into the great inner-alchemical secret of host and guest—the prior-heaven heart of the Way and the true yang within Kan in the lower Cinnabar Field are the "host," while the posterior human heart and the Li-spirit, the cognitive spirit, are the "guest." When the Breath has not yet stirred, the spirit does not rashly urge it on; once the Breath has stirred, the spirit joins it along the momentum.
  • Four periods: In traditional inner-alchemical fire phasing, the four key junctures of zi, wu, mao and you (or the four-part rhythm of advancing the yang fire, withdrawing the yin tally, and the mao and you ablutions). The core of all of them lies in following and waiting for the natural stirring of the Breath-mechanism, never pulling up the sprouts to help them grow.
  • Deviant fire burning the body; the furnace wrecked and the cauldron ruined: Warnings of the alchemists about deviations caused by going against natural rhythms and using the posterior cognitive spirit to forcibly fix the intention and urge the breath on; they show up as feverish heat and tension in body and mind, with the Essence and Breath (lead and mercury) dissipated instead.
  • Acting where there is nothing to be done: From the Mencius's comment on Yu the Great's flood control; it means that although inner alchemy is a reversal returning to the root, the actual fire phasing must guide along the momentum, without the slightest strain or contrivance.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Don't let the "anxious brain" issue wild orders on behalf of the "honest body." When you cannot sleep, the more you stare at the clock and order yourself to "fall asleep now," the more wired you become; when sitting quietly, the more you order yourself to "feel the qi now," the more your head swells and your chest tightens. Learn to "be the guest," and hand control back to the body's natural rhythm.
  2. Put an end to "one day of sun, ten days of cold"—better ten minutes every day. "Advancing an inch and retreating a foot" is what ruins people most: practising hard for two days on a whim, then giving up completely for half a month, starting over from scratch each time. Shrink your goal to the smallest dose you can easily complete every day; never breaking the thread is the greatest victory.
  3. Keep a reverence that "does not underestimate the enemy" in everything. When facing the rebound of a bad habit, the final stage of a project, or a smooth patch in life, it is easiest to grow careless. Treat each response as a reminder of "walking on spring ice, treading on a tiger's tail," and you will actually be safest.
  4. True strength carries "being unable to bear it." Whether competing or communicating, if your heart holds goodwill and consideration ("the one who grieves"), then even when you stand firm on principle, the other side can feel your integrity rather than malice—and this is often what best wins lasting trust.

Translator's Notes

  1. "I dare not advance an inch but rather retreat a foot": Modern scholarship mostly reads this sentence as "I dare not rashly advance an inch, but would rather retreat and hold a foot"; in §2 and §3, however, Huang Yuanji approaches it from perseverance in practice and reads it as "better to hold steadily the inch one has gained than ever dare to advance an inch and retreat a foot (gaining little and losing much, one day of sun and ten of cold)." The plain-language rendering keeps both meanings in view, while the commentary follows Huang's reading throughout.
  2. "Guan Zhong's campaign against the Shanrong, Ziyu's battle at Chengpu": Guan Zhong assisted Duke Huan of Qi in the northern campaign against the Shanrong, planning cautiously and achieving success; at the Battle of Chengpu between Jin and Chu in the Spring and Autumn period (632 BCE), the Chu chief minister Ziyu, stubborn and contemptuous of the enemy, suffered a crushing defeat and finally took his own life (see the Zuo Commentary, Duke Xi, 28th year).
  3. "Mengming leaping onto the chariots as they passed; Gao Gu selling his surplus courage": From the Zuo Commentary, Duke Xi, 33rd year (the Qin general Mengming Shi, on his way to attack Zheng, passed the north gate of Zhou, where three hundred Qin chariot crews showed off by leaping onto their chariots; later the whole army was wiped out at Xiao), and Duke Cheng, 2nd year (at the Battle of An between Qi and Jin, the Qi grandee Gao Gu charged into the Jin army with a single chariot and came out crying that he would sell his "surplus courage"; the Qi army was eventually routed), respectively.
  4. "Yu controlling the floods, acting where there is nothing to be done": From Mencius, "Li Lou II": "Yu's guiding of the waters was acting where there was nothing to be done," an image of guiding along the momentum without going against nature.
  5. Textual note: The base edition's chapter title reads "第六十九章 一哀者胜矣," which has been standardized as "第六十九章 · 哀者胜矣." In the classic text, "太上曰" was misprinted with 日, and the colon after "用兵有言" was misprinted as a semicolon "；". In the original commentary, 吾岂敢 was misprinted as 音岂敢; 不予以可攻之势 as 可政; 而我无能敌者然 as 面我无能故者然; 而己无兵 as 而已无兵; 将帅 as 将师; 流露 as 流簬; 非哀痛之心 as 衰痛, with a superfluous comma after it; 漫以神火 appears in the base edition as 慢以神火 (emended to 漫 according to the sense of "rash, reckless action"); 铅汞 was misprinted as 铅永; and 惟仁慈一片 was misprinted in the base edition as 仁感一片 (emended to 仁慈 in light of §2's "benevolent and compassionate heart, urgent grief" and the surrounding context). All have been corrected one by one.
