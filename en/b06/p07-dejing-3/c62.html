Chapter 62 · Prized by All Under Heaven

The Way is the hidden refuge of all things; good people treasure it, and even those who are not good are preserved by it. Refining the elixir does not hinge on old age or depleted essence, but on the union of Original Spirit, original breath, True Breath, and True Intention

About This Chapter

Chapter 62 opens with high praise for the nobility of the Way: it is the deep refuge of all things and the treasure of good people, and even those who are not good, who have gone astray, can preserve themselves by turning back to it. Even one honored as the Son of Heaven or ranked among the Three Dukes—who travels with attendants bearing great jade discs before him and rides in a splendid carriage drawn by four horses—would do better to quiet the heart, sit down, and advance in this Way.

Huang Yuanji develops the chapter on two levels:

  1. Heaven's Way abandons no one, and seeking the Way happens right now: Whether people do good or evil is not, at bottom, a matter of a different origin; it is only that their temperament has become lopsided and material desires have clouded their original heart. As long as one is willing to guide and teach them, even the most wicked can turn their hearts toward the good. Precisely because the Way is complete in everyone from the start, "seek it and you will find it"—there is no need to strain after it outside.
  2. The subtle order of the four elements of cultivating the elixir: Many people give up on themselves once they grow old, feeling that their essence and breath are depleted and they can no longer cultivate the Way. Huang firmly dispels this misconception: what truly brings the Way to completion is not tangible posterior-heaven essence and breath, but the layered cooperation of four core elements—the Original Spirit as the foundation, the original breath as its support, True Breath (embryonic breathing) to bind them together, and, as commander-in-chief, the single point of True Intention that arises spontaneously in utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness. He also gives a special warning: never rashly stir up a "greatly bright, wakeful mind" that deliberately watches and discriminates, for that would only scatter the Spirit and breath one has just gathered.

Safety note: This chapter discusses the work of stillness and concentration involving embryonic breathing, True Intention, and the return of Spirit and breath to the root of life. Beginners must never deliberately hold their breath in imitation of "embryonic breathing," nor should they chase special lights or strange bodily sensations during sitting; simply keep the breathing light, slow, and natural, and the mind settled and unhurried.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：道者，万物之奥，善人之宝，不善人之所保。美言可以市，尊行可以加人。人之不善，何弃之有？故立天子，置三公，虽有拱璧以先驷马，不如坐进此道。古之所以贵此道者，何也？不曰求以得，有罪以免耶？故为天下贵。

Laozi says: The Way is the deepest shelter and refuge of all things; it is the priceless treasure of the good, and also what those who are not good rely on to preserve themselves. Fine words in accord with the Way can win the respect and esteem of the world; noble conduct in accord with the Way can move and surpass the multitude. If a person is not good for a time, how could one so easily cast them away?

Therefore, at the grand ceremonies of enthroning the Son of Heaven and installing the Three Dukes, even if one presents a precious jade disc as large as two arms can hold, preceded by a team of four horses as a lavish gift, it is not as good as quietly sitting down to offer up and practice this Way. Why did the ancients prize this Way so highly? Was it not because whoever sincerely seeks it can obtain it, and whoever has erred can, by turning back through it, escape calamity? For this very reason the Way is the most precious thing under heaven.

§2
Original (Chinese)

夫道者，生于天地之先，混于虚无之内，杳冥恍惚，视不见，听不闻，搏不得，而实万物所倚以为命者也。子思子曰：「君子之道费而隐。」无道无物，无物无道。大周沙界，细入微尘，不可以迹象求，不可以言语尽，诚至无而含至有，至虚而统至实，浩淼无痕，渊深莫测。万物之奥，莫奥于此。善者知此道，为人身所最重，故珍而藏之，炼而宝之，不肯一息偶离。不善者，亦知有道，则身可存而福可至，无道则命难延而祸亦多。保身良策，莫道若也。况本中庸之道，以发为言，则为美言，犹美货之肆于市朝，人人知爱而慕之，且欲抚而有之。本寻常之道，以见诸行，则为尊行，犹王公大人之身价，人人皆敬而礼之，且各尊而上之。若非言可为表，市之反以招辱；若非行可为坊，加之又以致谤。《诗》曰：「天生烝民，有物有则。民之秉彝，好是懿德。」足见善恶虽殊，而其好德之心，则一而已。见有善者，吾当敬之，即有不善者，亦乌可恶之？不过气质之偶偏，物欲之未化，而有戾于道耳，而其源终未有或异也。人能化之导之，即极恶之人，亦可转而之善。甚矣，天地无弃物，圣人无弃人也！如有弃人，是自弃也，岂有道者所忍出哉？天生民，而立之君，即作之师，将以君临天下，而置三公，无非统驭群黎，化导万姓，正一身以正朝廷，正朝廷以正天下，务使万邦协和，而四方风动，天子长保其尊，三公长享其贵而后已。假使不能奉若天道，以与斯民维新，又安有永保天命以享无疆之福乎？虽有拱璧之贵，罗列于前，驷马之良，驰驱于后，亦不能一息安也。又何如日就月将，时时在道，朝乾夕惕，念念不忘，而坐进此道也哉？《楚书》曰：「楚国无以为宝，惟善以为宝。」《尚书》曰：「所宝惟贤，则迩人安。」是道也，自古帝王公卿，所贵重者也。古之所以重此道者何？以道为人人固有之道，求则得之，其势最为捷便。人能奉持此道，则为人间一大丈夫。若违悖此道，则为天地一大罪人，岂但有过而不免入于邪途也耶？子思子曰：「道也者，不可须臾离也。」人其勉之！

The Way was born before heaven and earth, blended undivided within emptiness, the dim and obscure: the eyes cannot see it, the ears cannot hear it, the hand cannot grasp it, and yet it is the very root on which heaven, earth, and all things depend for their life. Zisi says in the Doctrine of the Mean: "The Way of the noble person is vast yet hidden"—that is, the workings of the Great Way are boundlessly broad, while its substance is secret and subtle. Without the Way there would be no things; there is nothing in which the Way is not contained. In its largeness it pervades the whole universe of worlds as numerous as grains of sand; in its smallness it can enter a single mote of dust. It cannot be sought merely through outward traces, nor can it be fully explained in words; truly, within utmost nothingness it contains utmost being, and within utmost emptiness it governs utmost fullness—vast and boundless, leaving no trace, too deep to be fathomed. It is called "the hidden refuge of all things"—the deepest shelter of all things; is there anything in the world more profound than this?

Good people understand that this Way is what matters most in life, so they treasure it in their hearts like a jewel, cultivating and guarding it at every moment and refusing to leave it even for an instant. Even people who are not good actually understand in their hearts that if they accord with the Way, their persons and households will be preserved and blessings will come; if they turn their backs on it, their lives will be hard to keep and calamities will follow one after another. Therefore, there is no better strategy for preserving oneself than following the Way.

What is more, if one speaks from the ordinary, everyday Way of the Mean, what one says will be "fine words"—like exquisite goods displayed in the marketplace, which everyone knows to love and admire and wishes to take home and own. If one acts from the plain, steady Way, what one does will be "noble conduct"—like the lofty prestige of kings, dukes, and great men, whom everyone treats with respect and courtesy and willingly honors with a high place. Conversely, if one's words cannot serve as a model, displaying them will only bring humiliation; if one's conduct cannot set a standard, insisting on placing oneself above others will only invite criticism and slander. The Classic of Poetry says: "Heaven gave birth to the multitudes; where there are things, there are laws. The people hold to their constant nature and love this fine virtue." Heaven brought forth the multitude of people; wherever there are things, there are laws governing them, and the people, holding to their innate constant nature, naturally love beautiful virtue. From this we see that although the world distinguishes between good people and bad, the heart that yearns for virtue deep within everyone is in fact one and the same.

So when we meet good people, we should respect them; and even when we meet people who are not good, how can we simply loathe and reject them? It is only that their inborn temperament happens to be lopsided and their acquired desires have not yet been dissolved, so for a time they have strayed from the Great Way; traced back to the source, their nature is not the least bit different from ours. As long as someone sincerely moves and guides them, even the most wicked can change their minds and turn toward goodness. How profound this is: heaven and earth have never discarded a single thing, and the sage has never abandoned a single person! To abandon others lightly is really to abandon one's own benevolent heart—how could a person of the Way bear to do so?

Heaven gives birth to the people and at the same time establishes rulers and appoints teachers for them, to govern the world, and sets up the Three Dukes to assist—all for the purpose of leading the common folk and educating the people: rectifying oneself so as to rectify the court, and rectifying the court so as to rectify the world, making sure that the ten thousand states live in harmony and the four quarters respond and are transformed. Only then can the Son of Heaven long preserve his honored position and the Three Dukes long enjoy their wealth and rank. If one cannot reverently follow the Way of Heaven and lead the people to renew themselves day by day, how could one keep Heaven's mandate forever and enjoy lasting blessings? Even with precious jade discs arrayed before one and teams of four fine horses following behind, the heart would not find a moment's peace! How could that compare with accumulating day by day and month by month, abiding in the Way at all times, staying alert and diligent from morning to night, never forgetting it for a single thought, and sitting down to practice and offer up this Great Way?

The Book of Chu says: "The state of Chu holds nothing else as its treasure; it holds only good people as its treasure." The Book of Documents also says: "If what one treasures is only the worthy, then the people close at hand will be at peace." Clearly, this Way is precisely what emperors, kings, dukes, and ministers have prized most since ancient times. Why did the ancients value it so highly? Because the Way is complete in every person from the start; whoever is willing to seek it can obtain it right now, more directly and conveniently than anything else. One who upholds the Way is a great person standing tall between heaven and earth; one who turns against the Way is a great offender between heaven and earth—far worse than merely erring and inevitably straying onto a crooked path. Zisi said: "The Way cannot be departed from even for an instant." The Way is something one cannot leave even for the blink of an eye; let everyone strive diligently!

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言道为人生一件大事，无论天子三公，俱宜珍重，虽有拱璧驷马，不如坐进此道之为愈，切勿谓衰迈年华、铅汞缺少、自家推诿可也。要知金丹玉丹，虽借后天精气种，而成仙证圣，此却一毫用不着。古云「太和所谓道」，又曰：「虚无即道。」可见学道人，不悟虚无之理、太和之道，纵使炼精伏气，修入非非，亦与凡夫无别。所以吾道炼丹，必须以元神为主，元气为助神之用，以真呼吸为炼丹之资。若无元神，则无丹本；若无元气，则无丹助，是犹胎有婴儿，不得父精母血之交媾，亦是虚而无著。既得元神、元气，不得身正胎息，则神气不能团凝一处，合并为一，以返于太素之初。吾更转一语曰：夫人修炼，既得元神、元气，又有真息运用，使之攒五簇四，合三归一，然非真意为之主帅，必然纷纷驰逐，断无有自家会合而成丹也。虽然，真意又何自始哉？必从虚极静笃，无知无觉时，忽焉气机偶触而动，始有知觉之性，此即真意之意，非等凡心凡性也。故古云：「仙非他，只此一元真性，修之而成者。」然不得水中之金、精中之气，以为资助，则元性亦虚悬无着，不免流于顽空。既知金生，不得真息调摄，又安能采取烹炼而成丹？然则真息为炼丹之要具，而真意尤为真息之主宰。学道人，未得神气合一，安能静定？苟得神气归命，必要酝酿深厚，而后全丹始得成就。切不可起大明觉心，直使金木间隔，坎离不交也。吾借此以明道奥。后之学者，有得于中，尚其宝之慎之！

Applied to cultivation, this chapter tells us that cultivating the Way is the most important matter in life; whether you are the Son of Heaven or one of the Three Dukes, you should cherish it all the more. Even possessing riches such as jade discs and four-horse carriages is not as precious as sitting quietly to cultivate the Way. Never use the excuse that you are old and frail, or that your essence and breath—your Lead and Mercury—are insufficient, to find reasons for putting off cultivation!

Understand this: although refining the Golden Elixir and the Jade Elixir requires borrowing posterior-heaven essence and breath as a starter at the outset, when it comes to the lofty attainment of becoming an immortal and realizing sagehood, that bit of flesh-and-blood essence and breath is of no use whatsoever. The ancients said, "Supreme Harmony is what is called the Way," and also, "Emptiness is the Way." Thus, if those who study the Way do not truly awaken to the principle of "emptiness" and the realm of "Supreme Harmony," then even if they painstakingly refine their essence and forcibly subdue their breath, even reaching the deepest, subtlest states of absorption (what Buddhism calls the "sphere of neither perception nor non-perception"), they are in essence no different from ordinary people.

Therefore, in the elixir-refining of our orthodox school of the alchemical Way, four core elements must be firmly grasped: first, take the Original Spirit as the main body; second, take prior-heaven original breath as the provision supporting the Original Spirit; third, rely on the continuous, unbroken true breathing (that is, True Breath) as the basis for refining the elixir. Without the Original Spirit, there is no root from which the elixir can form; without the original breath, there is no auxiliary condition for completing it—just as one cannot conceive a child without the union of the father's essence and the mother's blood: there would be only an idea with nothing to make it real. Yet even with the Original Spirit and the original breath, if the body is not upright and settled and one has not entered natural, finely woven "embryonic breathing," Spirit and breath still cannot gather and condense in one place, merge into one, and return to the undivided original state of "Primordial Simplicity."

Here I will take it one turn deeper: even if a practitioner has obtained both the Original Spirit and the original breath, and True Breath is circulating within, able to assemble the five phases and four images and to unite Essence, Breath, and Spirit, the three, into one—still, without True Intention at the center as commander-in-chief, Spirit and breath will surely go their separate ways and run off in all directions; there is absolutely no way they will obediently come together of themselves to form the elixir.

That said, where does this "True Intention" arise from? It is by no means the scattered thoughts that ordinarily run through our minds. It must be this: when one has "attained utmost emptiness and kept steadfast stillness" and reached a state of profound quiet, undivided and free of knowing and awareness, suddenly the dynamic of breath stirs ever so slightly, and in that instant a point of numinous awareness surfaces of its own accord—this alone is genuine "True Intention," something entirely different from the ordinary heart and ordinary nature of posterior heaven!

Thus the ancients said: "An immortal is nothing other than this one primordial true nature, cultivated to completion." Yet if one has only this point of true nature, without the prior-heaven True Metal born within the kidney water (that is, the true breath transformed out of essence) to nourish and support it, then that true nature will hang alone in midair with nothing to rest on, and will inevitably fall into the "dull emptiness" of sitting in dead stillness. Conversely, even if one knows that the True Metal in the Cinnabar Field has arisen, without True Breath to gently regulate and hold it, how could one gather it, cook and refine it, and form the elixir?

From this we see that True Breath is the most essential tool for refining the elixir, and True Intention is the real master that commands True Breath. If those who study the Way have not yet achieved the union of Spirit and breath, how can they speak of true stillness and concentration? Once Spirit and breath rely on each other and return to the stem of life, one must brew and nurture them quietly, deeply, and patiently; only then can the complete Golden Elixir truly be accomplished. At this juncture, never stir up a "greatly bright, wakeful mind"—that is, do not use the posterior-heaven cognitive spirit to stare deliberately or show off a self-satisfied clear-eyed awareness. The moment this discriminating mind stirs, Metal and Wood are cut off from each other and Kan and Li can no longer conjoin. Through this chapter of Laozi I have laid bare the profound depths of the Great Way; if later students come to some understanding in their hearts, let them treasure it within and practice it with care!

Key Terms

  • The dim and obscure: Describes the state of the Great Way, beyond the senses, seeming both present and absent, unfathomably deep.
  • Original Spirit: The numinous, innate master present from prior heaven, which Huang calls "the root of the elixir."
  • True Breath: The fine, natural inner breathing, distinct from the coarse, heavy breathing through mouth and nose (ordinary breathing); also called "embryonic breathing" in the text, it is the essential tool for condensing Spirit and breath.
  • True Intention: The single numinous awakening that arises spontaneously when the dynamic of breath happens to stir in utmost emptiness and steadfast stillness, free of knowing and awareness; it is the commander that leads Spirit, breath, and True Breath, and is distinct from the posterior-heaven cognitive spirit.
  • Lead and Mercury: The alchemists' figurative names for prior-heaven true yang and true yin; Huang reminds older practitioners not to shrink back or make excuses because they consider their "lead and mercury insufficient."
  • Conjunction of Kan and Li: The image of water and fire merging, Spirit and breath becoming one; rashly stirring up the posterior-heaven discriminating, watchful mind causes "Metal and Wood to be cut off, and Kan and Li to fail to conjoin."
  • Dull emptiness (wankong): A lopsided, withered state of sitting that clings only to dead, empty stillness while neglecting the nourishment of true breath.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Age and background are never an excuse for not cultivating yourself. Huang specifically warns against using "declining years and insufficient essence and breath" as a pretext. Nurturing the heart and regulating the breath depend on gathering the mind in the present moment; it is never too late to begin.
  2. Leave room for others, and do not lightly label anyone "beyond help." "Heaven and earth discard nothing; the sage abandons no one." A person's temporary bias or mistake is often driven by habit and desire; with a little more gentle guidance and a little less harsh rejection, your own heart will feel more spacious too.
  3. When sitting, do not "try too hard to stay alert." Many people, when meditating, habitually stare at their thoughts and keep checking whether they have "calmed down yet"—this is exactly the "greatly bright, wakeful mind" Huang warns against. Relax and let your attention rest softly in the present; true peace comes more easily that way.
  4. Bring "the Way" back into everyday speech and action. Words spoken sincerely and fittingly are "fine words"; things done uprightly and steadily are "noble conduct." There is no need to chase mysterious marvels; the measured propriety of everyday words and deeds is the most solid practice.

Translator's Notes

  1. "The Way of the noble person is vast yet hidden" and "The Way cannot be departed from even for an instant": Both from the Doctrine of the Mean in the Book of Rites, traditionally attributed to Zisi, the grandson of Confucius. "Vast" (fei) refers to the boundlessly broad workings of the Way, "hidden" (yin) to its subtle, hard-to-see substance.
  2. "Heaven gave birth to the multitudes; where there are things, there are laws. The people hold to their constant nature and love this fine virtue": From the Classic of Poetry ("Greater Odes," Zhengmin). Zhengmin means the multitude of people; bingyi, holding to one's constant nature; yide, fine virtue.
  3. "The state of Chu holds nothing else as its treasure; it holds only good people as its treasure": Cited from the Book of Chu in the Great Learning of the Book of Rites. "If what one treasures is only the worthy, then the people close at hand will be at peace": from the Book of Documents ("The Hounds of Lü"), where "people close at hand" means the nearby populace.
  4. "Reaching neither-nor" (xiu ru feifei): That is, the Buddhist "absorption of the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception," the highest meditative absorption of the formless realm; though extremely subtle and still, it has not yet left the cycle of rebirth in the three realms.
  5. "Primordial Simplicity" (taisu): In Daoist thought, the original undifferentiated substance before heaven, earth, and all things became distinct; see Liezi, "Heaven's Gifts."
  6. "No strategy is better than the Way," "True Intention," "Metal and Wood cut off": The original woodblock edition or circulating editions contain graphic errors such as 莫道着也, 兵意, and 金木间腐; these have been corrected according to the context.
