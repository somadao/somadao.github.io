Chapter 65 · Those Who Excelled at Practicing the Way

Governing a state prizes guarding simplicity over cunning, and cultivating the Way above all takes "guarding foolishness" as the seed of the Way; one must also nurture the breath to forge the bright mirror and the sword of wisdom, so as to see through selfish desire and cut off false thoughts

About This Chapter

The line "not to make the people clever, but to make them simple" in Chapter 65 is all too easily misread as a "policy of keeping the people ignorant." Huang Yuanji goes straight to the point: what Laozi opposes is not wisdom at all, but the posterior-heaven scheming mind and crafty cleverness of intrigue and mutual deception; and the "foolishness" Laozi extols is not stupidity or dullness, but the undivided simplicity and natural innocence that remain once cunning has been washed away. The whole chapter advances "guarding foolishness" step by step to the level of practical work in cultivating the heart and refining one's nature:

  1. Shedding cunning and guarding simplicity (§2): When everyone in society prizes petty cleverness, scheming minds multiply and people plot against one another; personal self-cultivation is the same—every extra measure of calculation and scheming in the mind means one less measure of pure, quiet authenticity. A person's "guarding foolishness" differs from the muddled state of plants and beasts: beasts are dazed all day long, while a human being can, through guarding foolishness, return to the root and trace the source, all the way to spiritual transformation and the Great Accord.
  2. Foolishness is the seed of the Way; a single awakening is the original person (§3): When one sits until one is undivided and muddled, as if foolish or drunk, without thought or worry, the point of clear, true awareness that suddenly appears is the original face of the Original Spirit, free from the interference of stray thoughts. Simply guard this single right mindfulness and do not let the posterior-heaven cognitive spirit sprout new complications; in time even the right mindfulness dissolves into luminous emptiness—this is the work of "refining emptiness."
  3. First forge the "bright mirror" and the "sword of wisdom" (§3): Here Huang is especially practical: for someone whose heart is full of habitual tendencies, merely sitting and forcibly suppressing thoughts often makes things worse the harder one presses. One must first build up an abundant, flood-like upright breath through everyday "accumulating righteousness and nurturing the breath." Once the breath is sufficient, the "bright mirror" of the numinous tower can clearly reflect heavenly principle and human desire, and the "sword of wisdom" in one's hand becomes sharp enough to cut selfish desire in two the moment it shows its head.

Safety note: §3 of this chapter speaks of "refining emptiness" and "forgetting the self," as well as metaphors from the old texts such as "the six supernormal powers" and "slaying demons." In everyday quiet practice, never misunderstand "forgetting the self" as forcibly suppressing thought or pursuing a dazed, trance-like or dissociated feeling, and above all do not use "refining one's nature" or "subduing demons" as a pretext to cling to strange sensations or illusions of sound and light. If you are ordinarily low in mood, burdened by excessive worry, or experiencing insomnia and anxiety, rely mainly on a regular routine, gentle exercise, and professional psychological support, rather than fighting yourself by forcibly restraining your thoughts.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：古之善为道者，非以明民，将以愚之。民之难治，以其智多。故以智治国，国之贼；不以智治国，国之福。知此两者，亦楷式。能知楷式，是谓玄德。玄德，深矣远矣，与物反矣，然后乃至于大顺。

The Most High Lord Lao says: The rulers of antiquity who excelled at practicing the Great Way did not incite the people to show off their cunning and cleverness, but guided them back to simplicity and sincerity. The reason the people are hard to govern is precisely that they have too much scheming and craftiness. Therefore, to govern a state by playing with clever tricks and cunning is in fact to be a thief who harms the state; to govern a state not by cunning tricks but by simplicity and non-action is the good fortune of the state.

To understand the advantages and harms of these two—"prizing cleverness brings harm, guarding simplicity brings fortune"—is itself a model and standard one can follow. To see through and hold to this standard is called "Mysterious Virtue." This Mysterious Virtue is both profound and far-reaching; on the surface it appears exactly contrary to the worldly tendency to chase after things and compete in cleverness, yet by following it one finally arrives at the state of "Great Accord," in which all things are harmonious and flow freely.

§2
Original (Chinese)

天下凡事尚智，惟道不尚智而尚愚，愚则近乎道矣。圣门一贯薪传，惟愚鲁之曾子得之。故古之圣人，以道治天下，与民相见以道，不若与民相化于道，浑浑噩噩，同归清净之天，而一时之耕田凿井者，日出而作，日入而息，忘帝力于何有，顺帝则于不知。休哉！何俗之醇欤？降及后世，士大夫不尚愚而尚智，则机械频生，人心愈坏，贪鄙日甚，风俗弥偷，斯民之败度灭礼，犯法违条，愍不畏死者，殊难枚举，要皆尚才华、重聪明之智者，希图取伪，斯民之愚者，亦好阴谋，民之天真凿矣，诡谲多矣，而熙来攘往，彼诈此虞，为上者固有治之不胜治者焉，故曰：「以智治国，国之贼；不以智治国，国之福。」其故何哉？盖使民有知识，已破其浑沌之真。若能不识不知，乃完其无名之朴。两者智愚分焉，利害判焉。与其尚智而有害，何如尚愚而获利？知此两者，非但治世如是，即修身亦然，均堪为楷式焉。知此楷式，则近道矣。大修行人，于不睹不闻之地，返其无思无虑之神，非屏耳目、黜聪明，不能归于定静也。苟有一毫计较，一念谋为，则太朴不完，浑沌之天丧矣。知智之有损于己，愚之有益于身，不逞其智，乐守其愚，是即谓之玄德。大凡可名者非玄德，惟不可以名言，深无其极，远莫能知，乃可为玄德。虽与飞潜动植、蚩蚩蠢蠢之物，同一无欲无知，但物不能即绪穷源，终日昏聩而已。人则由粗及精，从原达委，以至于三元合一，太极归真，犹可底于神化，至于大顺，不诚与物反哉？

Nearly everything in the world prizes cleverness and wit; only the cultivation of the Way does not prize wit but instead prizes "foolish simplicity"—guard this simplicity of great wisdom that looks like foolishness, and you are very close to the Great Way. Back then, the heart-method transmission of "one thread running through it all" in the school of Confucius was received precisely by Zengzi, who was by nature honest, solid, and plain.

So when the sages of antiquity governed the world by the Great Way, rather than putting on a stern face and lecturing the people on great principles, they let the people return together to the Way through quiet, imperceptible influence, undivided and simple, sharing the enjoyment of a pure and carefree world. In those days, the common folk who tilled the fields and dug wells rose with the sun to work and rested at sunset, never feeling the intervention of the emperor's power at all, and without knowing it they accorded with the laws of heaven and earth. How fine it was—how pure and wholesome the customs of that time must have been!

In later ages, the scholar-officials no longer esteemed simplicity but single-mindedly esteemed wit and clever argument. As a result, cunning schemes appeared one after another, people's hearts grew ever more corrupt, greed and meanness grew worse day by day, and social customs became ever more shallow and slippery. Countless were the cases of common people corrupting the law, scorning ritual and righteousness, breaking the law and defying regulations, brazenly unafraid of death. Traced to the root, it was all because the "clever ones" above, who flaunted their talents and valued petty cleverness, took the lead in deceit and pretense, so that even originally honest common folk followed them in learning intrigue and trickery. People's natural innocence was bored through, crafty and devious minds multiplied, the streets bustled with crowds coming and going, you plotting against me and I guarding against you, and however hard those in power racked their brains to govern, they simply could not keep up.

So the classic says: "Governing the state with cunning and cleverness makes one a thief who harms the state; governing the state without cunning and cleverness is the good fortune of the state." Why is this? Because once the people's endless scheming and calculating knowledge has been stirred up, their original, undivided and innocent vitality is bored through; conversely, if one does not show off cleverness or give rise to false knowledge, one can instead preserve whole the original truth of the "nameless uncarved block."

On these two paths, the dividing line between cleverness and foolishness is perfectly clear, and the consequences of benefit and harm are plain to see. Rather than prizing cunning and bringing harm on oneself, how much better to guard simplicity and gain endless benefit! Understanding the difference between these two holds not only for governing the world but equally for personal self-cultivation; both can serve as a standard (model) for conducting oneself. To truly understand and hold to this standard is to be not far from the Way.

A truly great practitioner, in the stillness of withdrawing sight and turning hearing inward, where nothing is seen or heard, seeks to recover that original mind-spirit free of thought and worry; without putting away the eyes and ears that chase outward and setting aside the petty cleverness that shows off and calculates, one can never return to concentration and stillness. As soon as even the tiniest bit of calculation mixes into the heart, or a single scheming, plotting thought arises, the originally whole "Great Simplicity" is broken, and the undivided, innocent inner realm is lost. To understand that flaunting cleverness harms oneself and guarding clumsiness and foolishness benefits one's person, and so no longer to show off one's intelligence but contentedly and gladly to guard simplicity—this is called "Mysterious Virtue."

Whatever can be flaunted through name and appearance is not Mysterious Virtue; only that profound virtue which cannot be boasted of in words, bottomlessly deep and unfathomably far, deserves to be called Mysterious Virtue. On the surface, this state free of desire and knowledge seems no different from the muddled, dull creatures that fly in the sky, swim in the water, run on the ground, and grow in the soil; but the difference is enormous! All other creatures are merely in torpor and confusion all day long, utterly unable to follow the first clues back to the source of life; a human being, by contrast, can, within this simplicity, stillness, and concentration, proceed from the coarse to the refined, from the source to its streams, cultivating all the way until the three origins of Essence, Breath, and Spirit unite as one and the Supreme Ultimate returns to its original truth, thereby entering the realm of wondrous transformation and reaching the Great Accord in which nothing fails to go well—is this not something that seems contrary to the worldly chase after things, but is in fact a return to the root and back to the source?

§3
Original (Chinese)

治国不尚智，而修道尤贵愚，诚以智为国之贼，愚为道之种也。夫愚何以为道种哉？试思混沌中，无念虑，无知识，非所谓愚耶？忽然一觉，即是我不生不灭之本来人。莫说把持此觉，修成无上正等正觉，方能免却轮回，不受阴阳鼓铸，不为鬼神拘滞，即此混混沌沌中，忽然一觉，我以真意守而不散，此一觉已到般若波罗蜜。果能拳拳服膺，常常把守，而轮回种子，即从此断矣。若另起一念，生一见，就是后天识欲之神，夹杂其中，所谓「无量劫来生死本，痴人唤作本来人」是也。要之，神一也，有欲则为二矣。二意三心，即是杂妄根尘，所以有生死之路。惟有一心，无二念，有正念，无邪心，道在是矣。若能并将此一心正念而悉化之，是为太极还于无极。金仙之成，即在此炼虚矣。何谓炼虚？即如混沌之际，懵懵懂懂，如愚如醉，无觉无知，即虚也。坐到无人无我，何地何天，即炼虚也。又曰：学道之要，始而忘人，继而忘我，终而忘法，以至于忘忘之极，乃为究竟。人能以把此一刻为主，以真觉为用，道不远矣。然炼虚之法，虽是如此，其功必自炼性始。炼性，古人名为铸镜也。若心有不炼，则昏昏罔罔，冥然无觉，虽近在目前，尚且不能知，何况具六通者乎？盖皆由私欲之杂乱其心志，而未至于虚也。如真觉之后，不许一丝半蒂存于胸中，即灵台之宝镜，常放光明。而又非必功满行圆，乃放毫光也，即此混混沌沌中，忽然一知，不复他知，忽然一觉，不更他觉，此一刻中，即洞彻光明，四达不悖。虽然，学人满腔私欲，忽期洁白晶莹，如玉如金，夫岂一念之虚静，所能了哉？必要先铸雌雄二剑，以去有形无形之魔。此剑不利，则欲魔色魔、天魔人魔，难以扫除净尽，现出乾元真面目也。盖人欲天理，混杂多年，虽欲独立中流，势有难于抵敌者，以故明知之而明蹈之，皆由引之入人欲者众，引之入天理者少也。今为学人告，欲成清净法身，必先有清净之神；欲得清净之神，必先有浩荡之气。所云铸剑，无他，即由平旦之气，直养无害，以至于浩然刚大，斯神剑成而锋芒利，可以斩妖断邪。斯时也，莫说淫声绝色入目而心不乱，即有美女同眠，亦不知也；莫说凶魔恶曜到身边而神自如，即有泰山崩前，亦不畏也。此神剑之造成者，自有志气如神之一候。只恐工行不深，或作或辍，不肯当下立定脚跟耳。若能一刀两断，一私起即灭除，灭除不复再生，此断生死轮回之路矣。学道人别无他妙，只怕认不得明镜神剑耳。如能认得，此刻中有明镜普照，恶妄不容，慧剑长悬，欲魔立断，自此一念把持将去，然后神室可成，而仙丹可炼矣。此明镜慧剑，为修道人之要务。设剑锋不利，安能断绝邪魔？所以心愈制而愈乱也。宝镜无光，难以分别理欲，所以己弥克而弥多也。孟子言养气而不言养心，诚谓气足而心自定耳。彼徒强制夫心，而不知集义生气，去道远矣。李二曲云：「人心本自乐，自将私欲缚。私欲一萌时，良知还自觉。一觉便消除，此心依旧乐。」拙翁云：「光明寂照遍河沙，凡圣原来共一家。一念不生全体现，六根才动被云遮。断除烦恼重增病，趋向真如亦是邪。世事随缘无罣碍，涅槃生死等空华。」有心性学者，当三复斯言！

Governing a state does not prize cunning, and cultivating the Way values "guarding foolishness" all the more—truly because cunning and cleverness are thieves that ruin the state, while guarding clumsiness and foolishness is the seed that gives birth to the Great Way. Why is "foolishness" called the seed of the Great Way? Think about it: when one has become so still as to be in an undivided chaos, without the slightest thought arising and without the slightest stirring of knowledge or discrimination, is that not precisely the state of "foolishness"? And right in this undivided, silent chaos, a point of clear, bright illumination suddenly arises—that is my true self, originally complete, unborn and undying (the Original Spirit).

Do not think you must cultivate until your merit is perfect and you realize unsurpassed, perfect, complete enlightenment before you count as free of bondage. Simply within this undivided, muddled stillness, when that point of true awareness suddenly appears, if you can guard it steadily with True Intention and not let it scatter, this moment of clear illumination has in fact already entered what the Buddhists call "prajñāpāramitā" (the wisdom that crosses to the other shore of awakening). If you can hold fast to it and guard this single clear thought at all times, the seeds of false thought that drag you through birth and death can be cut off on the spot.

Conversely, if after this you raise another calculating thought or produce a posterior-heaven view, then the posterior-heaven cognitive spirit and selfish desire have mixed in. A verse of an ancient master puts it well: "The root of birth and death through countless kalpas—the foolish call it the original person." That is, people in the world often mistake the cognitive spirit that daydreams all day long for their true self. In the final analysis, Spirit is originally pure and one, not two; once it is tainted by selfish desire, it splits into two. A divided, wavering mind is nothing but confused false thoughts and the grime of the senses, and it is precisely the forked road of revolving in birth and death. Only by guarding a heart that is pure and one, not two, with only right mindfulness and no deviant mind, is the Great Way right here. If one goes further and completely dissolves even the subtle trace of "I am guarding this single right mindfulness," that is the Supreme Ultimate returning to the Ultimate of Non-being. The key to accomplishing golden immortality lies entirely in this work of "refining emptiness."

What is "refining emptiness"? It is like the time before chaos was opened: outwardly one looks dazed and confused, as if foolish or drunk, without awareness or knowledge—this is "emptiness." Sitting until there is neither other nor self, forgetting what place one is in and what heaven is above—this is "refining emptiness." The ancients also said: the essential point in studying the Way is first to forget others, then to forget oneself, and finally to forget even the method one practices, until even the thought of "forgetting" is completely forgotten—only then has one reached the ultimate. If practitioners can take the stillness of this moment as their mainstay and that point of spontaneously arising true awareness as its wondrous function, they are not far from the Great Way.

However, although the principle of refining emptiness is thus, actual practice must begin with "refining one's nature" (refining the self). The ancients vividly called this refining of one's nature "casting the mirror." If one is ordinarily unwilling to put effort into tempering mind and nature, the heart will be drowsy and muddled, unable to see clearly even what is right before one's eyes, let alone any penetrating, clear-sighted state. All of this is because selfish desire has stirred up the mind and will, so that one has never reached the realm of luminous emptiness and stillness. If, after true awareness has appeared, one does not allow the slightest selfish or stray thought to remain in the breast, then the precious mirror of the numinous tower will naturally give forth light. Nor is it necessary to wait until one's practice is complete for it to shine—right in the midst of the undivided muddle, one suddenly knows clearly, just once, with no other stray knowing mixed in; suddenly illumines plainly, just once, with no other false awareness springing up—and in that very instant the heart is already thoroughly luminous, open in all four directions without the slightest obstruction.

That said, we practitioners have carried so many years of selfish desire and habitual tendency in our hearts; to want to become all at once as white and crystalline as fine jade and true gold—how could that be settled by a moment of quiet sitting? One must first forge in one's mind and will the sharp "male and female swords of wisdom" in order to sweep away all the demonic obstacles, visible and invisible. If this sword is not sharp enough, the demon of desire and the demon of lust within, and the disturbances of circumstance and the temptations of human affairs without, will all be hard to clear away completely, and one's original pure true face of Qian will not appear. For human desire and heavenly principle have been mixed together in the heart for so many years that even if one very much wants to stand firm like a rock in midstream, one is often willing but powerless—so one often knows perfectly well one should not do something and yet cannot help jumping in! The reason is that the forces pulling you down into human desire are too many and those leading you toward heavenly principle too few.

Today I tell practitioners the plain truth: if you wish to accomplish the pure spontaneous Dharma Body, you must first have a pure mind-spirit; if you wish to obtain a pure mind-spirit, you must first nurture a vast, abundant upright breath. What is called "forging the sword" is no mysterious magic; it is simply beginning with the clear, peaceful "breath of early dawn" each morning, correcting one's words and deeds, nurturing it in accord with principle without harming it, until it becomes what Mencius called the flood-like, strong, and great breath. Once the upright breath is fully nurtured, the spirit-sword in the heart is truly forged, its edge gleaming, able to slay goblins and cut off the deviant. When one reaches this degree of fire, never mind decadent music and peerless beauty flashing before one's eyes without stirring the heart—even with a beautiful woman at one's side, the heart remains still as water; never mind keeping one's composure when perilous and evil circumstances press close—even if Mount Tai collapsed before one's eyes, one would not panic in the least. Once this spirit-sword has been accomplished, there naturally comes the stable power and state of "a will and breath like that of a spirit."

The only fear is that people's practice does not go deep enough—working two days and resting three—and that they are unwilling to resolve firmly, right now, to plant their feet. If one can truly cut cleanly in two, cutting off each selfish thought the moment it shows its head, and not letting it rekindle like dead ashes after it has been cut off, that is the fundamental path to severing the cycle of afflictions and rebirth. Those who study the Way have no other secret; the only fear is that they fail to recognize this "bright mirror" and "spirit-sword." Once you recognize them clearly, there is in your heart at this moment a bright mirror hanging high and shining everywhere, leaving no room for evil or false thoughts, and a sword of wisdom always in hand, able to cut off selfish desire the instant it stirs; from then on, hold this single thought steadily, and only then can the chamber of the spirit be built and the Golden Elixir be refined.

This bright mirror and sword of wisdom are truly the foremost task of the practitioner of the Way. If the edge of the sword is not keen, with what will you cut off the deviant demons? That is why merely suppressing by force only makes the heart more disordered the more it is suppressed. If the precious mirror is covered with dust and gives no light, one cannot even tell what is heavenly principle and what is selfish desire; that is why the more one tries to overcome the selfish self, the more selfish thoughts spring up. Mencius emphasized only "nurturing the breath" and did not talk idly of "nurturing the heart" precisely because he knew deeply that once the upright breath is abundant, the mind-spirit naturally settles. Those who know only how to hold down their thoughts by force and grit their teeth, but do not understand nurturing the upright breath through accumulating virtue and practicing righteousness in daily life, are still far from the Great Way!

The great late-Ming Confucian Li Erqu wrote a poem: "The human heart is originally joyful and at ease; it is only that one binds oneself with selfish desire. Whenever selfish desire first sprouts, one's innate good knowing can in fact perceive it by itself; as soon as this perception dissolves the selfish desire on the spot, this heart at once returns to its original joy." The verse of awakening of Zhang Zhuo (the "Old Man Zhuo") of the Tang dynasty also says: "The originally complete light shines in silence, filling worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges; ordinary people and sages were originally of one family. When not a single false thought arises, the whole of the Great Way appears at once; as soon as the six roots—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind—stir wildly after external objects, the original heart is veiled by floating clouds. Deliberately wrestling to the death with afflictions only adds a further sickness of the heart; greedily pursuing so-called 'suchness' also falls into deviation. Respond to the affairs of the world as conditions arise, leaving no attachment or hindrance in the heart, and even nirvana and birth-and-death look like nothing more than illusory flowers in the sky." All who aspire to cultivate mind and nature should ponder these two passages again and again and engrave them on their hearts!

Key Terms

  • Mysterious Virtue (xuande): A profound virtue that does not flaunt names, forms, or clever skills, deep and far beyond measure, and in accord with the original nature of the Great Way; outwardly it looks foolish, simple, and ignorant, but inwardly it returns to the root and to the truth.
  • Original Spirit and cognitive spirit: The point of clear, true awareness that suddenly arises in the undivided muddle of utmost stillness is the Original Spirit; the posterior-heaven thoughts of calculation, discrimination, and attachment that arise afterward are the cognitive spirit.
  • Refining emptiness (lianxu): In stillness and concentration, forgetting others, then oneself, then the method in turn, until even the subtle trace of "guarding right mindfulness" naturally dissolves, returning to the luminous emptiness of the Supreme Ultimate returning to the Ultimate of Non-being.
  • Bright mirror and sword of wisdom (refining one's nature, forging the sword): Huang's vivid metaphors for the work of refining the self. The "bright mirror" refers to the clarity of the heart's substance, the awareness that distinguishes principle from desire at a glance; the "sword of wisdom" refers to the flood-like steadiness and decisiveness nurtured through everyday accumulation of righteousness and nurturing of the breath. Neither can be missing.
  • Breath of early dawn (pingdan zhi qi): From Mencius ("Gaozi I"); the clear, peaceful breath of a person who has just awoken in the early morning, before being stirred up by the day's many desires and utilitarian calculations.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Use less "petty cleverness" and keep more "plodding effort." In dealing with people and getting things done, the more you calculate and show off your wit every day, the more easily you fall into the drain of mutual suspicion; bring your heart back, hold to openness and sincerity, and things actually go most smoothly (the Great Accord).
  2. Do not try to calm the mind by "forcibly suppressing thoughts." Huang exposes a common misconception: the harder you wrestle with stray thoughts, the more disordered the mind becomes ("the more the heart is suppressed, the more disordered it grows"). If you want the mind to settle, the work lies not in gritting your teeth at the moment of sitting but in "accumulating righteousness and nurturing the breath" in everyday life—doing fewer things that weigh on your conscience and drain your spirit, and more things that are open and aboveboard and nourish the breath.
  3. Practice "first-second awareness" (Li Erqu's heart-method). When you cannot stop scrolling on your phone, or when envy or complaint is just beginning, do not start by blaming yourself; simply see clearly, as if looking in a mirror, "Oh, selfish desire is stirring," and this awareness itself will remove most of its stickiness.
  4. Guarding foolishness is not acting dumb; it is "leaving blank space." Each day, give yourself ten-odd minutes of "foolishness-guarding time" in which you do not scroll through information or calculate gains and losses, letting your tense brain step back from over-analysis; your intuition and judgment will actually become clearer.

  2. "Is also a model" (yi kaishi): Most received editions of the Daodejing read 亦稽式 (ji being interchangeable with kai, meaning model or standard); Huang's commentary edition reads 楷式 directly, and the translation follows Huang's edition.
  3. "The root of birth and death through countless kalpas—the foolish call it the original person": From a verse by the Tang Chan master Changsha Jingcen: "Those who study the Way do not recognize the true, simply because all along they have taken the cognitive spirit for it. The root of birth and death through countless kalpas—the foolish call it the original person."
  4. "Prajñāpāramitā": A transliteration of the Sanskrit, meaning "wisdom gone to the other shore," a figure for clear awakening that transcends attachment and penetrates directly to the true nature of reality.
  5. Li Erqu and the Old Man Zhuo: Li Erqu (1627–1705), personal name Yong, courtesy name Zhongfu, sobriquet Erqu, was a native of Zhouzhi in Shaanxi and a famous Neo-Confucian of the early Qing, ranked with Sun Qifeng and Huang Zongxi as the "Three Great Confucians of the early Qing"; his works include Records of Reflecting on the Four Books in Oneself and the Collected Works of Erqu. (The base edition misplaced this footnote into the main text of Chapter 66 and misread 黄宗羲 as 黄宗發; it has been moved back to this chapter and corrected here. Note that the poem "The human heart is originally joyful" is also widely attributed in Ming and Qing texts to Wang Yangming or his disciples; Huang's commentary cites it as Li Erqu's words.) "The Old Man Zhuo" refers to Zhang Zhuo of the Tang dynasty, whose verse of awakening, "The light shines in silence, filling worlds like the sands of the Ganges," is recorded in the Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp.
  6. Textual note: In the original commentary, the base edition miswrote 耕田凿井 as 料田凿井, 日入而息 as 日人而息, 无思无虑 as 尢思无虑, 己 in 有损于己 and 己弥克 as 已, 道种哉 as 道种裁, 忽然一觉 as 忽怎一觉, 无觉无知 as 无党无知, and 盖皆由私欲 as 若皆由私欲; the OCR garbage character "コ" mixed into 明知之而明蹈之 has also been removed. All have been corrected one by one.
