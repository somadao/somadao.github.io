Chapter 70 · Wearing Coarse Cloth, Holding Jade Within

The Great Way is supremely easy to know and supremely easy to practise, and lies entirely in one word in the human body, "center"—keep the center that has form to concentrate Spirit and regulate the breath, keep the formless center to contemplate the state before the emotions arise; a level heart is itself the Mysterious Pass

About This Chapter

Chapter 70 opens with a frank sigh from Laozi: "My words are very easy to understand and very easy to practise, yet no one in the world can understand them, no one can practise them." Why is it that such plain, easily practised words are precisely what the world cannot grasp or carry out? Because people have not seized the master principle: "Words have an ancestor; deeds have a lord!" Huang Yuanji reveals this master principle in a single phrase—that "ancestor" and that "lord" is the one word "center" in the human body:

  1. The Way is near, yet people seek it far away (§2–§3): People always assume the Great Way is unfathomably deep, and plunge into literary composition and rote recitation, numerology and side paths, their hearts growing more barren the more they learn—forgetting that the Great Way lies in each person's innate good knowing and good capacity and in everyday conduct. The sage does not care whether the world understands him; outwardly wearing coarse cloth, inwardly holding the most precious jade, he keeps to himself in peace.
  2. Cultivating the "center that has form" and the "formless center" in parallel (§3): Here Huang Yuanji gives an exceptionally clear method that points straight to practice. The "center that has form" is reverting the light to shine back within and gently resting attention on the place of the compass's center one cun three fen below the navel (the lower Cinnabar Field), neither touching nor leaving. The "formless center" is "contemplating, in stillness, the state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight arise," as transmitted by the Song Confucian Luo Congyan to Li Yanping. One cultivates life (ming), the other cultivates nature (xing), proceeding step by step from effortful holding into pure naturalness.
  3. The two words "level heart" are the Mysterious Pass (§3): Whether concentrating Spirit or regulating the breath, a thousand words and ten thousand phrases come down to the two words "level heart"—a heart that raises no waves is called level; being able to hold steadily to the center is called level. When the heart is level, Spirit concentrates of itself; when the Breath is harmonious, the breathing regulates itself. Where Spirit and Breath rely on each other, the Mysterious Pass has its foundation and the Golden Elixir its beginning.

Safety note: §3 of this chapter discusses inner-alchemical methods such as "keeping the center that has form, neither touching nor leaving the place one cun three fen below the navel," as well as "advancing the fire and withdrawing the tally, turning the River Chariot." When practising "attention to the compass's center," always remember what Huang Yuanji repeatedly stresses—"neither touching nor leaving" and "a level heart and harmonious Breath." Rest your attention lightly, as if holding and yet not holding; never stare fixedly at the lower abdomen or use forceful intention to guide and transport the breath, or you may easily bring on distension and oppression in the lower abdomen, stagnant qi and rising heat, or agitation and insomnia. If you feel any discomfort, release the intention at once and return to natural breathing.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：吾言甚易知，甚易行，天下莫能知，莫能行。言有宗，事有君。夫惟无知，是以不我知。知我者希，则我贵矣。是以圣人被褐怀玉。

Lord Lao says: What I say is in fact very easy to understand and very easy to carry out, yet the people of the world can neither make sense of it nor do it! Know that every word I speak has a single overall source (an "ancestor"), and every matter I speak of has a single overall master (a "lord"). It is precisely because people do not understand this fundamental ancestor and master that they do not understand me. The rarer those who truly understand me, the more precious the Great Way I hold appears. Therefore the sage would rather wear, outwardly, a short garment of coarse, lowly cloth, while inwardly he quietly treasures a jade beyond price.

§2
Original (Chinese)

夫道者，人心固有之良，日用常行之事，至近至约，不可须臾离也，离则无道，无道则无人，又何言之有？况吾之所言，虽累千累万，盈箧溢籍，不可胜数，要皆切于人心，近于日用，无有难知难行者。顾何以天下莫能知、莫能行也？岂吾言之不易知、不易行乎？盖言有宗也，即人所不学而知之良知也；事有君也，即人所不学而能之良能也。惟言知有宗，则近取诸身，而言皆善言；事知有君，则默窥其隐，而行皆善行。夫道若大路然，岂难知难行者哉？「反身而诚，乐莫大焉。」若不知言之有宗，事之有君，而求诸高远之地、广博之乡，是以玩物丧志，务广而荒，心为形役，性为气累，而本来天德之良，迷而不悟，竟以吾言之甚易者，转似大而莫之纪、远而无可稽，不良可慨欤？虽然，其知也，于我何加？其不知也，于我何损？况我之所以为我，初不因人之知不知也。知我者希，则我之贵乎我者，仍自若也。是以圣人外被至贱之褐，内怀至贵之玉，晦迹山林，藏身岩穴，亦惟顺性命之理、参天地之道，以修其在己，而人之知否从违，概不问焉，此所以圣者益圣，而愚者愈愚矣。

The so-called "Way" is in fact the good heavenly nature that every person's heart possesses from the start, and also the ordinary things done at every moment of daily life. It is right before our eyes, simple and concise, and cannot be departed from for a single moment—depart from it and there is no Way; without the Way one is no longer truly human—so what empty words would there be left to speak?

Moreover, the principles I speak of, even if written down in thousands upon thousands of words, filling book chests and piling up into canons beyond counting, are in the final analysis all plain truths close to the human heart and suited to daily use; there is nothing in them hard to understand or hard to carry out. Why, then, can the people of the world neither make sense of them nor do them? Is it really that my words are too abstruse to understand and too exacting to practise?

Not at all! The reason is this: what I say has a fundamental source ("words have an ancestor"), and that source is what Mencius called "the good knowing that people know without learning"; what I speak of has a mainstay ("deeds have a lord"), and that mainstay is "the good capacity that people possess without learning." As long as you understand that there is this "ancestor" in the words and examine everything close at hand in your own body and mind, every word you speak will be a word of supreme principle and goodness; as long as you understand that there is this "lord" in deeds and quietly observe the subtle first stirrings within your heart, every deed you do will be a good deed.

The Great Way is like a broad, level highway—how could it be some sheer cliff or perilous path hard to know and hard to travel? The Mencius puts it well: "To turn back upon oneself and find oneself sincere—there is no greater joy than this!" If you do not understand that words have an ancestor and deeds a lord, but instead run about searching in lofty, unreachable abstractions and in boundless, jumbled seas of books, the result will be that toying with things you lose your aims, greedy for breadth you grow neglectful, the heart-spirit becomes a slave to the body's desires for things, the nature is dragged down by a restless temperament, and the good knowing and good capacity originally bestowed by Heaven are completely lost. You then take these plainest, most easily practised words of mine for a heavenly book so vast you cannot find its edges and so remote there is no way to verify it—is this not deeply to be lamented?

Even so, what would the world's understanding me add to me? What would the world's not understanding me take away from me? Moreover, what makes me who I am does not depend in the least on whether others understand. However rare those who understand me may be, that truly noble authentic self within me remains steady, secure and at ease as ever!

Therefore the sage would rather drape himself outwardly in the coarsest, most inconspicuous short hemp garment, while inwardly clasping close the most precious, flawless jade. Perhaps living in seclusion in mountain forests and hiding in rocky caves, he only attends to complying with the principles of inner nature and life and assisting the Great Way of heaven and earth, steadily cultivating what lies within his own province. As for whether others approve of him or follow him, he never inquires or concerns himself at all. As a result, the sage grows ever clearer and holier the more he cultivates, while ordinary people, obstinately deluded, sink ever deeper into confusion and folly!

§3
Original (Chinese)

太上之言，头头是道，字字切身，即人以言道，即道以言身，易莫易于此矣，夫何难知难行者哉？顾人之昧昧者，良由道在迩而求诸远，事在易而求诸难，不务真常大道，反求糟粕绪余，如辞章记诵、刑名术数之类，学愈博而心愈荒，事愈繁而性愈劣，无怪乎太上道言，当时为人心所同，后世为太上所独也，良由不明言之有宗、事之有君耳。夫宗者、君者，即人身之「中」也。尧舜授受心传，无非「允执厥中」而已。后如文之「纯一」，参之「慎独」，轲之「良知」，莫非人身之一「中」也。此个「中」字，所包甚广。其在人身，一在守有形之「中」，朱子云「守中制外」。夫守中者，回光返照，注意规中，于脐下一寸三分处，不即不离是。一在守无形之「中」，《中庸》云「喜怒哀乐之未发谓之中」。罗从彦教李延平「静中观喜怒哀乐未发气象」，此未发时，不闻不睹，戒慎恐惧，自然性定神清，方见本来面目，然后人欲易净，天理复明。自古圣贤仙佛，皆以此为第一步工夫。但始须守乎勉然之中，终则纯乎自然之中。三圣人名目各有不同，总不外此「中」字，为之宗、为之君。即如吾教以凝神调息为主，然后回观本窍，心无其心，气无其气，乃得心平气和，心平则神始凝，气和则息始调，其要只在「心平」二字。心不起波之谓平，能执其中之谓平，平即在此中也。心在此中，即丹经之玄关一窍。到得神气相依，玄关之体已立，此为大道根源，金丹本始。他如进火退符，搬运河车，有为有作，总贵谦和柔顺，以整以暇，勿助勿忘，有要归无，无又生有，至有无不立，方合天然道体，此即「得一而万事毕」，「吾道一以贯之」之旨也。学者知此，太上之经可解，庶不为旁门左道所惑也。若不知言之有宗，事之有君，未许升堂入室而不迷于他往者。人能知此行此，自然有得于中，无慕乎外，如圣人之被褐怀玉，而融融泄泄不已焉。

Every word Lord Lao speaks accords with the Way at every point and every character bears close upon the body—speaking of the Great Way in terms of the human body and mind, and settling body and mind in terms of the Great Way. Nothing in the world could be simpler or more direct than this; what is there that is hard to understand or hard to practise?

Yet people remain muddled and cannot see clearly, and the root lies in the fault Mencius criticized: "The Way is clearly near at hand, yet they insist on seeking it far away; the task is clearly simple, yet they insist on burrowing into the difficult and complicated." They will not steadily cultivate this eternal, true and constant Great Way, but instead study its husks and dregs—such as showing off literary style and reciting canons from memory, or delving into the techniques of law and statecraft and all kinds of esoteric numerology. As a result, the more they read, the more jumbled it becomes and the more barren their hearts; the more they busy themselves, the more restless and base their nature becomes. No wonder that the supreme words of Lord Lao, which in his own time were common knowledge shared by every human heart, became in later ages a lost art understood by Lord Lao alone—all because later people failed to see through "words have an ancestor, deeds have a lord"!

So what exactly are this "ancestor" and this "lord"? To reveal it in one phrase: it is the one word "center" in the human body (guarding the center and embracing the One)! The sixteen-character heart method that Emperor Yao passed to Emperor Shun has at its core nothing but "sincerely hold fast to the center." Later, King Wen of Zhou spoke of "purity and oneness without admixture," Zeng Shen in the Great Learning spoke of "vigilance in solitude," and Meng Ke (Mencius) spoke of "good knowing"—different words, yet all of them in fact speak of this one word "center" in the human body!

The meaning of this word "center" is extremely broad. Applied to our own bodies, it divides into two complementary practices:

The first is keeping the "center that has form." Zhu Xi once said, "Keep the center to govern the outer" (keep the center of the heart, and you can restrain external temptations). In actual practice, this means reverting the light to shine back within and gently resting attention on that Cavity of Breath at the compass's center, one cun three fen below the navel (the lower Cinnabar Field / concentrating Spirit and entering the Cavity of Breath), achieving "neither touching nor leaving"—neither staring and clinging fixedly, nor letting it go and scatter completely.

The second is keeping the "formless center." The Doctrine of the Mean says: "The peaceful state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight have arisen is called the 'center.'" The great Northern Song Confucian Luo Congyan, teaching his disciple Li Yanping, specifically transmitted "observing, in quiet sitting, the state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight have arisen." Right at this moment before the emotions arise, withdrawing sight and turning hearing inward, hearing nothing and seeing nothing, holding a clear, reverent caution and wariness, the heart and nature naturally become settled and the spirit clear; only then does one truly see one's original face. From then on, the human desires of Posterior Heaven are easily washed clean, and the heavenly principle of Prior Heaven shines forth clearly once more.

Since antiquity, among the sages, worthies, immortals and buddhas of the three teachings of Confucianism, Buddhism and Daoism, there is not one who did not take keeping this "center" as the very first step of practice! It is only that at the outset one inevitably still has to rely on a measure of effort to keep this "effortful center"; once the work has matured, it naturally transforms into the effortless "natural center." Although the sages of the three teachings gave it different names, in the end none of them departs from this word "center" as the fundamental ancestor and master.

Take our own alchemical Way: it begins chiefly with concentrating Spirit and regulating the breath, then turns the light back to contemplate the original wondrous opening, growing still until one forgets deliberately applying the mind and forgets deliberately managing the Breath ("the heart without its heart, the Breath without its Breath"); only then does one truly enter the state of "a level heart and harmonious Breath." When the heart is level, Spirit can truly concentrate; when the Breath is harmonious, the breathing can truly become even. Of all the thousands of words, the most essential secret lies in the two words "level heart"!

What is "level"? When the water of the heart raises no waves, that is called level; being able to hold steadily to the place of central uprightness without leaning is called level—"level" lies within this very "center"! As long as the heart rests steadily on this "center," that is at once the single opening of the Mysterious Pass that the alchemical classics call for a thousand times over. When one has cultivated to the point where Spirit and Breath naturally rely on and accompany each other, and the substance of the Mysterious Pass stands firmly established, this is the root and source of the Great Way and the true starting point of the Golden Elixir.

As for the later methods that seem to involve action and doing—advancing the yang fire and withdrawing the yin tally (fire phasing), turning the River Chariot (the Celestial Circuit)—what matters is always maintaining humility, harmony, gentleness and compliance, unhurried and composed ("orderly and at leisure"), following the rhythm of "neither helping nor forgetting." The work of action must finally return to the stillness of non-action, and from the utmost stillness of non-action the vitality of action naturally arises again, until one has cultivated to the point where not even a trace of the opposition between "being" and "non-being" remains standing—only then is one truly in step with the natural substance of the Way! This is the whole purport of Laozi's "attain the One, and the ten thousand affairs are done" and Confucius's "my Way is threaded through by one."

Once the student of the Way sees through this layer, every word and phrase of the whole Daodejing can be readily understood, and he will never again be bewildered by the flashy side doors and crooked paths of the world. If one does not understand that "words have an ancestor, deeds have a lord" lies entirely in this word "center," then even after reading ten thousand volumes, one need not hope ever truly to ascend the hall and enter the inner chamber without straying down wrong paths. If a person can truly understand this and steadily act on it, his inner self will naturally be full and abundant, without the least envy of vanities outside himself—just like the sage who wears coarse cloth outside and holds fine jade within, all day long harmonious and at ease, his joy without end!

Key Terms

  • Guarding the center and embracing the One (the center that has form, the formless center): Huang Yuanji uses the word "center" to thread together Confucianism and Daoism. The "center that has form" refers to reverting the light to shine back within and gently resting attention on the lower Cinnabar Field one cun three fen below the navel (neither touching nor leaving); the "formless center" refers to the clear, peaceful state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight arise, as described in the Doctrine of the Mean.
  • Yanping's contemplation of the not-yet-arisen: The Neo-Confucian method of quiet sitting transmitted by Luo Congyan of the Northern Song to Li Tong (styled Yanping)—"contemplating, in stillness, the state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight arise"—whose aim is to realize the original heart and heavenly principle in a stillness where the emotions have not yet stirred.
  • A level heart and harmonious Breath (the two words "level heart"): Huang Yuanji's most concise master formula for concentrating Spirit and regulating the breath—"A heart that raises no waves is called level; being able to hold to the center is called level." When the heart is level, Spirit concentrates of itself; when the Breath is harmonious, the breathing regulates itself; a heart at rest in this center is itself the Mysterious Pass.
  • Orderly and at leisure: Describes being calm, composed and methodical in practice and in handling affairs, never thrown into disarray by haste.
  • Wearing coarse cloth and holding jade within: Plain and ordinary on the outside ("wearing coarse cloth"), yet full and precious within, embracing the true Way ("holding jade"); describes the self-sufficient bearing of the true practitioner, who neither shows off nor craves vanity.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Don't get lost in "the more I learn, the more anxious I get." Today information and courses are everywhere, and it is easy to end up as Huang Yuanji criticized: "the more widely they learn, the more barren their hearts." First reclaim the core "ancestor and master"—is your heart level or not? Is your daily life running smoothly? Apart from these two, however many fancy concepts you learn are only dregs.
  2. Practise the basic skill of a "level heart" for five minutes every day. Sit upright and close your eyes; there is no need to seek any mysterious response. First rest your attention very lightly around the lower abdomen (the center that has form, neither touching nor leaving), while sensing the still surface of water before the emotions have begun to surge (the formless center). A heart that raises no waves is the best cultivation.
  3. Move from "effortful" to "natural." When you first begin calming the mind and restraining emotions, it is perfectly normal for it to feel a bit "deliberate and laborious" (the effortful center). Don't give up because you feel you are not natural enough; keep at it long enough and deliberateness will turn into instinct (the natural center).
  4. Get used to not being understood, and live out "wearing coarse cloth, holding jade within." People who live earnestly and cultivate themselves steadily often dislike crowds and are in no hurry to prove themselves to the whole world. "The rarer those who know me, the more precious I am"—saving your energy to nourish the jade within you is far more solid than running around courting applause.

Translator's Notes

  1. "It cannot be departed from for a single moment" and "the state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight arise is called the center": Both from the Book of Rites, Doctrine of the Mean, Chapter 1.
  2. "The good knowing that people know without learning," "the good capacity they possess without learning," "the Way is like a great road," "to turn back upon oneself and find oneself sincere—there is no greater joy," "the Way is near, yet they seek it far away; the task is easy, yet they seek it in the difficult": All from the Mencius (found variously in "Jin Xin I," "Gaozi II" and "Li Lou I").
  3. "Sincerely hold fast to the center": From the Book of Documents, "Counsels of Great Yu": "The human heart is precarious, the heart of the Way is subtle; be refined, be one, sincerely hold fast to the center." Song–Ming Neo-Confucianism, and Huang Yuanji with it, regarded this as the master outline of the heart-transmission in which Confucianism and Daoism meet.
  4. "King Wen's 'purity and oneness,' Zeng Shen's 'vigilance in solitude,' Meng Ke's 'good knowing'": Referring respectively to King Wen of Zhou's virtue, "pure and unceasing" (Doctrine of the Mean); the "one must be vigilant in solitude" of the Great Learning transmitted by Zeng Shen; and the "good knowing and good capacity" advocated by Meng Ke (Mencius).
  5. "Luo Congyan taught Li Yanping 'to contemplate, in stillness, the state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight arise'": Luo Congyan (1072–1135) was known as Master Yuzhang; Li Tong (1093–1163), known as Master Yanping, was the teacher of Zhu Xi. Their transmission of "observing in stillness the state before joy, anger, sorrow and delight arise" is one of the most representative methods of quiet-sitting self-cultivation in Song–Ming Neo-Confucianism.
  6. "Attain the One, and the ten thousand affairs are done" and "my Way is threaded through by one": The former comes from the Zhuangzi, "Heaven and Earth": "Penetrate the One, and the ten thousand affairs are done" (often adapted in the alchemical classics as "attain the One, and the ten thousand affairs are done"); the latter is Confucius's saying in the Analects, "Li Ren."
  7. Textual note: The base edition's chapter title reads "第七十章 一 被褐怀玉," which has been standardized as "第七十章 · 被褐怀玉." In the classic text, "太上曰" was misprinted with 日, 则我贵矣 as 则我费矣, and 被褐怀玉 as 被揭怀玉. In the original commentary, the base edition dropped the character 臾 from 不可须臾离也; 况吾之所言 was misprinted as 祝吾; 累千累万，盈箧溢籍 as 累干累万，盈箧盗籍; 不学而知 as 不学面知; the phrase "则我之贵乎我者，仍自若也" was wrongly punctuated in the base edition as "则我之贵乎？我者仍自若也"; 辞章记诵 was misprinted as 肆章; 尧舜 as 免舜; the 轲 of 轲之『良知』 as 柯; the quotation mark after 有形之『中』 was reversed; and 喜怒哀乐 was misprinted as 喜怒衰乐. All have been corrected one by one.
