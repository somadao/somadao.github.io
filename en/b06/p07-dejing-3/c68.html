Chapter 68 · The Virtue of Not Contending

The good warrior is not angry, the good victor does not contend, the good employer of people places himself beneath them; so too, advancing the fire in elixir refining must move with the momentum of things—concentrating Spirit without concentrating, regulating the breath without regulating—and perfect the virtue of not contending in humble, gentle compliance

About This Chapter

Chapter 68 uses the art of war and the art of employing people to peel back, layer by layer, the deep meaning of "the virtue of not contending" and "the power of employing others." Huang Yuanji first draws on the Book of Changes, the Eastern Han "Great Tree General" Feng Yi, Zhuge Liang and other allusions to explain thoroughly the sage's magnanimity in "using force without being forceful, humbling himself beneath others," and then turns it seamlessly into the core method of inner alchemy, "advancing the fire when the Medicine arises":

  1. The bearing of a great general: no show of force, no anger, no contending for merit (§2): One who truly understands warfare never relies on hot blood to act fiercely, and still less grabs credit after a victory. Open-minded as an empty valley, he is skilled at borrowing the talents and wisdom of the many for his own use; so he can be "awe-inspiring without anger," complying with the people's hearts below and according with the Way of Heaven above.
  2. Fierce cooking and rapid refining—yet "using force without force" (§3): When the true yang stirs in inner alchemy, although one speaks of the martial fire of "fierce cooking and rapid refining," that means assisting along the momentum of the true Breath as it naturally grows strong; it is by no means forcing and pressing hard out of one's own impatience and competitiveness. Only by acting in accord with the momentum is there no danger of "spilling the elixir and overturning the cauldron."
  3. Concentrating Spirit without concentrating, regulating the breath without regulating (§3): When the work has become fully mature, even the straining mind that says "I am overcoming myself and curbing desire, I am striving to accomplish the work" must be laid down. Let the Breath-mechanism move and rest of itself, and cooperate with its circulation humbly, harmoniously, gently and compliantly—this is "the virtue of not contending" and "the power of employing others" in inner alchemy.

Safety note: §3 of this chapter mentions traditional alchemical metaphors such as "advancing the fire when the Medicine arises," "fierce cooking and rapid refining," and "the ordinary bones transformed into jade bones," yet what it emphasizes is precisely "moving according to the time, proceeding with the momentum, not being fond of forcing." In everyday quiet sitting and breath regulation, never blindly imitate a so-called "fierce refining with martial fire" by holding the breath with effort, forcibly pumping the breath, or pressing hard for sensations of qi, lest you bring on discomforts such as dizziness, chest tightness, rebellious qi and feverish heat. Always make softness, smoothness, ease and naturalness the first principle.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：善为士者不武，善战者不怒，善胜敌者不争，善用人者为之下。是谓不争之德，是谓用人之力，是谓配天，古之极。

Lord Lao says: A commander skilled at leading troops does not rely on hot blood to show courage and fight fiercely; one skilled at battle does not charge blindly on a moment's anger; one skilled at defeating the enemy does not clash head-on to contend for supremacy; one skilled at employing people is willing to bow humbly before them.

This is called "the virtue of not contending"; this is called "being good at using the strength of others"; this is called being matched with the Way of Heaven—this is the highest standard for conducting oneself and governing the world since antiquity.

§2
Original (Chinese)

士，士师也。士师用兵，原是尚武。《易》曰：「刚中而应，行险而顺」，「神武而不杀」。是用武不武，士之善为士者。及大敌交锋，两军对垒，不得不陈师鞠旅，称干比戈，势奔山河，声震雷电，然究其心，只诛无道，非有恶于人也，虽战而无战，是为善战。纵师徒他出，士卒无多，而强敌忽然压境，不难弹琴退中原之寇，和曲解敌国之围，所谓「不怒而威于鈇钺」者是。迨至班师振旅，奏凯言旋，人皆盈廷奏绩，而彼独逊谢不前，所谓「大树将军」者，可以无愧矣。即或上赏频加，而反躬常觉赧颜，此善胜敌者所由不争也。《书》曰：「汝惟不争，天下莫与汝争能」，其斯之谓欤？若此者，皆由推诚布公，集思广益，不自恃其才，善用众人之才以为才，不自矜其智，善用众人之智以为智，所谓「卑以下人」者此也。倘非察纳雅言，咨诹善道，虚怀若谷，谦尊而光，乌有此善战善胜之能王天下犹反掌耶？是皆无争之德，有以服民心也；是皆用人之力，有以威天下也；是皆下顺民心，上合天道，与天地参，而立万古之人极也。噫！非圣人至诚尽性，焉能于干戈扰攘之际，隐然寓太平揖让之风，用武不武，行怒不怒，相争不争如此乎？又况宽以御众，虑以下人，贤者在位，能者在职，天下之士，皆效忠抒悃，而愿赴功趋事，舍生奉命于其间，一如天道不言，四时流行，万物献瑞，此所以配天地而立极也。《诗》曰：「思文后稷，克配彼天，莫匪尔极。」微斯人，其谁与归？

The "shi" here refers to the general who commands the troops (the shishi). Leading troops in war originally prizes martial force, but the Book of Changes puts it well: "Firm and central, it meets with response; it walks a dangerous path yet in compliance," and also "Divinely martial, yet without killing." To be able to use martial force without wantonly flaunting martial might—this is the general who is best at leading troops ("a good leader of men is not martial").

When it truly comes to crossing swords with a great enemy and the two armies face each other, he cannot avoid marshalling his troops and swearing them in, raising shields and halberds; the great army surges like mountains and rivers pouring down, and its war cries shake like thunder and lightning. Yet look deeply into the commander's heart: he only punishes the tyrannical and lawless, and by no means acts out of personal resentment or hatred of others. Though he is fighting, his heart bears no warlike hostility at all—this is "one good at battle is not angry." Even if his main forces are deployed far away and he has few soldiers at hand, and a strong enemy suddenly arrives beneath the city walls, he can be calm and composed enough to repel the enemy by playing the zither and lift the siege by playing a tune—this is what the Doctrine of the Mean calls "without anger, yet more awe-inspiring than the axe and halberd."

When he returns victorious and leads his ranks back to court with songs of triumph, all the civil and military officials crowd the hall vying to report their merits, while he alone quietly yields and declines, never pushing forward—just like that Eastern Han "Great Tree General," Feng Yi, who whenever merits were discussed would withdraw alone beneath a great tree; truly he had nothing to be ashamed of! Even if the sovereign repeatedly bestows rich rewards, when he examines himself he often still feels ashamed and uneasy. This is the portrait of "one good at defeating the enemy never contends for merit." The Book of Documents says: "Precisely because you do not contend, no one in the world contends with you in ability"—is this not speaking of exactly this realm?

The reason he can do all this lies entirely in opening his heart, being impartial and selfless, and gathering the ideas of many to broaden the benefit: he does not act arbitrarily on the strength of his own bit of ability, but is good at gathering the talents of the many into his own talent; he does not show off his own bit of cleverness, but is good at blending the wisdom of the many into his own wisdom—this is "humbly placing oneself beneath others" ("one good at employing people places himself beneath them"). Had he not humbly listened to loyal and upright counsel, widely sought good policies for governing the state, with a breast as broad as an empty valley—the humbler, the more honoured and radiant—how could he possess such skill at fighting and winning that bringing peace to the state and stability to the realm is as easy as turning over his hand?

All of this stems from "the virtue of not contending," which wins the hearts of the people of the world; from "being good at using the strength of others," which establishes a prestige that awes the world; from complying with people's hearts below and according with the Way of Heaven above, forming a triad with heaven and earth and establishing a human ultimate that endures for ten thousand ages! Ah, were it not for the sage's utmost sincerity and fulfilment of his nature, how could he, amid the turmoil of swords and spears on the battlefield, still radiate the courteous, composed bearing of an age of great peace—using force without flaunting force, wielding awe without anger, defeating the enemy without contending for merit, to such a degree?

Moreover, he treats people generously and is everywhere careful to humble himself; he lets the worthy occupy key positions and the capable each manage their duties. Of the aspiring men of the world, there are none who do not wish to give their utmost loyalty and pour out their sincerity, vying to go forth and render service, even to sacrifice their lives in obedience—just as Heaven utters not a word, yet the four seasons turn as always and all things vie to display auspicious signs. This is why he can be matched with heaven and earth and establish the human ultimate. The Classic of Poetry praises: "O accomplished Hou Ji, you are worthy to be matched with yonder Heaven; there is none of the people who do not rely on the ultimate you established." Without such a person, to whom could the hearts of the world turn?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此言药生进火，虽有猛烹急炼法工，然亦因时为动，顺势而行，用武无武，所以无倾丹倒鼎之患也。纵气机之动、真阳之生，至大至刚，充塞乎两大，何异战者之赫然震怒，所向披靡？况采取进火，只因其气之浩然者而扩充之，非好为强也，故一经洗炼，而凡骨化为玉骨，凡身化作金身，所谓「一战而天下平」，无非因民之怒，而己无与焉，所以取金丹于反掌，犹取天下如拾芥也。惟其神凝无凝，息调无调，纯任乎天，不杂以人，虽天人交争、理欲迭起，不得不存理以遏欲、尽人而合天，迨至学粹功深，义精仁熟，毫无胜私克己、争功争能之心，仁者所以无敌于天下也。若是者，皆由谦和柔顺，虚己下人，一听气机之动静，而与之为转移。故丹之成也，有不见而章，不动而变，无为而成者焉，何殊善用人者为之下乎？修炼之道，果能在在安和，时时柔顺，欲不用遏而自遏，理不用存而自存，是谓不争之德也。且以不争之心，顺理以施，随机而运，犹用人之力，以成一己之功，是能范围天地之化而不过也。孔子曰：「天何言哉？四时行焉，百物生焉。」圣人与道合真，正不啻天经地纬，而立万世之人极也。

Brought down to actual inner-alchemical practice, this chapter says the following: when the true Medicine of Prior Heaven arises in the body and the fire must be advanced to cook and refine it, although the elixir method does include the martial-fire work of "fierce cooking and rapid refining," that too is entirely a matter of moving according to the time and proceeding with the momentum. It seems to use the martial fire, yet the heart bears no trace of forcing or impatience ("using force without force"); therefore it will never provoke the calamity of "spilling the elixir and overturning the cauldron."

Even when the Breath-mechanism in the body bursts forth and the true yang arises, that momentum is supremely great and supremely firm, filling the space between heaven and earth—it looks just like a fierce warrior on the battlefield flaring into rage and sweeping all before him. But see clearly: when the alchemist gathers and advances the fire, he only follows that naturally arising, flood-like upright Breath and expands it along its own momentum; it is by no means artificially playing the hero and forcibly pumping up the breath. Therefore, after a course of pure cleansing and refining, the turbid breaths are transformed and cleared, as if the ordinary womb-born, vulgar bones had been refined into clear, translucent jade bones, and the ordinary mortal body transformed into an adamantine Dharma body. This is just like the historical "one battle and the world was pacified," which was nothing but following the people's natural righteous indignation, without the commander adding the slightest personal anger of his own. Therefore accomplishing the Golden Elixir is as easy as turning over one's hand, just as winning the world is as effortless as bending down to pick up a blade of grass.

Its mystery lies entirely in this: Spirit, though concentrated, bears no clinging trace of "I am concentrating Spirit with all my might"; the breath, though harmonized (regulating the breath), bears no contrived effort of "I am deliberately regulating the breath." Everything is left purely to nature, unmixed with the selfish intentions of Posterior Heaven.

Of course, in the early stage of refining the self, heavenly principle and human desire inevitably clash, and reason and selfish desire take turns surfacing; at that time one has no choice but to deliberately restrain selfish desire and preserve heavenly principle, exhausting human effort to accord with the Way of Heaven. But once learning has become pure, the work deep, righteousness refined and benevolence mature, there is simply no longer any tense thought in the heart of "I must forcibly overcome selfish desire" or "I must contend for merit and compete in ability"—this is precisely the transformed realm of "the benevolent have no enemies in the world."

The reason one can reach this realm lies entirely in a heart that is humble, harmonious, gentle and compliant, empty of self and placed beneath others, which leaves everything to the natural movement and rest of the prior-heaven Breath-mechanism in the body and shifts along with its rhythm. Therefore, when the Great Elixir is accomplished, it is as the Doctrine of the Mean describes: "Without display it shines forth; without movement it transforms; without action it is accomplished." How is this any different from the scripture's "one good at employing people willingly places himself beneath them"?

In the Way of cultivation and refinement, if one can truly be calm and peaceful everywhere and gentle and unhurried at all times, then selfish desire is extinguished without being forcibly suppressed, and heavenly principle constantly endures without being rigidly guarded—this is called "the virtue of not contending." Moreover, with this heart of not contending, acting in accord with heavenly principle and circulating along with the Breath-mechanism is like being good at using the strength of others to accomplish one's own great work; it is enough to encompass the transformations of heaven and earth without the slightest deviation. Confucius exclaimed: "What does Heaven say? Yet the four seasons run their course and the hundred things are born." When the sage has cultivated to the point of uniting with the Great Way in truth, he is as natural and constant as the warp of heaven and the weft of earth, firmly establishing a human ultimate that will not shift for ten thousand generations!

Key Terms

  • The virtue of not contending: The highest cultivation praised by Laozi. Outwardly it shows as not flaunting force, not growing angry, and not grabbing credit; applied to inner cultivation, it means a calm, harmonious, gentle and compliant state of mind that neither wrestles hard with selfish thoughts nor strains against the Breath-mechanism, so as to reach "desire is curbed without curbing, principle is preserved without preserving."
  • Using force without force: A key point of inner-alchemical fire phasing. Even when the Breath-mechanism is abundant and "fierce cooking and rapid refining" is needed, one must follow the natural momentum, with the heart unhurried and the intention not forcing; only then is there no worry of "spilling the elixir and overturning the cauldron."
  • Concentrating Spirit without concentrating, regulating the breath without regulating: The natural state reached when regulating the breath and concentrating Spirit have become fully mature—Spirit is settled yet without any sense of deliberately grasping, the breath is even yet without any sense of artificial control, left purely to the natural rhythm.
  • Encompassing the transformations of heaven and earth without excess: From the Book of Changes, "Appended Statements I"; the sage acts in accord with natural laws and is in perfect step with the transformations of heaven and earth, neither falling short nor going too far.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Before you lose your temper, check your motive: are you solving a problem or venting personal resentment? "One good at battle is not angry." Matters of principle should be upheld just the same, but once emotion takes over and it becomes venting and proving a point, judgment instantly goes offline, leaving an opening for the other side.
  2. The greatest skill in a team is "lifting others up." "One good at employing people places himself beneath them." A truly excellent leader does not steal the spotlight from subordinates or show off as the cleverest in everything; give the credit and the stage to those who do the work, and everyone will naturally pull together.
  3. In practice and self-discipline alike, do not "fight with yourself." In the early stage, discipline is inevitably needed ("preserving principle and curbing desire"), but if self-discipline is turned long-term into a chore full of guilt and confrontation, it will rebound sooner or later. The sooner you move into a calm, smooth daily rhythm ("concentrating Spirit without concentrating, regulating the breath without regulating"), the longer it will last.
  4. The smoother things go, the more you should learn from the "Great Tree General." When you achieve results, step back half a pace: it spares you envy and the flattery that ruins, and keeps steady the rarest thing within—clear-headedness.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Firm and central, it meets with response; it walks a dangerous path yet in compliance" and "Divinely martial, yet without killing": From the Book of Changes, the Judgment commentary on the hexagram Shi (The Army), and "Appended Statements I," respectively.
  2. "Repelling the invaders of the Central Plains by playing the zither, lifting the siege of an enemy state with a tune": A general allusion to famous generals of antiquity who calmly repelled the enemy (such as the widely told popular and fictional story of Zhuge Liang playing the zither on the walls of an empty city to repel Sima Yi), used as an image of composure in danger and awe without anger.
  3. "Without anger, yet more awe-inspiring than the axe and halberd": From the Book of Rites, Doctrine of the Mean: "Thus the noble person is revered without moving, trusted without speaking… without rewarding, the people are encouraged; without anger, the people are more in awe than of the axe and halberd." 鈇钺 is read fū yuè—the axes and halberds used in ancient times for executions.
  4. "Great Tree General": From the Book of the Later Han, "Biography of Feng Yi." Feng Yi, a famous general of the founding of the Eastern Han, was modest and never boasted; whenever the army halted and the generals sat together discussing their merits, Feng Yi alone withdrew beneath a great tree, and the army called him the "Great Tree General."
  5. "Precisely because you do not contend, no one in the world contends with you in ability": Adapted from the Book of Documents, "Counsels of Great Yu": "Precisely because you do not boast, no one in the world contends with you in ability; precisely because you do not vaunt yourself, no one in the world contends with you in merit."
  6. "Examining and accepting refined counsel, consulting on the good way" and "thinking to place oneself beneath others": The first two phrases come from Zhuge Liang's "Memorial on Dispatching the Troops" (Three Kingdoms); "thinking to place oneself beneath others" comes from the Analects, "Yan Yuan": "Plain and upright and fond of righteousness, examining words and observing countenances, thinking to place oneself beneath others," meaning that one's mind constantly dwells on treating people with modest respect and placing oneself beneath them.
  7. "O accomplished Hou Ji, you are worthy to be matched with yonder Heaven; there is none who do not rely on your ultimate": From the Classic of Poetry, "Hymns of Zhou," Si Wen, praising the virtue of Hou Ji, the first ancestor of the Zhou, who taught the people agriculture—a virtue worthy of being matched with Heaven, establishing the central and upright ultimate.
  8. "Without display it shines forth; without movement it transforms; without action it is accomplished": From the Book of Rites, Doctrine of the Mean, Chapter 26.
  9. Textual note: In the original commentary, the 士 of 士师也, 士师用兵, 士之善为士者 and 士卒无多 was misprinted throughout the base edition as 土; "《易》曰," "《书》曰" and "《诗》曰" were misprinted with 日; 鈇钺 as 铁钺; 盈廷奏绩 as 盈延; 凡骨化为玉骨 as 凡肯化为玉胥; 克己 and 一己 as 已; and the 遏 of 欲不用遏而自遏 as 過. All have been corrected one by one.
