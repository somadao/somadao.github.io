Chapter 66 · King of the Hundred Valleys

Rivers and seas become king of the hundred valleys because they excel at staying low; so too the elixir method must lower the heart-fire into the lower Cinnabar Field, letting Spirit be the guest and Breath the host, and moving humbly and compliantly so that the elixir is never spilled nor the cauldron overturned

About This Chapter

Chapter 66 is famous for its line "Rivers and seas excel at staying low, and so they can be king of the hundred valleys." Huang Yuanji first explains thoroughly why the sage's humble virtue—"placing himself below them in his words, putting himself behind them in his person"—naturally wins people's hearts. Then he turns his brush and maps "rivers and seas and the hundred valleys," "the sage and the myriad people" onto the great method of rising and descending between the heart-spirit and the lower Cinnabar Field within the body:

  1. Dwelling below and staying behind wins the whole world's allegiance (§2): The harder you try to dominate others and push ahead of them, the less willing they are to submit. One who sits high without arrogance, consults humbly and yields in everything neither makes people feel oppressed ("he dwells above, yet the people feel no weight") nor blocks their way forward ("he stands in front, yet the people feel no harm"); the world then gladly and willingly raises him up and supports him.
  2. The heart as rivers and seas: lowering the spirit-fire into the lower Cinnabar Field (§3): Within the body, the heart is the most honored, like the rivers and seas or the sage; the lower Cinnabar Field and the hundred members lie below, like the hundred valleys or the myriad people. Ordinarily stray thoughts fly about and the original Breath of Prior Heaven is scattered throughout the body. Only when you set down all entanglements, lower the curtains and close the mouth, and concentrate Spirit and enter the Cavity of Breath—gently lowering the heart-fire perched on high into the lower Cinnabar Field—will the Breath of the whole body gather of itself, as a hundred rivers return to the sea.
  3. The fire-phasing secret of "Let him be host, and I the guest" (§3): Ordinary people let the posterior heart-spirit run the household all their lives, dragging the breath about at random. The accomplished person does exactly the opposite: while contemplating, neither clinging nor letting go, as if present and as if absent; and when the Breath in the Cinnabar Field begins to stir of itself, he willingly withdraws to the guest's seat, lets Breath be the host, and humbly follows its movement. Only thus does he avoid forcing it in haste and incurring the deviation of "spilling the elixir and overturning the cauldron, mercury running off and lead flying away."

Safety note: §3 of this chapter involves "lowering the curtains and closing the mouth, looking at the nose with the eyes, aligning the nose with the navel, lowering the heart-fire into the Cinnabar Field," together with the traditional inner-alchemy fire-phasing warning that "if the fire is too fierce it scorches the essence and blood and chokes the spiritual workings." When beginners practise sitting still and observing the breath, they must never stare rigidly at the tip of the nose or forcibly press or hold the breath down into the lower Cinnabar Field; an over-tight intention can easily bring on dizziness and swollen eyes, tightness in the chest and abdomen, a dry mouth and feverish heat, or agitation and insomnia. Always keep the relaxed feeling of "neither clinging nor letting go, as if present and as if absent"; at the first sense of strain, relax, get up and move about.

Original Text and Translation

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：江海所以能为百谷王者，以其善下之，故能为百谷王。是以圣人欲上人，必以言下之；欲先人，必以身后之。是以圣人处上而人不重，处前而人不害，是以天下乐推而不厌。以其不争，故天下莫能与之争。

Lord Lao says: The reason rivers and seas can be king of the hundred valleys is that they excel at lying below all the streams; so all waters rush toward them and gather there, and that is how they become king of the hundred valleys.

Therefore, when the sage wishes to stand above the people, in his words he first humbly lowers himself; when he wishes to go before the people, in his conduct he first sets himself behind them. Precisely because of this, though the sage dwells in a high position, the people do not feel weighed down or oppressed; though he walks in front, people do not feel that he obstructs them. So the whole world gladly raises him up and supports him and never tires of him. Because he never contends with anyone, no one in the world is able to contend with him.

§2
Original (Chinese)

夫人莫不欲人之服己也，乃有不欲服而人服，益欲服而人愈不服者，无他，以其自高自大，而不肯低其心、下其气也。试观江海为百谷之所归往者，以其能下之故，所以为百谷王。设江海如百谷之自处于上，百谷虽有归往之势，奈彼无容受何？是以圣人早见及此，欲上人，必以言下之，如尧之咨于四岳，舜之询于四门，举凡教条号令，事事访于臣邻，而不自高其智，此所以愈下而人愈上也。欲先人，必以身后之，如禹、皋、伊、旦，虽属先知先觉，而在在让人以先，自处于后，此所以愈后而人愈先也。惟其自处于下与后，虽居帝王之位，而无震慑之威，所以不重也；掌神灵之统，而无凌厉之气，所以不害也。故天下乐推而为先，绝无厌恶之心焉。《诗》曰：「在彼无恶，在此无斁。庶几夙夜，以永终誉。」此岂有他哉？以其不争人上，不争人先，而人自上之、先之，服教畏神，沐恩戴德之不已，又安忍争上争先，而与圣人角胜竞长也哉？

There is no one in the world who does not want others to admire and submit to him. Yet some people never even think of making others submit, and others genuinely submit to them from the heart; while some, the more they want others to submit, the less others are willing to. The reason is nothing else: they are self-important and puffed up, and unwilling to lower their hearts and soften their bearing.

Look at the great rivers and seas: the reason they become the place where countless valley streams finally gather is simply that they lie low and excel at staying beneath, and so they can be king of the hundred valleys. Suppose the rivers and seas, like the mountain streams, held themselves on high—even if the hundred streams had a tendency to flow toward them, the ground would be too high to hold them and they could not receive them. How then could the water flow in?

The sage saw through this principle long ago. When he wants to guide the people and stand above them, his words must first be modest and inquiring—as Emperor Yao consulted the chief officials of the four quarters (the Four Peaks) on every matter, and Emperor Shun opened the four gates wide to hear opinions from all under heaven; in all matters of decrees and moral instruction, they consulted their ministers and neighbours on each and every point and never boasted of their own cleverness. As a result, the lower his posture, the more the people raised him up to the position above them. When he wants to lead the people and walk before them, in his conduct he must first withdraw behind them—like Yu the Great, Gao Yao, Yi Yin and the Duke of Zhou, those worthy ministers of antiquity: though each was a sage who knew first and awoke first, they at every turn courteously let others walk ahead while willingly staying behind. As a result, the further back they stepped, the more everyone honoured them and pushed them to the front.

Precisely because the sage always willingly dwells downstream and in the rear, even when he ascends the throne of emperor and king he carries none of that trembling, overawing pressure, and so the people do not feel burdened ("he dwells above, yet the people feel no weight"); even when he holds the great mandate of Heaven and the spirits, he acts without any aggressive, overbearing force, and so people do not feel he blocks their way or harms them ("he stands in front, yet the people feel no harm"). Thus the whole world gladly and sincerely raises him up as their leader, without the slightest thought of weariness or resistance. The Classic of Poetry praises: "There, no one dislikes him; here, no one tires of him. Diligent from dawn to dusk, his good name will last forever."

Is there any other hidden secret in this? It all comes from his not contending with others over who is higher and not competing over who goes first, so that others naturally place him above and push him to the front. They are heartily won over by his teaching and grateful for his kindness, and can hardly get enough of it—how could they bear to contend with him for the higher place or the first place, and measure their strength against the sage?

§3
Original (Chinese)

此喻炼丹之学，始以神火下入丹田，然后火蒸水沸，水底金生，长生之药，始得而有。夫人受天地之中以生，原是完完全全；自有生后，气质拘之，物欲蔽之，所得于天之元气，悉散漫于一身尸气之间，不能会萃一区者久矣。今欲攒簇五行，和合四象，会于中宫，归于玄窍，其必万缘放下，一私不起，垂帘塞兑，以目视鼻，由鼻对脐，降心火于丹田，不过片晌工夫，即见玄关窍开，一阳来复，周身之气，自然齐集丹田，融融泄泄，乐不可名。但观照之初，火不紧则金不出矿，火太猛则又烧灼精血、窒塞灵机。惟有不粘不脱，若有若无，而下丹田之气，自跃跃欲动。此犹江海之能下百谷，百谷所以归往，圣人能下天下，天下所以归心。夫人一身，心为至大至贵，百体皆小焉贱焉者耳。太上故以江海之大、圣人之贵，喻心；百谷之小、万民之贱，喻百体、喻下田。修道者，亦当以下为本，以贱为基，而不自处于高于贵，庶低下于人，所成自易。若论凡人，原以神为主，气则随之动静，所以生男育女而有生有死。至人则以气为主，而神则听之转移，《悟真》云「饶他为主我为宾」是。大修行人，于气机之动，逆施造化，颠倒乾坤，一听其上下往来，归炉封固，再候真信，循环运转，全不以神为主持，但观真气之冲和，逆施倒行，功成九转，丹熟珠灵，岂不高高乎在上、赫赫乎居先，而为万夫之仰、天下之观者耶？惟其处下居后如此，则一片恬澹之志、谦和之心，所以无倾丹倒鼎、汞走铅飞之害，故处上而人不重，居后而人不害，以其不争，故天下莫能与之争也。

Brought down to actual inner-alchemical practice, this chapter is a metaphor for exactly the following: at the very start of refining the elixir, the fire of Spirit that hangs high above must first be gently lowered into the lower Cinnabar Field; then the heart-fire warmly steams the kidney-water, the water grows warm and the Breath melts, and the true metal at the bottom of the water (the true yang of Prior Heaven) can emerge—only then does the great Medicine of long life have a foothold.

When a person is born, receiving the central harmonious Breath of heaven and earth, he is originally whole and complete, pure and without lack. But from the time he drops to the ground and grows up, he is first confined by the temperament and habits of Posterior Heaven, and then veiled layer upon layer by desires for external things. The original Breath he received from Heaven long ago scattered and drifted among the turbid ordinary breaths of the body, and for a long time it has been unable to gather into one mass. Now, if you wish to draw the scattered Breaths of the five phases back together ("assembling the five phases") and to unite the four images into one, gathering them all back into the central palace and collecting them into the Mysterious Pass, you must first set down every worldly entanglement and let not a single selfish thought arise; gently lower the curtains, close the mouth and do not speak ("blocking the openings"), let your gaze lightly rest on the tip of the nose, then follow its line inward toward the navel, and slowly settle the heart-fire that floats above into the lower Cinnabar Field (concentrating Spirit and entering the Cavity of Breath).

After no more than a few moments of quiet effort, you will feel the wondrous opening of the Mysterious Pass open of itself, and the single true yang Breath quietly return and arise; the scattered breaths of the whole body naturally gather together in the Cinnabar Field, warm and melting, utterly at ease—a joy that words simply cannot describe.

But in the earliest stage of contemplation, the measure of fire phasing is most severely tested. If the intention is too lax and the fire not tight enough, the true Breath will not gather (just as when the furnace fire is not hot enough, the gold cannot be smelted out of the ore); but if the intention fixes too rigidly and the fire is too fierce, it will instead produce dryness and heat that injure the yin, and stifle and block the lively vital workings ("scorching the essence and blood, choking the spiritual workings"). Only by keeping a soft, focused attention that is "neither clinging nor letting go, as if present and as if absent"—neither stuck fast in one spot nor scattered and detached—will the true Breath in the lower Cinnabar Field stir vigorously of its own accord. This is just as the rivers and seas are willing to bend low beneath the hundred valleys, and so the waters of the hundred valleys surge in to return to them; and the sage is willing to humble himself beneath all under heaven, and so the hearts of all under heaven gladly turn to him.

Within this single body, the heart-spirit is the greatest and most honoured; the four limbs, the hundred members and the lower Cinnabar Field all occupy a humble place downstream. Lord Lao uses the vastness of the rivers and seas and the honour of the sage as metaphors for the "heart," and the smallness of the hundred valleys and the lowliness of the myriad people as metaphors for the "hundred members" and the "lower Field." Those who cultivate the Way should likewise take "willingly dwelling below, taking the lowly as one's foundation" as the root, and must never let the heart-spirit sit high and aloof in arrogance. If you can let the heart-spirit come down humbly, gently and compliantly, the work will naturally be easy to accomplish.

At this point, the fundamental watershed between ordinary people and those who have attained the Way becomes perfectly clear. Ordinary people all their lives let the posterior heart-spirit (the cognitive spirit) be the boss; the Breath in the body runs after joy, anger, thought and worry, flowing forward and dissipating, and so they beget sons and raise daughters and walk the old road of birth, ageing, sickness and death. The accomplished person is exactly the opposite: he hands the leading role to the true Breath of Prior Heaven and lets the heart-spirit step back and follow the rhythm of the true Breath. Zhang Boduan's Awakening to Reality says, "Let him be host, and I the guest"—and this is exactly that secret!

Whenever the Breath-mechanism in the body stirs of itself, the great practitioner follows that vital impulse to reverse creation and invert Qian and Kun (reversal), letting the true Breath come and go above and below entirely of its own accord, returning it to the furnace to be warmly nourished and sealed, and then quietly waiting for the signal of the next stirring of true Breath (the "true tidings"), circulating round and round in endless cycles. Throughout, he never lets the posterior heart-spirit overstep its role and forcibly manipulate things; he only quietly contemplates the true Breath flowing in harmony, moving in reverse and returning to the source. When the ninefold cycle is completed, the Golden Elixir is fully ripe and the spiritual pearl is round and bright—tell me, is such an achievement not precisely to stand nobly high above others, to walk gloriously in front of them, revered by the multitudes and watched by all the world?

It is entirely because he keeps this attitude of dwelling below and staying behind—his breast tranquil and serene, humble and gentle—that he does not, through haste for quick results and forcing the fire, bring on the calamity of "spilling the elixir and overturning the cauldron, lead and mercury flying apart." This is the deep alchemical meaning of the scripture's words: "He dwells above, yet the people feel no weight; he stays behind, yet the people feel no harm. Because he does not contend, no one in the world can contend with him."

Key Terms

  • Concentrating Spirit and entering the Cavity of Breath (lowering the heart-fire into the Cinnabar Field): Gently lowering the attention and heart-spirit, which ordinarily hang high in the head and race outward, and settling them in the lower Cinnabar Field, so that the heart-fire warms and nourishes the kidney-water and Spirit and Breath unite.
  • Lowering the curtains and blocking the openings: Lowering the curtains means closing the eyes slightly, leaving a thread of faint light, so as to withdraw the gaze; "blocking the openings" comes from Chapter 52 of the Daodejing and means closing the mouth, speaking little, and gathering in the spirit that leaks outward.
  • Neither clinging nor letting go, as if present and as if absent: The golden measure of fire phasing in inner-alchemical contemplation—neither gripping tight and guarding rigidly (clinging, which easily causes dryness, heat and oppression) nor being careless and losing awareness (letting go, which easily causes distraction and torpor).
  • "Let him be host, and I the guest": From the Northern Song master Zhang Boduan's Awakening to Reality; in quiet practice, the natural rhythm of the true Breath of Prior Heaven takes the lead, while the heart-spirit withdraws to the guest's seat, quietly observing and complying, rather than letting the cognitive spirit forcibly direct the Breath-mechanism.
  • Spilling the elixir and overturning the cauldron; mercury running off and lead flying away: A warning phrase of the alchemists, describing how, through impatience, overly fierce fire phasing, or forcing and rash movement, the Essence, Breath and Spirit that had been gathered (lead and mercury) are dissipated and lost in an instant.

Putting It into Practice

  1. In communication, "lower your stance" first, and your influence will actually grow. Whether leading a team or discussing matters at home, first listen to what others say and ask once more, "What do you think?" ("placing oneself below in words"). People who do not feel pressured are more ready to accept your proposals.
  2. Bring your attention down from the "head" back to the "belly." Modern anxiety and insomnia often come from a "heart-fire hanging high"—all one's energy crammed into the brain, spinning nonstop. Before sleep or when irritable, gently close your eyes, loosen your shoulders, and let your breathing and attention settle softly into the lower abdomen; within a few minutes you can feel the heart's energy sinking and your whole self relaxing.
  3. In work and in regulating the breath alike, learn to "be the guest." The more you try to tightly control your sleep, your breathing or other people, the more likely things are to backfire. Learn to be "neither clinging nor letting go": create a stable, settled setting, and leave the rest to the body's own rhythm and the natural course of things.
  4. Push the credit forward and keep your position back. People of real ability do not need to grab the spotlight to prove themselves; leave room for others and do not block their way, and you become the core everyone most wants to rely on.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Yao consulted the Four Peaks; Shun opened the four gates": From the Book of Documents, "Canon of Yao" and "Canon of Shun." The Four Peaks were the chief officials in charge of the feudal lords of the four quarters under Yao; the four gates refer to Shun opening the gates of the four directions to recruit worthy men widely and to stay in touch with the people's conditions.
  2. "Yu, Gao, Yi and Dan": The four great worthy ministers of high antiquity—Yu the Great; Gao Yao (judge and worthy aide under Shun and Yu); Yi Yin (the worthy chancellor of Tang of Shang); and Dan, the Duke of Zhou (the sage minister who served as regent in the early Zhou).
  3. "There, no one dislikes him; here, no one tires of him. Diligent from dawn to dusk, his good name will last forever": From the Classic of Poetry, "Hymns of Zhou," Zhenlu ("The Egrets in Flight"). 斁 is read yì and means to weary of or reject.
  4. "Assembling the five phases, uniting the four images": A traditional inner-alchemy expression meaning that, through emptiness, stillness and concentration of Spirit, the five-phase Breaths of the five viscera and the four images (wood, fire, metal and water / Essence, Breath, Spirit and Intention) are drawn together and harmonized in the earth position of the central palace, turning dispersion into gathering.
  5. "Let him be host, and I the guest": From a heptasyllabic quatrain in Zhang Boduan's Awakening to Reality (Northern Song): "The true transmission of this method is rarely heard of in the world; know that host and guest must not be confused… let him be host, and I the guest."
  6. Textual note: Between "而不自" and "高其智," the base edition mistakenly inserts two lines of a "Li Erqu" footnote belonging to Chapter 65; these have been moved back to the notes of Chapter 65 and the original text of this chapter rejoined. In the classic and the original commentary, "太上曰" and "《诗》曰" were misprinted with 日; 皋 was misprinted as 𦤑; 角胜竞长 as 竟长; 气质拘之 as 枸之; 以目视鼻 as 以月视鼻; 窒塞灵机 as 室塞灵机; 若论凡人 as 几人; 岂不高高乎 as 岜，不高高乎; and 汞走铅飞 as 永走铅飞. All have been corrected one by one.
