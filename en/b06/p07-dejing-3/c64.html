Chapter 64 · Without Contrivance, Without Grasping

Curbing desire and preserving sincerity are best done before anything sprouts; a nine-story tower rises from heaped earth. Only by neither forcing nor forgetting, and by being as careful at the end as at the beginning, can one nurture a full Golden Elixir from the first stirring of the Mysterious Pass

About This Chapter

Chapter 64 has left us timeless sayings such as "A tree that fills two arms grows from a tiny shoot; a nine-story tower rises from heaped earth; a journey of a thousand miles begins beneath one's feet" and "be as careful at the end as at the beginning." Huang Yuanji brings the chapter's "govern before disorder arises" and "accumulate the minute to become manifest" straight down into the whole course of actual practice, from curbing desire and refining the self to nurturing the elixir at the Mysterious Pass:

  1. Deal with desire before it sprouts (§2): When stray thoughts and selfish desires first poke their heads up, they are soft and fragile and can most easily be cut off with a single stroke; once you let them wind and tangle together, it takes enormous effort to clear them up. Gathering the heart while living quietly day to day, and guarding against the slightest beginnings before a thought even stirs, is the least strenuous gateway of refining the self.
  2. Accomplishing the Way cannot be rushed; the fire phasing must be natural (§2): Although refining the self can be taken up right now, accomplishing the Great Way cannot be hurried; it must accumulate day by day and month by month, just as a great tree grows from a tender shoot and a high tower is built up from a single basket of earth. And one must follow Mencius's fire phasing of "neither forcing nor forgetting": deliberate meddling is sure to fail ("those who act on it spoil it"), clinging tightly is sure to lose ("those who grasp it lose it"); only by following nature and remaining careful from start to finish will one avoid failing at the last step.
  3. The first stirring of the Mysterious Pass and the image of the waxing moon (§3): The instant when movement arises at the extreme of stillness is the first opening of the wondrous aperture of the Mysterious Pass. At this moment the source of the water is at its clearest; if one simply holds it lightly and does not let posterior-heaven human desire mix in, it will be like the thin sliver of new moon on the third day of the lunar month, gradually waxing to the half disc of the first quarter and finally to the full moon on the fifteenth—nurtured all the way from the tiniest seed of vitality to the ripening and perfection of the elixir.

Safety note: §3 of this chapter describes traditional advanced stages of internal alchemy such as "the first stirring of the Mysterious Pass," "advancing the fire and withdrawing the tally," "the River Chariot's transport," and "the shaking of the six roots." In everyday practice, never treat these ancient metaphors as bodily benchmarks that must be forcibly pursued, and above all never forcibly drive the breath up along the spine or deliberately seek extraordinary phenomena. Let relaxation and quiet of body and mind and natural breathing be your measure in sitting. If you experience a swollen feeling in the head, palpitations, chest tightness, a feverish restlessness, or emotional overexcitement, stop sitting at once, take a walk, and return to your normal daily routine.

§1Classic Text
Original (Chinese)

太上曰：其安易持，其未兆易谋，其脆易破，其微易散。为之于未有，治之于未乱。合抱之木，生于毫末；九层之台，起于累土；千里之行，始于足下。为者败之，执者失之。是以圣人无为故无败，无执故无失。民之从事，常于几成而败之。慎终如始，则无败事。是以圣人欲不欲，不贵难得之货；学不学，复众人之所过。以辅万物之自然，而不敢为。

The Most High Lord Lao says: When a situation is stable, it is easy to keep hold of; when a matter has not yet shown any sign, it is easy to plan for. When something is brittle, it is easy to break; when its beginnings are minute, they are easy to scatter. Take precautions before calamity has taken shape; set things in order before disorder has arisen.

A great tree as thick as two people's encircling arms grew from a tender shoot as fine as a hair; a nine-story tower was built up basket by basket of heaped earth; a journey of a thousand miles began with the first step beneath one's feet. Those who act with contrivance end up spoiling things; those who cling and will not let go end up losing them. Therefore the sage follows nature and does not act rashly, and so does not fail; does not cling or crave, and so does not lose.

When people undertake things, they often spoil them just when success is within reach. If one remains as careful and reverent at the end as at the beginning, there will be no failures. Therefore the sage takes "not giving rise to selfish desire" as his desire, and does not value rare and hard-to-obtain goods; takes "not pursuing outward cleverness" as his learning, using it to remedy the faults of the multitude, who love grand achievements and stray from the Great Way. He merely assists all things according to their own natural nature, and never dares to act forcibly out of personal intent.

Original (Chinese)

修身之道，遏欲为先。遏欲之要，治于未然则易，治于将然则难；治于将然犹易，治于已然则难。故太上云：「其安易持，其未兆易谋。」言人当闲居独处之时，心不役于事，事不扰于心，寂然不动，安止其所，其持己守身最为易易；且不闻不睹，无知无觉，杳无朕兆可寻，于此发谋出虑，思闲邪以存诚，其势至顺，其机甚便。以凡气柔脆，凡心细微，未至缠绵不已、辗转无休，于此而欲破其邪念、散其欲心，以复天道之自然、至诚之无妄，又何难情缘遽断，立见本来性天？此岂别有为之哉？不过曰「为之于未有」而已。古君子，防患于未萌，审机于将动，所以烟云尽扫，荆棘不生。又如天下太平，偶有强梁小丑，乘间作乱，亦不难单骑突出，立见投诚，「治之于未乱」，其便固如斯也。此炼己之工，犹易就耳。若欲修成九转，又未可以岁月计者。胡碌碌庸流不知，道为乾坤大道，人为宇宙真人，或有法会偶逢，而一世竟成者，或有因缘不遇，而数世始成者；或有重修数劫，历遇良缘，而功德未圆，性情多僻，势将成而又败，竟败而无成者。甚矣！大道之奥，未易几也。人不知道有由致，请观物所以成。彼夫合抱之木，其生也，特毫末耳，因阴阳煦妪，日变月化，而遂成大木焉；九层之台，其起也，仅累土耳，因人工凑集，日新月盛，而顿见为高台焉。又如一统山川，千里邦畿，欲造其途、底其境，岂容举足便至，计程可期者哉？其始也，无非足下一步一趋，由近及远，而始至其地焉。道而曰大，实具包天容地之量，生人育物之能，岂不劳层叠而至，曲折而前乎？惟知道之至人，不求速效，不计近功，金玉有磨，而心志不磨，春秋有变，而精进不变，庶由小而大，自卑而高，从近及远，一如合抱之木、九层之台、千里之行，而顿见奇观。虽然，道为自然之道，而功须自然之功，孟子「集义生气」，功在「勿助勿忘」，始合天地运行而造化维新也，同日月往来而光明如故也。若使有为而为，则为者败矣；有执而执，则执者失矣。夫天地日月，古今运转不停者，以其无心而成化也。倘天地有为以迭运，日月有执以推移，又安能万古不磨耶？俗云「天若有情天亦老，日惟无意日常明」，不其然乎？是以古之圣人，精修至道，妙顺天然，为而无为，功无败也，执而无执，德何失焉？奈今之从事于道者，为无为有，或作或辍，不知时行则行，时止则止，动静偶乖，与道远矣。又有几成而忽败，一败竟无成者矣。《书》曰：「慎厥终，惟其始。」所以历亿万年而不替。至于难得之货，人所贵也，圣人混俗和光，与人无异，独欲道而不欲货，初不知人世间有此珍重者，故不贵之，其淡泊明志如此。他如视听言动，日用云为，其荡检逾闲者无论矣，即有从事于道，为虚为实，著有著无，皆为过失。兹独效法前人，遵行古道，特抒臆见，以为大道权衡，非不称卓卓者，第思道为我之道，学为我之学，我自有之而自得之，又何学之足云？况人多过举，我独无为。以我无为之道，补众人之过举，即正己以正人也。且以我无为之道，辅万物之不及，即整躬以率物也。其不敢为如此。此圣人重德而贱货，正己以化人，民日迁善而不知为之者。噫！此圣人之身，即道之所寄，民物之所依，讵可一息偶违哉？

The foremost juncture of self-cultivation lies in restraining selfish desire. The secret of restraining selfish desire is this: dealing with a thought before it arises is easy, and dealing with it when it is about to arise is hard; dealing with it when it is about to arise is still manageable, but once it has become an accomplished fact, dealing with it is very hard indeed. Therefore the Most High Lord Lao says: "When stable, it is easy to keep hold of; when no sign has appeared, it is easy to plan for." This speaks of the times when a person lives at leisure and alone: the heart is not dragged around by affairs, and affairs do not disturb the heart; quiet and settled, firmly keeping to one's place, this is when it is easiest of all to restrain oneself and guard body and mind. Moreover, the eyes do not look about wildly, the ears do not listen wildly, the heart is clear and unstained, and not even the faintest sprout of desire has yet appeared. Establishing one's line of defense early, right here, intent on warding off evil thoughts and preserving sincerity, one goes with the flow of things, and the opportunity is most convenient.

For at this time the posterior-heaven floating breath is still soft and fragile, and the selfish, distracted mind is still extremely faint, far from having developed to the point of endless entanglement and restless tossing. If one takes this moment to break up evil thoughts and dispel the desiring mind, restoring the natural color of heaven's Way and the state of utmost sincerity free of falsehood, what difficulty is there in severing worldly attachments and seeing one's original heavenly nature on the spot? Does this require any extra effort or forcing? It is no more than "dealing with it before it exists."

The noble people of antiquity guarded against calamity before it sprouted and examined the moment carefully when a thought was about to stir, so clouds and haze were naturally swept away and not a single thorn could grow in the heart. It is like a time when the world is at peace and occasionally one or two arrogant petty bandits seize the chance to cause trouble: the court can simply send out a single horseman and they will surrender at once—"setting things in order before disorder arises" is just this easy and effortless.

However, this is only the basic work of refining the self, which shows results relatively easily; if one truly wishes to complete the nine-times-reverted Great Elixir, it cannot be reckoned in a few years or months. Alas for the mediocre, busy people of the world who do not understand that the Great Way is the Great Way that encompasses Qian and Kun, and that one who accomplishes it is a True Person standing between heaven and earth. Some happen upon a gathering of the Dharma and accomplish it in a single lifetime; some, whose affinities do not align, often need several lifetimes of accumulated cultivation before they succeed; and still others cultivate painfully for many years and repeatedly meet good opportunities, yet because their merit is not complete and their temperament still lopsided, just as they are about to succeed they fail halfway, and in the end accomplish nothing at all. Clearly the profound mystery of the Great Way is not something one can reach casually!

If people do not understand that cultivating the Way must proceed step by step, they might consider how the things of the world grow. Look at a great tree as thick as two people's encircling arms: when it first broke through the soil it was as faint as a fine hair, and only through the daily nourishment and nurturing of heaven and earth, yin and yang, changing slowly, did it grow into a towering tree. A nine-story tower was at first nothing but a handful of earth heaped beneath one's feet; only by human labor adding to it bit by bit, renewing it daily and expanding it monthly, did it rise into a lofty tower. Or again, to travel across ten thousand miles of mountains and rivers or hasten to a capital a thousand miles away—if you want to complete the journey and reach the destination, how could you get there by lifting your foot once, or count on your fingers and expect to arrive on the spot? At the start it is nothing but one footstep after another, from near to far, until at last you arrive.

The Way is called "great" because it truly has the capacity and the power to embrace heaven and earth and to transform and nurture humanity and all things; how could one reach heaven in a single step without passing through layer upon layer of accumulation and a winding road forward? Only the accomplished person who truly knows the Way seeks no quick results and covets no immediate gains: even gold and jade can be worn down, but their resolve for the Way cannot; spring and autumn, cold and heat keep alternating, but their diligent practice does not change in the least. In this way one goes from small to great, from low to high, from near to far, and, just like the tree that fills two arms, the nine-story tower, and the journey of a thousand miles, an astonishing sight unfolds before one knows it.

That said, since the Way is the Way of nature, the effort too must be natural effort. As Mencius speaks of "accumulating righteousness to generate the breath," the whole secret lies in the words "neither forcing nor forgetting"—neither pulling up the shoots to help them grow, forcibly pushing and pressing, nor forgetting it altogether and leaving it to itself. Only thus does one accord with the rhythm of heaven and earth's unceasing revolutions and generation, and resemble the sun and moon coming and going with their light ever renewed. If one insists on acting deliberately out of personal intent, the more one meddles, the more one spoils things; if one clings tightly to some state and will not let go, the harder one grasps, the more one loses. Look at heaven and earth, sun and moon, revolving without stopping from ancient times to the present—it is all because they "accomplish transformation without intent, naturally." If heaven and earth turned only through deliberate exertion and the sun and moon moved only through rigid clinging, how could they endure through the ages without decline? As the saying goes, "If Heaven had feelings, Heaven too would grow old; the sun, having no intent, is ever bright"—is this not exactly the principle?

Therefore the sages of antiquity, refining the supreme Way, all followed nature wondrously: though they applied effort, they left no trace of contrivance, so their work was never ruined; though they kept hold, they formed no sticky attachment, so their virtue was never lost. Unfortunately, today's practitioners of the Way lean now toward dead emptiness, now toward clinging to existence, working by fits and starts, "fishing three days and drying the nets for two," not understanding the fire phasing of advancing when it is time to advance and stopping when it is time to stop; the moment movement and stillness go slightly awry, they are a thousand miles from the Great Way. And there are many who, just as their practice is about to bear fruit, suddenly ruin it through a moment's carelessness, and after that one failure never succeed again. The Book of Documents says: "Be careful of the end, as of the beginning"—the caution at the end must be exactly the same as at the start. Only by holding to this can the work of the Way endure through hundreds of millions of years without decline.

As for the rare and hard-to-obtain goods of the world, ordinary people value them more than life itself. The sage dims his light and mingles with the dust, living amid the common world and looking no different from ordinary people outwardly; yet he seeks only the Great Way and does not covet goods, and in his heart he does not feel there is anything in the dusty world worth showing off or treasuring, so he does not value them at all—such is the lofty purity of his heart, plain and serene, with clear resolve.

Further, in everyday seeing, hearing, speaking, and moving, and in dealing with people and things—never mind those who are dissolute and transgress all bounds; even among those who set their hearts on learning the Way, those who constantly cling either to "emptiness" or to "substance," falling into the bias of "attachment to being" or "attachment to non-being," are all at fault. There are also some who copy the rules of their predecessors and proclaim the ancient Way, while secretly mixing in their own subjective opinions, believing they have grasped the standard of the Great Way—it may look quite brilliant, but think about it carefully: the Way is from the start the Way complete in my own nature, and learning is from the start the learning inherent in my own heart; it is enough to realize it and attain it oneself—what flashy external "learning" is there worth boasting about?

Moreover, worldly people are constantly given to lopsided, excessive actions, while I alone keep to non-action. Using my Way of non-action and purity to remedy the faults of the multitude is precisely rectifying myself so as to rectify others; and using my Way of non-action to assist what all things themselves lack is precisely putting myself in order so as to be a model for all things—this is how modest, cautious, and unwilling to act rashly the sage is. From this we see that the sage values virtue and makes light of goods, first rectifying himself and then transforming others, so that the people grow better day by day without knowing it, entirely unaware that anyone is deliberately arranging things. Ah, the very person of the sage is where the Great Way resides and what the people and all things rely on—how could it deviate from the Great Way for even an instant or a single breath?

Original (Chinese)

开首言「其安易持」数句，是言玄关一窍，寂然不动，感而遂通，且不睹不闻之际，此中有无善无恶之真。佛曰「那个」，儒曰「缉熙」，皆是此物。如初日芙蓉，晓风杨柳，娇红嫩绿，嫣然可爱。《易》曰：「天地絪缊，万物化醇。男女媾精，万物化生。」无非言初气至柔，去天未远。朱子诗曰：「半亩方塘一鉴开，天光云影共徘徊。」此言道心、人心，瞥眼分明。于此持志养气，立教割断牵缠，诞登彼岸。《礼》曰：「人生而静，天之性也。感于物而动，性之欲也。」犹天地一元初复，万象回春，虽物交物感，情欲有动，犹是天性中事，出于虚静，本乎自然，只须些些把持，无容大费智谋，即可遏欲存诚，闲邪归正，以萌蘖脆嫩，根芽孱弱，人欲不难立断，天理即可复还。古人谓之玄关一窍，又曰生门死户，以人心退藏，天心照耀，皆由未有未乱之时而为之治之也。但一阳初动，其机甚迅，其势甚微，至于二阳、三阳，则神凝气聚，真精自动，浩浩如潮生，溶溶似冰泮，要皆自微而著，由小而大，自近而远。至于进火退符，河车搬运，阳铅再生，阴汞复合，时烹时炼，渐结渐凝，神完气壮，药熟丹圆，更有六根震动、六通具足之盛，皆自玄关一动始也。惟此时初动，水源至清，古云「白虎首经至宝，华池神水真金」是也。此时一觉而动，把持得定，由此日运己汞，包固阴精，恰如初三一痕新月，至上弦而半轮，至十五而盈满矣。是以圣人知天下事物，无不由卑至高，由近及远，俱有自然之道在，于是为而无为，执而无执，一若天不言而四时行、百物生，岂若民之隳乃事、败乃功者？若此者，皆由一片虚灵，浑然无间，自不知所欲，亦并忘为无欲，故曰：「欲不欲。」至于黍珠之贵，贵不曾有为，其自无而有，所以既有仍无，修道人素所自具，不待外求，即使有所学，仍是无所学，故曰「学不学」。他如以一己之纯，化天下之驳，合天下之驳，归一己之纯，其诱掖众人，辅相万物，亦本乎自然而已矣，岂同逞其私智者哉？

In terms of the actual practice of internal alchemy, the opening lines of this chapter beginning with "when stable, it is easy to keep hold of" are pointing directly to the wondrous state of the one aperture of the Mysterious Pass: when still, it is quiet and unmoving; when it moves, it responds and penetrates; and right in the deepest stillness, where nothing is seen or heard, lies hidden a point of numinous impulse, purely original, neither good nor evil. Buddhists provisionally call it "That," Confucians praise it as "continuous brightness" (jixi, light that continues without interruption), but in fact they all refer to the same thing. When it first appears, it is like a lotus just opening in the morning sun, or tender green willows brushed by the dawn breeze—delicately lovely, fresh and alive, pure and endearing. The Book of Changes says: "Heaven and earth intermingle their breaths, and all things are richly transformed; male and female unite their essences, and all things are born." All this says is that this first-stirring point of prior-heaven original breath is extremely gentle, pure, and tender, closest to its natural source.

Zhu Xi has a poem that puts it wonderfully: "A half-acre square pond opens like a mirror; the light of the sky and the shadows of clouds linger in it together." The surface of the square pond is clear as a mirror, and the sky's light and the clouds' shadows show in it with perfect clarity—this is a metaphor for the moment of empty stillness, when what is the prior-heaven heart of the Way and what is the posterior-heaven human heart can be distinguished clearly at a glance. If right at this crucial juncture one holds to one's resolve and nurtures the original breath, one can at once sever worldly entanglements and step firmly onto the other shore of awakening.

The Book of Rites says: "The stillness of a person at birth is the nature bestowed by Heaven; the stirring that arises when it is touched by external things is the desire of that nature." This is like the time when the one origin returns between heaven and earth and spring comes back to all things: although there has just been contact with external things and the passions stir slightly, at first this still does not depart from heavenly nature; it flows naturally out of empty stillness. At this point one need only hold it lightly with True Intention—there is no need to expend great cunning or scheming—and one can halt selfish desire, keep hold of sincerity, shut evil thoughts out of the door, and return to the right path. Why is it so effortless? Because when selfish desire has just sprouted, it is still brittle and tender, its roots shallow, and a single moment of clear wakefulness can cut off human desire on the spot, so heavenly principle naturally returns. Therefore the ancients called this place the one aperture of the Mysterious Pass, and also "the gate of life and the door of death": following desire, one revolves in birth and death—this is the "door of death"; turning back to guard purity and stillness, one transcends the ordinary and enters sagehood—this is the "gate of life." Letting the human heart withdraw and letting the heavenly heart shine forth all depends on acting in advance, at the juncture when thoughts "do not yet exist and are not yet disordered."

However, the vitality of this "first stirring of the single yang" comes very swiftly, and its momentum is very faint. If one continues to nurture this vitality closely and continuously, then when it advances to the growing strength of the second and third yang, Spirit naturally condenses and breath gathers, and true essence spontaneously arises and fills one—vast and surging like the spring tide, warm and melting like ice and snow thawing—all of it growing step by step from faint to manifest, from small to great, from near to far. As for what the alchemical classics describe afterward—advancing the yang fire and withdrawing the yin tally (that is, regulating the fire phasing), the turning of the River Chariot (the circulation of the Celestial Circuit), the regeneration of yang lead and its union with yin mercury (the intermingled refining of Lead and Mercury), warming, nurturing, cooking, and refining at all times, gradually condensing and forming the elixir, until Spirit is complete and breath full, the medicine ripe and the elixir round, and even the appearance of the state of the shaking of the six roots and of perfect, unobstructed numinous awareness—traced back to the root, all of it begins from that first faint stirring of the Mysterious Pass.

Above all, recognize this: at the very moment of this first faint stirring, the source of the water is at its clearest and purest (that is, the clear source). An old alchemical formula uses the metaphor "the first flow of the White Tiger is the supreme treasure; the divine water of the Flowery Pool is true gold," using the image of what is newly born and utterly unstained to describe how precious this prior-heaven original breath is, not yet polluted by posterior-heaven desire. As long as one holds steady at this first stirring of awareness, and from then on uses pure True Intention day by day to enfold, warm, and nurture this newly born essence and breath, it will be just like the moon in the sky—from the thin sliver of new moon on the third day of the lunar month, to the half disc of the first quarter on the eighth, nurtured all the way to complete fullness on the night of the fifteenth.

Precisely because he understands this law, the sage knows deeply that all things under heaven grow from low to high, from near to far, according to natural rhythms; and so he applies effort without leaving marks of human chiseling, and keeps hold without falling into greedy, rigid attachment—just as Heaven speaks not a word, yet the four seasons run their course and all things flourish. How could he be like worldly people, who give up halfway and destroy their own achievements with their own hands? To be able to reach this point depends entirely on that expanse of empty, luminous, numinous awareness in the heart being undivided and uninterrupted, so that naturally no selfish desire arises, and even the thought "I am deliberately being free of desire" is completely forgotten; hence the classic says, "he desires not to desire."

As for the "mysterious pearl the size of a millet grain" that the alchemists regard as the supreme treasure (the Golden Elixir), what makes it most precious is precisely that it is not forcibly manufactured by posterior-heaven personal intent—it arises naturally out of utmost stillness and non-action, and once arisen it still returns to empty stillness without clinging. This is a treasure already complete in every practitioner of the Way, with no need to search painfully for it outside; even though one seems to be studying and applying effort, in the end it is no more than laying down false attachments and returning to what one originally has—after all the learning, nothing external has actually been added, and so it is said, "he learns not to learn." As for using one's own single pure and quiet heart to transform and guide the mixed confusion of the world, gathering all the world's confusion back to simple original truth, helping the multitude and assisting all things along the way—all of this is simply following the principle of nature. How could it be compared to those who show off their cleverness and play with their personal wits?

Key Terms

  • Refining the self: The most fundamental foundation-laying work before cultivating the elixir; its core is guarding against the slightest beginnings, restraining selfish desire, and steadying the mind-spirit, both when alone and when dealing with things, so as not to be led around by posterior-heaven false thoughts.
  • Mysterious Pass (the gate of life and door of death): The pivotal instant when movement arises at the extreme of stillness and the heart of the Way and the human heart part ways. Following desire and revolving makes it the "door of death"; holding and turning back with pure True Intention makes it the "gate of life."
  • Fire phasing (advancing the fire and withdrawing the tally): The sense of rhythm and measure in internal alchemy for handling movement and stillness, advance and retreat, tightness and looseness; Huang sums up its general principle with Mencius's "neither forcing nor forgetting."
  • Lead and Mercury: Alchemical ciphers; "lead" usually stands for prior-heaven original essence and original breath (the true yang within Kan), and "mercury" for numinous spirit-intention (the true yin within Li). "Daily moving one's own mercury to enfold and secure the yin essence" means using settled, still spirit-intention to warm and nurture the newly born true breath.
  • Flowery Pool and clear source: The Flowery Pool originally stands for the saliva of the mouth, and alchemists also use it for the place where the divine water of the Cinnabar Field gathers. "The first flow of the White Tiger is the supreme treasure; the divine water of the Flowery Pool is true gold" is a traditional metaphor of the alchemical classics referring specifically to the utterly clear prior-heaven vitality of the first stirring of yang, not yet fallen into posterior-heaven sensual desire and distracted thought.
  • The shaking of the six roots: A term used in the old alchemical classics for signs of extreme clarity and transparency of body and mind at an advanced stage of forming the elixir; it describes a state that comes of itself when practice is deep, and must never be deliberately imitated or pursued.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Press pause when an emotion "just sprouts." When anger, anxiety, or craving first arises as a tiny thought, it is at its most "brittle," and one deep breath can dispel it; once you follow it and rehearse it over and over in your head (winding and tossing), it snowballs into an emotional avalanche that is hard to stop.
  2. Replace the fantasy of "quick success" with the mindset of "heaped earth." Whether nurturing body and mind or accomplishing something, first admit that it takes time. Doing a little solidly each day, without demanding an overnight transformation, is actually the easiest way to cross the threshold.
  3. Remember the words "neither forcing nor forgetting." With quiet sitting and building habits, trying too hard (forcing) makes you tense and anxious, while being too lax (forgetting) makes you work by fits and starts; keeping a gentle, steady, uninterrupted rhythm is the modern, practical way of "without contrivance, without grasping."
  4. The closer you are to the finish, the steadier you must stay. "When people undertake things, they often spoil them just when success is within reach." When a project is wrapping up, when practice starts to show results, or when good times arrive, it is easiest to get carried away; finishing with the same care you had at the very start is what makes the work truly complete.

Translator's Notes

  1. "Warding off evil to preserve sincerity": From the Book of Changes, "Commentary on the Words of the Text" for the Qian hexagram: "ward off evil and preserve one's sincerity," meaning to guard against evil thoughts and nurture a sincere heart.
  2. "Accumulating righteousness to generate the breath," "neither forcing nor forgetting": Adapted from the chapter on nurturing the flood-like breath in Mencius ("Gongsun Chou I"): "It is produced by the accumulation of righteousness, not seized by a sudden act of righteousness" and "There must be work on it, but do not anticipate results; let the heart not forget it, but do not help it grow."
  3. "If Heaven had feelings, Heaven too would grow old": A famous line from the Tang poet Li He's "Song of the Bronze Immortal Taking Leave of Han": "Withered orchids see off the traveler on the Xianyang road; if Heaven had feelings, Heaven too would grow old." Later generations often paired it with "the sun, having no intent, is ever bright," to convey that Heaven's Way endures because it is selfless and unattached.
  4. "Be careful of the end, as of the beginning": From the Book of Documents ("The Announcement of Zhonghui"), meaning that if one wishes to make no mistakes at the end, one must remain cautious from the very beginning.
  5. "That" and "continuous brightness": Chan Buddhist public cases often use "That" to refer to the inexpressible original face; "continuous brightness" (jixi) comes from the Classic of Poetry ("Greater Odes," King Wen): "O continuous and bright, reverent and still," referring to virtue and the substance of the heart shining on without interruption.
  6. "A half-acre square pond opens like a mirror; the light of the sky and the shadows of clouds linger in it together": From the Southern Song scholar Zhu Xi's "Thoughts on Reading Books."
  7. "The first flow of the White Tiger is the supreme treasure; the divine water of the Flowery Pool is true gold": A traditional cipher of internal alchemy that uses the image of what is newly born and utterly pure as a metaphor for the prior-heaven original breath at "the first stirring of the single yang, when the source of the water is clearest"; it does not refer to the literal physiological phenomenon.
  8. Textual note: The chapter title in the base edition reads "第六十四章 一 无为无执"; the "一" is a misreading of a dash or separator and has been standardized as "第六十四章 · 无为无执." In the classic text, 九层之台 was miswritten in the base edition as 九层之合. In the original commentary: 持己, 正己, and 一己 were miswritten as 持已, 正已, and 一已; 杳无朕兆 as 香无朕兆; 辗转 as 镶转; 至诚之无妄 as 无安; 木 in 合抱之木 and 大木 as 术; 曰 in 道而曰大, 《书》曰, 《易》曰, and 《礼》曰 as 日; 乎 in 曲折而前乎 as 平; 足云 in 又何学之足云 as 足去; 况人 in 况人多过举 as 祝人; 嫣然 in 嫣然可爱 as 媽然; 絪缊 as 纲縕; 由卑至高，由近及远 as 由早至高，由近几远; 隳乃事 apparently as 愿乃事; 自不知 in 自不知所欲 as 目不知; 黍珠 as 泰珠; and 者哉 in 岂同逞其私智者哉 as 者战. All have been corrected one by one. The interlinear gloss at the end of the classic text in the base edition—"(脆，言其弱也。)", "'brittle' means weak"—has been incorporated into the plain-language rendering and is not included in the quoted text.
