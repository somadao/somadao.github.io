Contemplating the Opening, Contemplating the Wondrous

Contemplating the opening, contemplating the wondrous — use a little intention to contemplate the one opening of emptiness; blood and breath merged, free and untroubled, is the wondrous; walking, standing, sitting and lying all have their ways of contemplating

About This Chapter

This section turns Laozi’s two lines “with desire, contemplate the opening; without desire, contemplate the wondrous” into a “living” method that can be used at any time:

  1. What “desire” is: not selfish thoughts, nor deliberate intention, but something between intention and no intention — that is, “using a little intention”.
  2. What “the opening” is: not one cun three fen below the navel, nor the water country of the lower field, but the one opening of emptiness (Qiu Ling’s note: the Cave of Emptiness below the heart and above the kidneys).
  3. The method of contemplating the opening: use a little intention, close off the light of the eyes, still the hearing of the ears, seal the tongue’s urge to speak, keep the body upright — standing tall like a crag on a high mountain, serene like the bright moon reflected in a pool; cut off with a single stroke the desires for things that run outward and the stray thoughts that tangle within, gather all the spirit of the belly into the one opening of emptiness, and look after it at all times. In time, the heavenly truth appears and yang breath comes to life.
  4. What “the wondrous” is: when the practice reaches the point where blood and breath merge and the spirit is at ease, one is free and untroubled, without thought or worry — not wholly without thought, but thoughts arising like floating clouds passing across the great void, not lingered over in the least. Those who were usually gloomy are no longer gloomy, those who were usually dull and listless are no longer so — this is the working of the wondrous. “Immortals have no other method; they only give rise to joy, not to sorrow.”
  5. How to “contemplate”: shining on the square inch, yet with no square inch to shine on; attending to the elixir field, yet not confined to the elixir field. As if with intention and as if without, following the breath as it comes and goes, I simply remain clear, not pulled along by each rise and fall.
  6. Teaching according to the person: seeing that his disciples’ bodies and breath were declining and their spirits weary, Huang Yuanji feared that if, like young people, they shone rigidly on the one cun of the elixir field, it would be too tiring; so he taught this “lively way of contemplating”, which can be done anytime, anywhere. He also adds methods for sleeping, walking and standing.

Qiu Ling’s note: relaxing the standard in this way, not guarding the elixir field rigidly, practicing freely and at ease — students can do it at any time.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

窃思太上言“有欲观窍，无欲观妙”二句，以为生补漏之局，亦为生成真了道之本焉。欲，非私意也，亦非有意之意也。要想此个欲字，是在有意无意之间，即吾常言略用意思。是窍非脐下一寸三分之谓，亦非下田水乡之称。

I think the two lines of the Most High (Laozi), “with desire, contemplate the opening; without desire, contemplate the wondrous”, can serve as your framework for “mending the leaks” (repairing the depletion of essence and breath), and also as the foundation for your attaining truth and realizing the Way. The “desire” here is not selfish, stray thoughts, nor deliberate intention. Think clearly about this word “desire”: it lies between intention and no intention — it is what I often call “using a little intention”. This “opening” does not mean one cun three fen below the navel, nor does it refer to the water country of the lower field (the lower elixir field).

§2
Original (Chinese)

教生略用意思，闭眼光，凝耳韵，缄舌意，持身以正，亭亭矗矗如岩石之耸高山，悠悠扬扬似皓月之印潭水，收我一切驰逐于外之物欲，牵缠于内之杂念，一刀斩断，一齐收拾，片丝不挂，半蒂不存，于是以满腹精神尽收入于虚无一窍之中，时时照顾，念念不忘。久之自有天真发现，阳气来生，将尔腹中久积阴霾之气自然变化而无复有焉。由此再加猛烹急炼，常常了照，则真阳一动，收而采之，行升降之法，用进退之功，而大丹不患无成。自然精不外泄，气不外倾，神不分散，而色身可固，法身亦即此存矣！此即太上有欲观窍之说，

I teach you: use a little intention, close off the light of the eyes, still the hearing of the ears (not chasing sounds), seal the tongue (not wanting to speak), and keep the body upright — standing tall and erect like a crag towering on a high mountain; serene and unhurried like the bright moon reflected in a pool. Cut off with a single stroke all the desires for things that chase outward and all the stray thoughts that tangle within, clear them all away together, without a thread clinging, without a trace left; then gather all the spirit in your belly into the one opening of emptiness, look after it at all times, and never forget it from thought to thought. In time, naturally the heavenly truth (the original true nature) appears and yang breath comes to life, naturally dissolving the gloomy yin breath long accumulated in your belly until it is no more. Then, adding fierce cooking and urgent refining, constantly shining on it, as soon as true yang stirs, gather it in and collect it, carry out the method of rising and descending, use the work of advancing and withdrawing, and there is no fear that the great elixir will not be completed. Naturally essence does not leak out, breath does not pour out, spirit does not scatter; the physical body can be made secure, and the Dharma-body is preserved right here! This is the meaning of the Most High’s “with desire, contemplate the opening”.

§3
Original (Chinese)

至于妙之一字，道之实难。总在生做功到气血融和之地，精神舒畅之时，潇潇洒洒，无思无虑，亦不是全无思虑，祇是思虑之起如浮云之过太虚，毫不留恋，了不介意，此即妙之之象。然后还要从此推之，举凡平时抑郁者到此能不抑郁，昏颓者到此能不昏颓，皆是妙之作用。古云：“神仙无别法，只生欢喜不生愁”，斯得妙之真矣！

As for the word “wondrous”, it is truly hard to explain. In short, it is when your practice reaches the point where blood and breath merge and the spirit is at ease, free and untroubled, without thought or worry — not that there is no thought at all, but that when thoughts arise, they are like floating clouds drifting across the great void, not lingered over in the least, not minded at all: this is the scene of “the wondrous”. Then one must extend it from here: whoever was usually gloomy can at this time not be gloomy; whoever was usually dull and listless can at this time not be dull and listless — all this is the working of “the wondrous”. The ancients said: “Immortals have no other method; they only give rise to joy, not to sorrow.” This is to have grasped the true meaning of “the wondrous”!

§4
Original (Chinese)

再示观之之实。到此时也未尝不照于方寸，而实无方寸之可照。未尝不注于丹田，而却非丹田所能了。若有意若无意，随其气息之往还，我惟了了于中极，不为气息之一上一下所牵引焉，此观之之妙也。总之，始而稍稍垂头以顾諟，继而微微伸腰以缉熙，终而至于天机活泼，气节峻峋，即是长生之诀也。

Let me also tell you the actual practice of “contemplating”. At this time, it is not that one does not shine on the square inch (the heart), yet in fact there is no square inch to shine on; it is not that one does not attend to the elixir field, yet it is not something the elixir field can confine. As if with intention and as if without, following the breath as it comes and goes, I simply rest clearly in the middle, not pulled along by the breath’s rising and falling — this is the wonder of “contemplating”. In short: at first, lower the head slightly and watch over attentively (gushi); then straighten the back a little and let the brightness continue unbroken (jixi); finally reach the point where the heavenly dynamic is lively and one’s bearing upright and lofty (junxun) — this is the formula of long life.

§5
Original (Chinese)

吾见生等形气衰颓，精神疲惫，教之如后生小子实实了照于丹田一寸之间，则恐用力太劳反为不妥。故示以活泼之观法，无论随时随地俱可做得。然而坐有坐法，睡有睡法。坐法吾且不说。至于睡法，“未睡身，先睡心”。举凡一切事为，已就床榻思之何益？而且枉劳其心。惟有收摄神光，以头微微曲照入于一窍之中，自然神与气交而熟睡，火与水济而安闲。至于行也，须将神光照在两三步远，有如清风拂拂，缓步而行，不使累身可矣！若住立于何处，须知卓立不摇，如松柏之挺持，不拘束不放旷，斯住之法得矣！

I see that your bodies and breath are declining and your spirits weary; if I taught you, as I would young people, to shine solidly and rigidly on the one cun of the elixir field, I fear the effort would be too tiring and would do more harm than good. So I teach you this lively way of contemplating, which can be done anytime, anywhere. However, sitting has its method, and sleeping has its method. The method of sitting I will leave aside for now. As for the method of sleeping: “Before the body sleeps, let the mind sleep first.” Once you are in bed, what is the use of thinking about anything? It only wears out the mind in vain. Just gather in the spirit-light, bend the head slightly, and let the light shine into the one opening; naturally spirit and breath meet and you sleep soundly, fire and water help each other and you are at peace. As for walking: let the spirit-light shine on the ground two or three steps ahead, like a gentle breeze, walking at an easy pace, without tiring the body — that is all! If you are standing somewhere: know to stand tall without swaying, upright like pine and cypress, neither constrained nor slack — this is to have the method of standing!

Qiu Ling's noteon §1–§5
Original note (Chinese)

此段阐述老子《道德经》第一章“有欲观窍，无欲观妙”二句，而介绍活用之法。理解老子此二句关键在于懂得何者为“窍”？何者为“妙”？黄氏认为，“窍”非脐下一寸三分之丹田，而是心之下肾之上的虚无窟子。“有欲观窍”即为用点意思去观照虚无一窍。“妙”乃浑身气血融和、精神舒畅潇潇洒洒之象，“无欲观妙”即是无思无虑去观照这种畅快之景。如此放宽尺度，不死死守着丹田，自由自在行功，学者随时都可做得。

This passage expounds the two lines “with desire, contemplate the opening; without desire, contemplate the wondrous” from the first chapter of Laozi’s Daode jing, and introduces a way of applying them flexibly. The key to understanding these two lines of Laozi lies in knowing what “the opening” is and what “the wondrous” is. Huang holds that “the opening” is not the elixir field one cun three fen below the navel, but the Cave of Emptiness below the heart and above the kidneys; “with desire, contemplate the opening” means using a little intention to illuminate this one opening of emptiness. “The wondrous” is the scene of the whole body’s blood and breath merging, the spirit at ease, free and untroubled; “without desire, contemplate the wondrous” means illuminating this scene of ease without thought or worry. Relaxing the standard in this way, not guarding the elixir field rigidly, practicing freely and at ease — students can do it at any time.

Key Terms

  • With desire, contemplate the opening; without desire, contemplate the wondrous: based on the Daode jing, chapter 1: “Always without desire, so as to contemplate its wonders; always with desire, so as to contemplate its boundaries.”
  • Mending the leaks: repairing the depletion of essence and breath.
  • Using a little intention: a touch of intention between intention and no intention.
  • The one opening of emptiness: the Cave of Emptiness below the heart and above the kidneys; see Cave of Emptiness.
  • Closing off the light of the eyes, stilling the hearing of the ears, sealing the tongue: the eyes not looking outward, the ears not listening outward, the mouth not wanting to speak.
  • Gushi, jixi (顾諟、缉熙): watching over attentively, brightness continuing unbroken; from the Great Learning and the Book of Odes.
  • Junxun (峻峋): upright and lofty.
  • Before the body sleeps, let the mind sleep first: before sleeping, first let the mind settle.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Contemplating the opening: use a little intention; the eyes not looking outward, the ears not listening outward, the mouth not wanting to speak; the body upright; gather your spirit into that empty space below the heart and above the kidneys, and look after it at all times.
  2. Contemplating the wondrous: when sitting brings ease to body and mind, let thoughts that come pass like floating clouds across the sky, neither kept nor lingered over — this lightness and pleasure is itself “the wondrous”.
  3. Sleeping: once in bed, think about nothing more; gather back the spirit-light, lower the head slightly, shine lightly within the body, and fall asleep naturally.
  4. Walking: let the gaze fall two or three steps ahead, walking slowly and easily.
  5. Standing: straight and steady, neither stiff nor slack.
  6. Especially suited to the frail: Huang Yuanji says that people whose bodies and breath are declining and whose spirits are weary should not shine rigidly on the elixir field; this lively way of contemplating is safer.

Translator's Notes

  1. The original text of this section is one paragraph; the original book does not give the source. It is divided into five short passages.
  2. “It is only that thoughts arise” (祇是思虑之起): 祇 is the same as 只, “only”.
  3. “At first, lower the head slightly and watch over attentively; then straighten the back a little and let the brightness continue” (始而稍稍垂头以顾諟，继而微微伸腰以缉熙): gushi comes from the Great Learning and jixi from the Book of Odes; Huang Yuanji borrows them to describe adjustments of posture and of the spirit-light.
