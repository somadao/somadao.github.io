Introduction to Chapter Ten

What this chapter covers

The last chapter of the book. Qiu Ling has chosen three of Huang Yuanji’s talks that have the character of a summing-up:

  1. An account of the whole practice — the last paragraph of the commentary on the final chapter of the Commentary on the Daode jing: it strings together guarding the center, the River Chariot and fire phasing in one breath.
  2. Decoding the coded language of the elixir classics — Huang Yuanji explains one by one the coded terms of the elixir books — dragon and tiger, lead and mercury, wu and ji, other and self: in the end they are all “refining the breath with the spirit”; he also uses the six lines of the Qian hexagram to describe the rhythm of yang energy growing from faint to full and, once full, inevitably waning, and stresses that “what the world calls moving the breath is utterly mistaken”.
  3. Explaining Master Shao’s poem on the Cave of the Moon and the Root of Heaven — using a poem by Shao Yong, it matches the coming and going between the Root of Heaven (the one yang of the lower elixir field) and the Cave of the Moon (the Muddy Pellet) with the waxing and waning of yin and yang in the four seasons, the phases of the moon, and day and night — the close of the whole book.

What this chapter is about (in one sentence)

The great Way is no more than the two words spirit and breath: refining the breath with the spirit — dragon and tiger, lead and mercury are only metaphors for it; at the stage of guarding the center one may count the breaths to gather the mind, and once the breath-dynamic stirs of itself, leave it to nature; yang energy “comes and goes at leisure” between the Root of Heaven and the Cave of the Moon — it is nourished into being, not moved into being.

Reading tips

  1. Section 2 is the key to reading all elixir books. Remember: dragon, mercury, Li, self and ji all refer to “spirit”; tiger, lead, Kan, other and wu all refer to “breath”.
  2. The whole book comes to rest on the word “naturalness”. Section 1 says that early in guarding the center one may use the martial fire of counting the breaths to gather the mind, but once the breath-dynamic stirs of itself, further effort is “chasing the runaway pig”; Section 2 says “the breath can be nourished, but must not be over-nourished”; Section 3 says the coming and going between the Root of Heaven and the Cave of the Moon is “at leisure” — composed and unhurried.
  3. On content involving the upper elixir field (the Muddy Pellet, the Cave of the Moon), please still heed Qiu Ling’s warnings in Chapters One and Nine: people with yin deficiency and yang excess, or with high blood pressure, should not shine much on the upper elixir field.
  4. Sections 2 and 3 contain many trigram and hexagram images. Qiu Ling’s notes have already explained them clearly with hexagram symbols and plain language; readers unfamiliar with the Book of Changes need not strain — it is enough to grasp the one idea that “yang energy grows from faint to full, declines once full, and comes round again”.
