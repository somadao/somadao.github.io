Author’s Preface

Front Matter · Qiu Ling’s Preface

Translation

Having just finished compiling this book, I cannot help feeling privately consoled, and very fortunate to be able to present to readers the teaching on nourishing life of the Qing-dynasty qigong master Huang Yuanji!

Huang Yuanji, also called Huang Shang, is a man whose life is little known. We know only that he came from Fengcheng, that he was originally a widely learned scholar who had studied the classics and histories deeply and was also versed in Buddhism, that he travelled widely to famous mountains and sites, and that in the end he received from an extraordinary person the true formulas of the Daoist heart-method and entered the Way. In the Daoguang and Xianfeng reigns of the Qing he went to Sichuan, and for more than ten years taught students around Fushun in what is now Zigong, lecturing day and night on Laozi’s Daode jing and on traditional quiet practice for nourishing life; his disciples numbered no fewer than several thousand. He himself wrote a Commentary on the Daode jing (Daode jing zhushi 道德经注释), and his disciples compiled his sayings into a volume which, checked and corrected by Huang himself, became the Sayings from the Hall of Joyful Nurture (Leyutang yulu 乐育堂语录). Both books were printed in the Guangxu reign and reissued in the early Republic by the Jini Xianfang in Sichuan; later there were Chengdu, Zhenjiang, Hong Kong and Thai editions. Huang’s works have always been held in high esteem among Daoists and in qigong circles, and the contemporary master of the art of nourishing life Chen Yingning called his two books required reading for Daoists.

Since the founding of the People’s Republic, and especially after the ten years of turmoil, Huang’s works have become very rare in China, and few people know the name of Huang Yuanji. Yet his two books can be called major works among the alchemical scriptures and Daoist books of all ages on the dual cultivation of nature and life and quiet practice for nourishing life. Their reasoning is detailed and clear and their methods concrete; they fuse the true transmissions of the three teachings into one, say what earlier writers had not said, and sweep away the old fault of alchemical scriptures — dry, obscure and full of coded and secret language — while often sparkling with fine sayings that draw the reader in. Xiao Tianshi’s preface to the Taiwan edition of the Sayings from the Hall of Joyful Nurture judges Huang’s work thus: “Although it is a Daoist book, it can truly be regarded as an essential classic of the true transmission of the three teachings”; “every word reaches the great Way, every character is worth a thousand pieces of gold, and much of it reveals what the alchemical scriptures of a thousand years have not revealed and points to what the Daoist classics of a thousand years have not pointed to”; “its reasoning is plain and not abstruse, its meaning deep yet easy to grasp; in handing down a lost learning it is as simple and fluent as can be, so that anyone can understand it; in discussing the work it is as orderly and clear as can be, so that anyone can practice it.” Huang’s disciples also say in the preface to the Commentary on the Daode jing: “Every sentence of the master’s commentary is grounded in body and mind, and is especially close and not superficial; this is the learning that corrects the root and clears the source, the work of fulfilling the nature and establishing life, truly beyond comparison with other books.” These words of praise are not excessive.

In compiling this book I have mainly reworked the Sayings from the Hall of Joyful Nurture, adding in places parts of the Commentary on the Daode jing as a supplement, with brief notes of my own. Because the Sayings is a record of Huang’s teaching as it happened, its content is inevitably disordered and repetitive; so I selected its essentials and arranged them by the subjects discussed in the original, into chapters on the single opening of the Mysterious Pass, the gate of the Mysterious Female, the true intention, clarifying the mind and seeing the nature, essence, breath and spirit, the union of Kan and Li and the union of Qian and Kun, turning the River Chariot, gathering, refining and fire phasing, and so on. So as not to leave out any of the finest parts of Huang’s work, I made a separate chapter, Other Secret Formulas, to hold them. Finally I opened a chapter, Conclusion, containing the account of the complete work at the end of the Commentary on the Daode jing and the closing words Huang addressed to his disciples when he closed his school — ten chapters in all. All the titles and subtitles in the text were added by the editor.

The publication of this book will let readers glimpse the finest parts of Huang’s original works, and let the writings of a great qigong master of his age reappear in the world and be carried forward. Huang’s works are broad and deep, yet clear and easy to understand; they cover and run through every area of quiet practice for nourishing life and are full of practical experience. They are highly distinctive and may be called a unique learning, which is why I have given them the name “heart-method of quiet practice for nourishing life”. The editor’s notes merely combine my own modest experience of practice with my first understanding of Huang’s works, and serve only to add the finishing touch. Mistakes are inevitable, and I hope the worthy at home and abroad will correct them. I believe that a student who holds this one volume, studies it closely and practices it diligently, testing and verifying for himself, will not find it hard to enter the inner chamber of traditional quiet practice for nourishing life!

Qiu Ling, Mid-Autumn Festival 1987, in Guangzhou
