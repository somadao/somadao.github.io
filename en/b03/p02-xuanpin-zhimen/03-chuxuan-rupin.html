Chapter Two · The Gate of the Mysterious Female · 出玄入牝之处

Where One Goes Out by the Mysterious and In by the Female

Where one goes out by the mysterious and in by the female — concentrate the spirit in emptiness, merge the breath in vastness; below the navel, one cun three fen from the flesh, neither inside nor outside

About This Chapter

This is the most practical section of the chapter. Huang Yuanji himself says it is “the secret not transmitted through the ages, which I now speak out in one breath”: where exactly the gate of the Mysterious Female (the place of going out by the mysterious and in by the female) is, and how to begin.

  1. First, yinyun: the Changes says, “Heaven and Earth intermingle, and all things are transformed and made rich.” The intermingling breath in the human body is when all fire and tally are stopped, everything is undivided and unmoving, and the breath is left to flow of itself. When the work is fully mature, no intention is used at all; until one reaches this point, the mind of awakened illumination must not be forgotten, otherwise one thought arises, one thought vanishes, and the original thing is drowned in the mind of arising and vanishing.
  2. Where is the wondrous opening? It is “concentrate the spirit in emptiness, merge the breath in vastness”: it is still in the elixir field, only the gaze does not stare rigidly inward, and spirit and breath are not packed rigidly inward — one simply concentrates the spirit below the navel, not far from the skin and flesh. This is “neither inside nor outside”.
  3. Concentrate and illuminate this place with the intention, and as soon as the breath of mouth and nose stops, the breath of the elixir field rolls and churns, twisting into a single mass where inside and outside meet, going out and in, communicating with the original breath of Heaven — this is “merging the breath in vastness”, and also “the breathing of the true person comes from the heels”.
  4. The position is given very concretely: in the elixir field below the navel, one cun three fen from the flesh, where the diffuse, mingling breath condenses into one piece.
  5. Usable at any time: at tea time and mealtime, while talking and socializing, use just a slight intention to concentrate the spirit in the cavity of emptiness.

Qiu Ling’s note rates it very highly: this method fits both Huang’s view that “in contemplating the lower field one should be neither inside nor outside and not cling too much”, and the principle that “the fetus’s navel connects with the mother’s navel and carries on inner breathing”, so it produces embryonic breathing more easily and mobilizes the true breath more easily; it is more flexible than the various methods of contemplating the elixir field, works well, and can be practiced at any time in daily life. He holds that this position had never been stated by earlier people, and that Huang revealed it outright.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

《易》曰：“天地絪缊，万物化醇。”这个絪缊之气在人身中即是停内火外符，浑然不动任气息之流行。在功夫纯熟者，斯时全不用意。若未到此境，觉照之心不可忘也。若或忘之，又恐不知不觉一念起一念灭，转转生生将一个本来物事竟为此生灭之心而汩没焉。古佛云：“了知起处，便知灭处。”如此存养久久而见起灭之始，又久久而见未有念之始，斯得之矣！

The Book of Changes says: “Heaven and Earth intermingle, and all things are transformed and made rich.” (The two breaths of yin and yang of Heaven and Earth blend closely, diffusing and circulating, and from this all things are nurtured into being, rich and full.) This “intermingling breath” in the human body means no longer deliberately carrying out the fire phasing (stopping the inner fire and outer tally) — body and mind are undivided and unmoving, and the breath is left to flow of itself. For those whose work is fully mature, no intention at all is used at this time. If one has not yet reached this state, that point of the mind of awakened illumination (clear looking-on) must not be lost. If it is lost, one fears that, without noticing, one thought arises and one thought vanishes, following one after another, arising and vanishing, until that original thing (the originally pure true mind) is drowned in these ceaselessly arising and vanishing thoughts. An ancient Buddha said: “Know clearly where it arises, and you know where it vanishes.” (See clearly where a thought arises, and you also know where it vanishes — once seen clearly, the thought comes to rest of itself.) Preserving the mind and nourishing the nature in this way, in time one can see the very beginning of the arising and vanishing of thoughts; later still, one can see the very beginning before there were any thoughts (the original purity) — and then one has it!

§2
Original (Chinese)

至于黄庭之说，在不有不无，不内不外，又在色身中，又不在色身中。此个妙窍到底在何处？古所谓“凝神于虚，合气于漠”是也。夫“凝神于虚，合气于漠”亦犹是在丹田中，但眼光不死死向内而观耳，神气不死死入内而团耳，惟凝神于脐下离色身肉皮不远，此即不内不外之说也。以意凝照于此，但觉口鼻呼吸之气一停，而丹田之气滚滚辘辘，在于内外两相交结之处纽成一团，直见絪絪缊缊、浑浑沦沦、悠扬活泼之机，一出一入真与天之元气两相通于无间，生精生气生神即在此处，与天相隔不远，此即合气于漠之说也。昔人谓之元气胎息，真人之息以踵者，非此而何？

As for what is said of the “Yellow Court” (in this book, the lower elixir field) — that it is in a place neither existent nor nonexistent, neither inside nor outside; that it is within the physical body, and yet not within the physical body — where, then, is this wondrous opening? It is what the ancients called “concentrate the spirit in emptiness, merge the breath in vastness”. This “concentrating the spirit in emptiness, merging the breath in vastness” is in fact still within the elixir field, only the gaze does not stare rigidly into the body, and spirit and breath are not packed rigidly inward; one simply concentrates the spirit below the navel, in a place not far from the skin and flesh of the body — this is the meaning of “neither inside nor outside”. Lightly concentrate and illuminate this place with the intention, and you will simply feel that as soon as the breathing of mouth and nose stops, the breath in the elixir field rolls and churns, twisting into a single mass where the inside and outside of the body meet; at this time you can see directly a vital impulse that is intermingling and diffuse, undivided and whole, free and lively: going out and coming in, it truly communicates with the original breath of Heaven and Earth without the slightest gap — the producing of essence, of breath and of spirit is right here, not far from Heaven; this is the meaning of “merging the breath in vastness”. Is this not what earlier people called “the original breath of embryonic breathing” and “the breathing of the true person comes from the heels” (the true person who has completed cultivation breathes deep and long, as if from the heels)?

§3
Original (Chinese)

吾示脐轮之气与外来之天气相接，不内不外絪缊混合打成一片，即是返还于受气之初而与母气相连之时，即是胎息也。所谓真人之息以踵者，盖以真人之息藏之深深、达之亹亹、视不见、听不闻、搏不得、深而又密、如气之极于脚底是也。彼口鼻之气非不可用，但当顺其自然，不可专以此气为进退出入。若第用此气而不知凝神于脐下一寸三分之地寻出这个虚无窟子，以纳天气于无穷，终嫌清浊相间难以成丹。

What I point out to you is letting the breath around the navel connect with the breath of Heaven and Earth coming from outside, blending and mixing in a place neither inside nor outside, merging into one piece — this is returning to the time when a person first received breath and was still connected to the mother’s breath in the womb; this is embryonic breathing. “The breathing of the true person comes from the heels” means that the true person’s breathing is hidden extremely deep, penetrating and unbroken (weiwei), unseen by the eye, unheard by the ear, ungraspable by the hand, deep and dense, as if the breath reached all the way to the soles of the feet (not that one really breathes from the heels). The breath of mouth and nose is not unusable; it should simply follow its natural course, and one must not rely solely on this breath of mouth and nose for the work of advancing and withdrawing, going out and coming in. If one uses only this breath and does not know how to concentrate the spirit one cun three fen below the navel, finding this Cave of Emptiness so as to draw in the breath of Heaven and Earth continuously, one will in the end find it a mixture of clear and turbid (the clear breath of Prior Heaven mixed with the turbid breath of Posterior Heaven), and it will be hard to form the elixir.

§4
Original (Chinese)

昔人云，天以一元之气生人，此气非口非鼻非知觉运动之灵可比。又云：“玄牝之门世罕知，休将口鼻妄施为，饶君吐纳经千载，怎得金乌搦兔儿。”即此数语观之，明明道出玄入牝，实在脐下丹田离肉一寸三分之间氤氤氲氲凝成一片者是。学人无论茶时饭时、言语应酬时，微微用一点意思凝神于虚无穴中，自然合气于漠，直见真气调动有不可名言之妙。然于此调息则知觉不入于内，而坎水自然澄清。此历代不传之秘，吾今一口吐出，后之学者勿视为见文而忽之也。

Earlier people said: Heaven gives birth to people with a mass of original breath; this breath is not the breath of the mouth, nor the breath of the nose, nor comparable to that bit of numinous capacity that can perceive and move. There is also a poem: “The gate of the Mysterious Female is rarely known in the world … how could the golden crow catch the hare?” From these lines alone it is stated plainly: going out by the mysterious and in by the female is really in the elixir field below the navel, one cun three fen from the skin and flesh, in that place where the diffuse, mingling breath condenses into one piece. Whether the student is drinking tea or eating a meal, or talking and socializing with others, if he simply uses the slightest bit of intention to concentrate the spirit in this cavity of emptiness, he will naturally “merge the breath in vastness” and see directly the true breath being mobilized, with a wonder beyond words. Regulating the breath here, perception (thoughts and the senses) does not slip in to disturb it, and the water of Kan (the essence-breath in the lower elixir field) naturally becomes clear. This is a secret the patriarchs of all ages would not transmit openly; today I speak it out in one breath, and later students must never treat it as an empty formality and neglect it!

Qiu Ling's noteon §1–§4
Original note (Chinese)

黄元吉氏于此又直道出历代不传之秘，即“凝神于虚，合气于漠”的具体功法及出玄入牝所在之处。

“凝神于虚，合气于漠”，虚即虚无，漠即淡漠。我凝神用性光合两眼神光下照于脐下丹田，却又不死死向内而观，而是观照下田离皮肉一寸三分之间，即是凝神于虚无之意。这样，使丹田之气与外来天气更易相接，两相混合，打成一片，絪絪缊缊，此即合气于漠之意。

此一功法符合黄氏观照下田于不内不外处，不要太过执着之说，也符合返还胎儿于母体时其脐轮与母之脐轮相接，得以行内呼吸之论。因此，此一功法更易产生胎息，更易调动真气。世有各种观照丹田之法，黄氏此法较为灵活，效果亦好，且可以结合生活，随时行之。

黄氏认为，出玄入牝亦即玄牝之门，实在脐丹田离肉一寸三分之间氤氤氲氲之气凝成一片之处，此论为前人所未说，而黄氏直泄之。

Here Huang Yuanji again states outright the secret not transmitted through the ages: the concrete method of “concentrating the spirit in emptiness and merging the breath in vastness”, and the place where one goes out by the mysterious and in by the female.

“Concentrate the spirit in emptiness, merge the breath in vastness”: emptiness is void, vastness is indifference. I concentrate the spirit, using the light of nature joined to the spirit-light of the two eyes to shine down on the elixir field below the navel, yet without staring rigidly inward, contemplating the lower field one cun three fen from the skin and flesh — this is the meaning of “concentrating the spirit in emptiness”. In this way the breath of the elixir field connects more easily with the breath of Heaven coming from outside; the two blend and merge into one piece, intermingling and diffuse — this is the meaning of “merging the breath in vastness”.

This method fits Huang’s view that “the lower field should be contemplated in a place neither inside nor outside, without clinging too much”, and also the principle of “returning to the time when the fetus in the mother’s body has its navel connected to the mother’s navel and so can carry on inner breathing”. Therefore this method produces embryonic breathing more easily and mobilizes the true breath more easily. There are all sorts of methods of contemplating the elixir field in the world; Huang’s method is comparatively flexible, works well, and can be combined with daily life and practiced at any time.

Huang holds that going out by the mysterious and in by the female, that is, the gate of the Mysterious Female, is really in the elixir field below the navel, one cun three fen from the flesh, where the diffuse, mingling breath condenses into one piece. This had never been said by earlier people, and Huang revealed it outright.

Key Terms

  • Yinyun (intermingling): the two breaths of yin and yang of Heaven and Earth blending closely; the same as 氤氲.
  • Stopping the inner fire and outer tally: stopping both fire and tally, no longer deliberately carrying out the fire phasing, letting the breath flow naturally.
  • The mind of awakened illumination: the point of looking-on that must not be forgotten until the work is mature.
  • Know clearly where it arises, and you know where it vanishes: see clearly where a thought arises and you know where it vanishes.
  • Yellow Court: in this book, the lower elixir field (see the reading tips of Chapter One).
  • Concentrating the spirit in emptiness: the spirit concentrated below the navel, not far from the skin and flesh, with neither the gaze nor spirit and breath pushed rigidly inward.
  • Merging the breath in vastness: the breath of the elixir field and the breath of Heaven from outside mixing in a place neither inside nor outside, merging into one piece.
  • Neither inside nor outside: not deep in the lower abdomen, nor outside the body, but not far from the skin and flesh.
  • The breathing of the true person comes from the heels: from Zhuangzi, “The Great Ancestral Teacher”, describing the true breath as so deep and dense it seems to reach the heels; see True Breath.
  • Navel wheel: the area around the navel.
  • One cun three fen below the navel: the place of going out by the mysterious and in by the female that Huang Yuanji points to; Qiu Ling’s note says “one cun three fen from the skin and flesh”.
  • Cavity of emptiness: that is, the Cave of Emptiness.
  • The water of Kan becoming clear: the water in the elixir field (the palace of Kan) clear and undisturbed by perception.
  • Secret not transmitted: the knack that alchemists of all ages would not state openly.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Position: the elixir field below the navel, about one cun three fen from the skin and flesh — not deep in the lower abdomen, and not outside the body. Qiu Ling says it clearly: “contemplating the lower field one cun three fen from the skin and flesh”.
  2. Method: use the spirit-light of the two eyes joined to the light of nature, shining gently down to this place, and simply concentrate and illuminate it with the intention. The gaze does not stare rigidly inward; spirit and breath are not packed rigidly inward.
  3. What appears: as soon as the breathing of mouth and nose stops, the breath of the elixir field rolls and churns, twisting into a mass where inside and outside meet, continuous and dense, with an ease beyond words.
  4. The breath of mouth and nose: it is not that you do not use it, but that you let it follow its natural course and do not use it for advancing and withdrawing, going out and coming in.
  5. You can practice at any time: while eating, drinking tea, talking or socializing, you can use just a slight intention to concentrate the spirit here — Qiu Ling says this is precisely the advantage of this method.
  6. The mind of awakened illumination must not be forgotten: until the work is mature, do not think that “letting things take their natural course” means attending to nothing; that point of looking-on must still be there.

Translator's Notes

  1. The original text of this section is in two paragraphs; the original book does not give the source. Each is divided into two short passages.
  2. “Heaven and Earth intermingle, and all things are transformed and made rich”: from the “Xici” (Appended Statements), part two, of the Book of Changes.
  3. “Stopping the inner fire and outer tally” (停内火外符): so in the original book; the general sense is stopping the inner and outer fire and tally, no longer deliberately carrying out the fire phasing, letting the breath flow naturally.
  4. “Penetrating and unbroken” (da zhi weiwei 达之亹亹): 亹亹 (wěiwěi) describes something continuous and unbroken. “Ungraspable by the hand” (bo bu de 搏不得) uses the wording of chapter 14 of the Daode jing, “grasp it and you cannot get it”.
  5. “Never treat it as an empty formality and neglect it” (勿视为见文而忽之也): 见文 is probably a mistake for 具文 (an empty formality); the translation follows 具文.
  6. “How could the golden crow catch the hare”: the previous section, quoting the same poem, reads 争得; each is recorded as in the original book.
  7. Qiu Ling’s note, “really in the navel elixir field one cun three fen from the flesh” (实在脐丹田离肉一寸三分之间): a character 下 (“below”) seems to be missing; it should read “the elixir field below the navel”, consistent with the original text.
  8. “One cun three fen”: the original book does not say which measure is meant; we record it as is, without converting it.
