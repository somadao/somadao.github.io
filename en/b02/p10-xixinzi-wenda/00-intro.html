Introduction to Part Ten

What this book is

Xixinzi came from Tongliang in Sichuan. His Sayings on Understanding the Dao (Mingdao yulu 明道语录), published at the end of the 1930s, is a collection of his replies to letters from people studying the Way in many places. The original book is only a few tens of thousands of characters long, yet it explains the methods of the first vehicle of alchemical practice — laying the foundation and the jade-liquid reverted elixir — almost without holding anything back. Qiu Ling selected its best parts and reprinted them here, calling it “truly a bridge for entering alchemical practice”.

The whole part is in question-and-answer form, more than a hundred and twenty exchanges. The questioners are all people actually practicing: What do I do about having many thoughts? Where exactly is “below the navel”? Does falling asleep count as practice? In stillness I see light, hear sounds, my body itches — is this good or bad? What do the terms in the alchemical scriptures (Xun wind, the handle of the Dipper, the intercalary remainder, muni …) mean? Xixinzi’s answers are extremely short and direct; many are a single sentence. Much of what is hard to understand in the previous nine parts can be found here in one plain sentence.

The Sayings on Understanding the Dao by Xixinzi of Tongliang in Sichuan was published at the end of the 1930s; it is a book compiled from his letters replying to people from many quarters who asked about the Way. Although the original book is only a few tens of thousands of characters long, it reveals almost entirely the methods of the first vehicle of alchemical practice, “laying the foundation and the jade-liquid reverted elixir”; it is truly a bridge for entering alchemical practice. The book answers all kinds of questions and echoes the alchemical scriptures selected above, helping to resolve their difficulties. I have now specially selected its best parts into one piece and reprinted it here, for readers to check and consult.

What this part is about (in one sentence)

Put down all ties, sit upright with legs crossed, rest the mind in the elixir field below the navel, let the breathing follow its own nature, neither forgetting nor helping, spirit and breath relying on each other; when stillness reaches the dim and obscure and both self and others are forgotten, that is the first return to emptiness, the first seeing of the nature; from here, when stillness reaches its extreme and yang is born, one can speak of gathering the medicine, regulating the medicine, laying the foundation and the Celestial Circuit — do not crave or cling to any scene, do not hold rigidly to any number, and without a true transmission do not act at random.

Reading tips

  1. Read the first and fourth sections first. The first section is about how to sit and where to put the mind; the fourth consists of practitioners’ own reports of what they experienced and Xixinzi’s judgments — the closest to real practice. The second and third sections are mostly short explanations of alchemical terms and can be used as a small dictionary.
  2. Xixinzi stresses three things again and again: “neither forgetting nor helping” — guarding without guarding rigidly, letting go without letting loose; “having no mind when facing circumstances” — do not crave or cling to any scene; “the pity is that without a true transmission, people muddle along and do not match the proper limits of the orthodox school” — without reliable transmission do not act at random, and above all do not guard the upper part (the root of the nose).
  3. Some especially sober remarks: “If you have not practiced it yourself, however clear the theory may be, it is in the end only the moon in the water and a flower in the mirror”; “this is an ancient method; there is no need to cling to it”; “do not be rigid about working with exact measurements; only then can it be lively — otherwise you cling to forms”; “although the circulation in the body has the same degrees as Heaven, it does not have the same years, months, days and hours as Heaven”.
  4. What this part teaches is the Wu-Liu school’s alchemy for men, often taking “the outer yang rising” or “the male organ rising vigorously” as the sign that the mechanism of the breath is stirring; we translate according to the original meaning. The last exchange deals specifically with methods for women.
  5. Xixinzi’s answers often cite The Immortal Lineage of Wu and Liu, the Secret Principles of the Three Carts, the Method of Gathering the Mind, the Hundred-Line Chapter and others, and can be read alongside the third and fifth parts and the sayings in the ninth part of this book.
