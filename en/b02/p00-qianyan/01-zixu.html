Author’s Preface

Front Matter · Qiu Ling’s Preface

Translation

The great Way of the Golden Elixir is by nature simple, plain and down to earth. It is nothing more than refining and nourishing essence, breath and spirit — taking emptiness as its substance, clarity and stillness as its function, naturalness as its guiding principle and non-action as its work — until one reaches the joint cultivation of nature and life. In truth anyone can cultivate it and anyone can practice it. But since ancient times the alchemical scriptures and Daoist books have deliberately used mysterious language, with countless veiled words and metaphors; and on top of that, the true formulas could supposedly be obtained only by oral transmission from a teacher. As a result, students could not find the door to enter.

Alchemical practice (dangong 丹功) has always been called “the orthodox lineage of the Daoist school”. It is a traditional, advanced form of qigong with a long history. Whether or not they have a teacher, students who want to enter it and go deeper have no choice but to study the alchemical scriptures and Daoist books and test them against their own practice. With this in mind, in 1987 I compiled Selected Ancient Chinese Qigong Texts, Annotated. It gathered several of the more accessible and practical alchemical methods of the Daoist Longmen school, together with the essentials of the Collection of Daoxiang (Daoxiang ji 道乡集) and the Concise Essentials of Great Accomplishment (Dacheng jieyao 大成捷要), both transmitted on Mount Lao, with Sun Bu’er’s poems on the stages of women’s inner alchemy as an appendix. My aim was to offer beginners some introductory readings, to spare them the labour of groping through the boundless sea of alchemical literature, and also to provide a reference for those studying alchemy in depth. This spring, travelling in Hong Kong for a fairly long stay, I came across a number of old alchemical books that cannot be found on the mainland. I felt that, although I had compiled the earlier book, I had not said all I wanted to say. So in my spare time I compiled this book and called it Essentials for Entering Alchemical Practice (Dangong rumen zhiyao 丹功入门指要). It may be regarded as a sister volume to the earlier one.

The alchemical scriptures and Daoist books collected here were chosen on the basis that earlier generations had already used them as bridges for beginners; I also took account of what people need today, and selected them only after confirming them through my own practice. Daoist alchemical practice values usefulness, so the first consideration must be practical value, not empty talk. The three works by Li Hanxu, patriarch of the Western school — the Classic of Ending through a Serial Account of the Posterior Heaven (Houtian chuanshu wenzhong jing 后天串述文终经), the Record of Following the Path through the Nine Levels of Refining the Mind (Jiuceng lianxin xuntu lu 九层炼心循途录) and the Secret Principles of the Three Carts (Sanche mizhi 三车秘旨) — not only set out the stages of the practice clearly but are especially detailed on how to begin; they emphasise the first and middle vehicles and are well suited to beginners. Master Cui’s Mirror for Compounding the Medicine (Cuigong ruyao jing 崔公入药镜), Patriarch Lü’s Hundred-Line Chapter (Lüzu baiju zhang 吕祖百句章), Patriarch Lü’s Hundred-Character Stele (Lüzu baizi bei 吕祖百字碑) and others are all famous old alchemical texts that no practitioner can afford not to read. Plain and Simple Words on the Dao (Daoyan qianjin shuo 道言浅近说) and Straight Talk on the Mysterious Pivot (Xuanji zhijiang 玄机直讲) by Zhang Sanfeng, patriarch of the Sanfeng school, and the sayings of alchemists selected by the Wu-Liu school introduce the knacks of the practice and are especially worth reading. Xixinzi’s Answers to Students’ Questions (Xixinzi da xuezhe wen 洗心子答学者问) resolves difficult questions students meet in practice, and its content echoes the earlier selections. The essay A Brief Account of the Essentials of the Various Schools of the Way of the Elixir (Dandao gepai yaozhi jianshu 丹道各派要旨简述) helps readers understand the origins of alchemy and the strengths and weaknesses of each school’s methods — something a beginner should know. The appendix, Quiet Sitting for Health (Jianshen jingzuo fa 健身静坐法), presents a fairly reliable method so that students can easily begin. All the notes to these works were either added by the editor or selected from older and newer commentaries that are easy to understand and faithful to the original meaning, so that students will not misread them and will begin on a sound footing.

Someone may ask: apart from the Wu-Liu school and Xixinzi, most of the works chosen here belong to the Daoist “human-elixir” methods (renyuan danfa 人元丹法), and some of them are written in terms of the “grafting” method (zaijie fa 栽接法, i.e. dual cultivation between a man and a woman). Why explain them in terms of the “pure and quiet” method (qingjing fa 清静法)? My answer is this. The human-elixir methods have always included both the pure-and-quiet method and the grafting method. The grafting method has always been the Daoist school’s greatest secret, known to very few people in the past or today. Even if some do know it, apart from a married couple practicing privately with one heart, promoting it publicly would certainly be rejected by social morality and provoke criticism — even in Hong Kong and Taiwan. As the saying goes, “Methods may differ in ten thousand ways, but the principle is one.” Although “the same kind” (tonglei 同类) refers to yin and yang, a single person’s body also has its own yin and yang, and Heaven and Earth too have yin and yang. If the grafting method is explained through the pure-and-quiet method, and the explanation is reasonable and fits reality, why not? Otherwise we could only stand by and watch a great number of alchemical scriptures that deal with the grafting method sink into oblivion and be lost — would that not be a pity?

In compiling this book I received much material from my fellow practitioner in Hong Kong, Mr Yao Yushan, who also honoured it with a preface. I offer him my sincere thanks.

Qiu Ling, 6 August 1988, while travelling in Hong Kong

Translator's Notes

  1. This preface is the author’s own writing in modern Chinese; the Chinese-language site shows it in the original only. It is translated here in full.
  2. “Grafting” (zaijie 栽接) is the Daoist term for alchemical methods practiced by a man and a woman together. Throughout this book Qiu Ling explains every such passage according to the “pure and quiet” method of solitary practice — “a single person’s body has its own yin and yang” — and the translations on this site follow the same principle.
  3. The preface by Yao Yushan that Qiu Ling mentions is not included on this site, in keeping with the rule that each book keeps only the author’s own preface.
