Li Hanxu’s Secret Principles of the Three Carts, Annotated · 篇首导读

Introduction to Part Three

What this book is

The Secret Principles of the Three Carts (Sanche mizhi 三车秘旨) is the last of Li Hanxu’s three introductory works. The “three carts” are three River Chariots — three stages of work going from shallow to deep:

  • The first River Chariot: moving the breath — the zi–wu Lesser Celestial Circuit; opening the passes, laying the foundation, obtaining the medicine and forming the elixir;
  • The second River Chariot: moving the essence — the jade-liquid River Chariot; drawing the lead of Kan, controlling the mercury of Li, refining one’s own nature;
  • The third River Chariot: moving essence and breath together — the Greater Celestial Circuit; the great elixir of golden liquid of the seven returns and nine reversions; refining the spirit to return to emptiness.

Qiu Ling points out specially that the three River Chariots are three stages of work; the route they travel is only one. Do not misunderstand them as three routes. The last section, “The method of beginning by gathering the mind”, is Li Hanxu’s own method for getting started; it is almost the same as the passage from Xixinzi quoted in Qiu Ling’s notes to Part One, and the two can be read side by side.

This book long existed only in old manuscript copies. In 1937 it was edited by Chen Yingning and published together with Li Hanxu’s Talks on the Openings of the Dao. In his “Notes for readers” at the head of the volume, Chen Yingning says that in the text on the three River Chariots “there are inevitably some exaggerated descriptions and inappropriate metaphors; readers should please not cling to the literal wording and twist it into explanations” — this sentence is the key to reading this part.

Qiu Ling's editorial note

本书作者为清代道门西派祖师李涵虚。此书过去为旧抄本，未曾刊版行世，迄1937年，由当代养生学大师陈樱宁氏校订，与李氏另一著作《道窍谈》合刊印刷出版，由流传至出版时隔八十余年。

李氏著述自成一派，有其特点。本书与《后天串述文终经》、《九层炼心循途录》互为表里，互相发明。书中大旨仍不出《参同契》、《悟真篇》之范围。

三件河车之说，乃指三段功夫，程度由浅入深。至于运转河车之路，仍只一条，勿误会河车有三条路也。

本书由编者略加注释

The author of this book is Li Hanxu, patriarch of the Western school of Daoism in the Qing dynasty. It used to exist only in old manuscript copies and was never printed. In 1937 it was edited by Mr Chen Yingning, the great modern master of the art of nourishing life, and printed and published together with Li’s other work, Talks on the Openings of the Dao. More than eighty years passed between its circulation and its publication.

Li’s writings form a school of their own and have their own characteristics. This book and the Classic of Ending through a Serial Account of the Posterior Heaven and the Record of Following the Path through the Nine Levels of Refining the Mind are the outside and inside of one another, each illuminating the others. The general meaning of the book still does not go beyond the scope of the Cantong qi and the Wuzhen pian.

The expression “three River Chariots” refers to three stages of work, going from shallow to deep. As for the route along which the River Chariot turns, there is still only one; do not misunderstand the River Chariot as having three routes.

The editor has added brief notes to this book.

What this part is about (in one sentence)

The same River Chariot route, travelled three times, each time deeper: first wait in the obscure for the return of one yang, follow it to move the breath through the passes and turn breath into liquid to form the elixir head; then move the jade liquid and refine one’s own nature; finally meet life with nature, through the seven returns and nine reversions, and refine the spirit to return to emptiness — and the starting point of it all is the section on “gathering the mind”: quieting the mind, closing the mouth, regulating the breath and boring into the obscure.

Reading tips

  1. We suggest reading section 4, “The method of beginning by gathering the mind”, first, then coming back to the three River Chariots. The first three sections all build on it.
  2. The most important sentence in the first River Chariot is the passage from Talks on the Openings of the Dao quoted in Qiu Ling’s notes: the River Chariot “is not a method of imagining and transporting” — one moves it only when there is a natural movement of breath; otherwise it is empty coming and going, of no benefit whatever.
  3. The second River Chariot speaks of “holding the breath in the nose, clenching the jaws, pressing both hands back against the seat, the head and face turned up towards the roof beam”, and the third River Chariot is full of veiled words such as “first menses”, “heavenly water”, “the infant and the lovely maiden”. Qiu Ling says that these metaphors “can be said to mean heart and kidneys, or spirit and breath, or even man and woman”. Following the principle he laid down in his preface, we translate them all according to the pure-and-quiet method (heart and kidneys, spirit and breath within one body), and we have added safety notes to methods such as holding the breath.
