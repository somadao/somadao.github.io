The Method of Beginning by Gathering the Mind

The Method of Beginning by Gathering the Mind — quieting the mind, closing the mouth, regulating the breath; neither void nor empty; boring into the obscure

About This Chapter

This is the section of the whole Secret Principles of the Three Carts — and of Li Hanxu’s three introductory works — most worth reading again and again, and usable by everyone. It is almost the same as the passage from Xixinzi quoted in Qiu Ling’s notes to Part One, the Serial Account of the Posterior Heaven — in fact Xixinzi was explaining this very section.

The order for getting started is:

  1. First quiet the mind, then close the mouth, then regulate the breath. When the mind is quiet the breath is even; “regulating without regulating is best”.
  2. Fix the spirit on the yinqiao for a moment, then raise it to the opening of emptiness. When the breath through the nose is even, close the eyes and look inward; first fix the spirit around the perineum for a moment, then raise mind and breath to the area “behind the navel and in front of the waist, below the heart and above the kidneys” — without clinging to a precise spot.
  3. The measure of civil and martial fire: if the mind is too tight it becomes heated, and one must follow what is natural (civil fire); if the mind is too loose it becomes cold, and one must keep to what is natural (martial fire).
  4. Neither void nor empty: the mind is like empty space, yet with breath to rely on it is not void, and with breath to follow it is not empty. Between not-void and not-empty, stillness upon stillness, clarity upon clarity.
  5. Boring into the obscure: let everything go, forget self and others; within the obscure there is breath, and a single spirit alone is aware of it — that is the true breath. When the true breath appears there is a warm, tingling, itching feeling in the heart; even then one must press it back into the opening of emptiness within the body.

The second passage explains “concentrating the spirit and regulating the breath” more thoroughly still: when the mind is not yet clear, do not close the eyes inward; first persuade and encourage yourself and bring it back; concentrating the spirit is “sitting in emptiness, leaning to neither side”; regulating the breath is “once the mind and spirit are still, let the breath be natural; I simply keep to it and go along with it”. The last four sentences are the essence of the whole text: the mind resting below the navel is called concentrating the spirit; the breath returning below the navel is called regulating the breath; keeping to its clear, pure naturalness is called not forgetting; going along with its clear, pure naturalness is called not helping.

Original Text and Translation

§1
Original (Chinese)

养生之道，真息为本。曹文逸云：“我为诸公说端的，命蒂从来在真息”。诚要言也。下手功夫，先静心，次缄口，次调息（心静则气平，不调之调为上），鼻息平和，然后闭目内观，神注肾根之下阴跷一脉（谷道前阴囊后）。如此片时，将心息提上虚无窍内（脐后腰前，心下肾上，中间一带，不可拘执），停神安息，以自然为主。

In the way of nourishing life, the true breath is the root. Cao Wenyi says: “Let me tell you all the truth: the stalk of life has always been in the true breath.” Truly these are essential words. The work of getting started is: first quiet the mind, then close the mouth, then regulate the breath (when the mind is quiet the breath is even; “regulating without regulating” is best). When the breath through the nose is even, close the eyes and look inward, and fix the spirit on the yinqiao channel below the root of the kidneys (in front of the anus and behind the scrotum). After a moment like this, raise mind and breath into the opening of emptiness (behind the navel and in front of the waist, below the heart and above the kidneys, the area in between — do not cling to a precise spot); let the spirit rest and the breath settle, taking naturalness as the main thing.

§2
Original (Chinese)

心太严则炎，务必顺其自然，即文火也。心太散即冷，务必守其自然，即武火也。文武烹炼，始终妙用。内息匀称，勿忘勿助。是时也，心如虚空，有息相依，则不虚。有息相随，则不空。不虚不空之间，静而又静，清而又清，气息绵绵，心神默默。

If the mind is too tight it becomes heated; one must follow what is natural — this is the civil fire. If the mind is too loose it becomes cold; one must keep to what is natural — this is the martial fire. Cooking and refining with civil and martial fire is the wonderful function from beginning to end. The inner breath is even, neither forgotten nor helped along. At this time the mind is like empty space; yet with breath to rely on, it is not void; with breath to follow, it is not empty. Between not-void and not-empty, stillness upon stillness, clarity upon clarity; the breath continuous and fine, the mind and spirit silent.

§3
Original (Chinese)

至此要一切放下，人我皆忘，此之谓钻杳冥。杳冥中有气，一神独觉，此乃真息也。真息发现，熏心酥痒，还要按入腔子里虚无窍内，积之累之，则命蒂生而阳气自长，乃可以开关运气矣。

At this point one must let everything go and forget both self and others; this is called “boring into the obscure”. Within the obscure there is breath, and a single spirit alone is aware of it: this is the true breath. When the true breath appears, the heart feels suffused with warmth, tingling and itching; even then one must press it back into the opening of emptiness within the body and let it accumulate. Then the stalk of life is born and the yang breath grows of itself, and only then can one open the passes and move the breath.

§4
Original (Chinese)

凝神调息是下手功夫。凝神者，是收已清心而入其内也。心未清时，眼勿内闭，先要自劝自勉，收他回来，清凉恬淡，始行收入气穴，乃曰凝神。坐虚无中，不偏不倚，即是凝神于虚。调息不难，心神一静，随息自然，我只守之顺之，加以神光下照，即是调。调度阴跷之息，与吾心之息相会于气穴中也。神在气中，默注元海，不交而自交，不接而自接，所谓隔体神交也。守其性不散乱，存其神不昏沉，故能杳冥恍惚。

Concentrating the spirit and regulating the breath is the work of getting started. Concentrating the spirit means gathering the mind that has already become clear and bringing it inside. When the mind is not yet clear, do not close the eyes inward; first persuade and encourage yourself and bring it back; when it is cool, calm and tranquil, then begin to gather it into the Cavity of Breath — only this is called concentrating the spirit. Sitting in emptiness, leaning to neither side, is “concentrating the spirit in emptiness”. Regulating the breath is not difficult: once the mind and spirit are still, let the breath be natural; I simply keep to it and go along with it, and add the light of the spirit shining downward — this is “regulating”. One directs the breath of the yinqiao so that it meets the breath of my mind in the Cavity of Breath. The spirit is within the breath, silently fixed on the Original Sea; without uniting, they unite of themselves; without joining, they join of themselves — this is what is called “the spiritual union across the body”. Keeping the nature from scattering and preserving the spirit from torpor, one can therefore enter the obscure and the dim.

§5
Original (Chinese)

心止于脐下，曰凝神。气归于脐下，曰调息。神息相依，守其清净自然，曰勿忘。顺其清净自然，曰勿助。勿忘勿助，以默以柔，息活泼而心自在，即用钻字诀。以虚空为藏心之所，以昏默为息神之乡，三番两次，澄之又澄，忽然心息相忘，神气融合，不觉恍然而阳生矣。

The mind resting below the navel is called concentrating the spirit. The breath returning below the navel is called regulating the breath. Spirit and breath relying on each other: keeping to their clear, pure naturalness is called not forgetting; going along with their clear, pure naturalness is called not helping. Neither forgetting nor helping, silently and softly, the breath lively and the mind at ease — then use the formula of the word “bore”: take empty space as the place where the mind is hidden, take dim silence as the home where the spirit rests; again and again, clarify it and clarify it again, until suddenly mind and breath forget each other, spirit and breath blend, and before one knows it, in a flash, yang is born.

Qiu Ling's noteon §1–§5
Original note (Chinese)

请参阅本书《后天串述文终经》一篇注释。

李涵虚氏另著有《收心法杂谈》，对钻杳冥功夫加以强调及解释。他说：“凡做功夫，钻杳冥是第一桩难事。但先天一气自虚无中来，必有真杳冥，乃有真虚无”。他又说：“大道者，先要清净身心，调理神气。其甚者，要能一切放下，钻入杳冥。必有此等真功夫，然后有真效验”。又说：“人能钻入杳冥，方能得成交媾。我劝人先在虚空中团炼，静之又静，定之又定，无人无我，无无亦无，自然入得杳冥，不交媾而自能交媾，以至阴中生出至阳来”。

Please refer to the notes on the Classic of Ending through a Serial Account of the Posterior Heaven in this book.

Mr Li Hanxu also wrote Miscellaneous Talks on the Method of Gathering the Mind (Shouxin fa zatan 收心法杂谈), which stresses and explains the work of “boring into the obscure”. He says: “In all the work, boring into the obscure is the first difficulty. But the One Breath of Prior Heaven comes out of emptiness, and only with true obscurity is there true emptiness.” He also says: “In the great Way, one must first clear and purify body and mind and regulate spirit and breath; going further, one must be able to let everything go and bore into the obscure. Only with this kind of true work will there be true results.” And again: “Only when a person can bore into the obscure can union be accomplished. I advise people first to practice refining in emptiness, stillness upon stillness, concentration upon concentration, without others, without self, without even ‘without’; then they will naturally be able to enter the obscure, and without uniting they will unite of themselves, until from within yin the utmost yang is born.”

Key Terms

  • “The stalk of life has always been in the true breath”: a line from Cao Wenyi’s Song of the Great Way of the Numinous Source. See True Breath.
  • Quieting the mind, closing the mouth, regulating the breath: the three steps for sitting down.
  • Regulating without regulating: when the mind is quiet the breath is even. See regulating the breath.
  • The yinqiao channel: in front of the anus and behind the scrotum, that is, the perineum.
  • Opening of emptiness: behind the navel and in front of the waist, below the heart and above the kidneys, the area in between — not to be fixed to a precise spot. See Cave of Emptiness.
  • Too tight and it becomes heated, too loose and it becomes cold: too much effort and too much slackness. See fire phasing and torpor and distraction.
  • Neither void nor empty: with breath to rely on, it is not void; with breath to follow, it is not empty.
  • Boring into the obscure: letting everything go, forgetting self and others. See the dim and obscure.
  • A single spirit alone aware: within the obscure there is only a point of awareness — the true breath.
  • Within the body (qiangzi li 腔子里): inside the trunk.
  • Concentrating the spirit in emptiness: sitting in emptiness, leaning to neither side. See concentrating Spirit and entering the Cavity of Breath.
  • Spiritual union across the body: the spirit within the breath; they unite of themselves without uniting.
  • Not forgetting, not helping: keeping to clear, pure naturalness is not forgetting; going along with it is not helping.
  • “Take empty space as the place where the mind is hidden, dim silence as the home where the spirit rests”: the formula of the word “bore”.

Putting It into Practice

  1. Three steps for sitting down: quiet the mind → close the mouth → regulate the breath. You do not need to “regulate” the breath; when the mind is quiet, it regulates itself — “regulating without regulating is best”.
  2. “When the mind is not yet clear, do not close the eyes inward.” When your mind is still in turmoil, do not hurry to close your eyes and turn inward; first talk yourself round, soothe yourself, coax the mind back, and begin only when it is cool and calm. This point is especially useful for people today.
  3. Use “tight” and “loose” to calibrate: if you feel tense or heated, ease off a little and go along with it; if you feel dull or lazy, hold on a little and lift your attention a little.
  4. Neither void nor empty: when you are so still that everything feels empty, know that the breath is still there — with this bit of awareness you will not fall into torpor.
  5. When the true breath appears and body and mind feel soft and pleasant, “one must press it back into the body” — do not cling to it; return to quietly keeping to the center.
  6. Remember the last four sentences: the mind resting below the navel is concentrating the spirit; the breath returning below the navel is regulating the breath; keeping to naturalness is not forgetting; going along with naturalness is not helping.

Translator's Notes

  1. Cao Wenyi: a Daoist woman of the Northern Song (also given as Cao Wenji), author of the Song of the Great Way of the Numinous Source (Lingyuan dadao ge 灵源大道歌); Chen Yingning’s preface (in the appendix of the first book) calls it one of the rare alchemical formulas written by a woman immortal herself.
  2. “If the mind is too tight … this is the civil fire. If the mind is too loose … this is the martial fire”: note how they are paired here — when the mind is overexerted, one adjusts it by “going along” (civil fire); when it is slack, one lifts it by “keeping to” (martial fire). This agrees with the general view that civil fire is light and martial fire strong.
  3. “Anus” translates gudao 谷道; the “Original Sea” is the lower Cinnabar Field.
  4. The original text of this section is two passages; we have divided it into five short sections.
