Introduction to Part One

What this book is

Essentials for Entering Alchemical Practice (Dangong rumen zhiyao 丹功入门指要) was compiled by Qiu Ling in Hong Kong in 1988 and published in 1991. He himself called it a “sister volume” to Selected Ancient Chinese Qigong Texts, Annotated. The first book leans towards the Longmen school and the texts transmitted on Mount Lao; this one is built mainly on the introductory writings of the Western school (Xipai 西派) and the Sanfeng school, with the sayings of alchemists through the ages, Xixinzi’s answers to students, and a short account of the various schools of alchemy added. In his preface Qiu Ling says that he chose texts “confirmed through my own practice”, valuing what is useful and “avoiding empty talk”.

The opening text, the Serial Account of the Posterior Heaven (Houtian chuanshu 后天串述), is by Li Hanxu (style name Changyi shanren, 1806–1856), founder of the Western school of Daoism in the Qing dynasty. What is distinctive about the Western school is that the practice begins by “gathering the mind” (shouxin 收心), then “seeking the breath” at the yinqiao point (the perineum), and only then concentrating the spirit in the “Cave of Emptiness” — quite different from the way the Longmen and Wu-Liu schools begin in the first book. It is worth comparing the two as you read.

The whole text is a single passage, yet it strings together the nine levels of introductory work from beginning to end — gathering the mind, seeking the breath, concentrating the spirit, unfolding the opening, opening the passes, laying the foundation, obtaining the medicine, forming the elixir, and refining the self — which is why it is called a “serial account”.

Qiu Ling's editorial note

此篇选自清代道门西派祖师长乙山人李涵虚（西月）编撰的《太上十三经注解》，乃李氏专为初学丹功者入门之作，言简意赅，程序井然，初学者按此下手行功，当可循正途。李氏著作有《道窍谈》、《三车秘旨》、《十三经注解》、《无根树道情注解》等，并编订有《吕祖全书》、《三丰全集》。本篇由本书编者略加注解。

This text is taken from the Annotations on the Thirteen Scriptures of the Most High (Taishang shisan jing zhujie 太上十三经注解) compiled by Li Hanxu (also called Xiyue), the Changyi hermit, patriarch of the Western school of Daoism in the Qing dynasty. Li wrote it specifically as an entry point for beginners in alchemical practice. The wording is concise and the meaning complete, and the procedure is clear and orderly; a beginner who starts practicing according to it should be able to follow the right path. Li’s works include Talks on the Openings of the Dao (Daoqiao tan 道窍谈), the Secret Principles of the Three Carts (Sanche mizhi 三车秘旨), the Annotations on the Thirteen Scriptures and Annotations on the Daoist Songs of the Rootless Tree (Wugenshu daoqing zhujie 无根树道情注解); he also edited the Complete Works of Patriarch Lü (Lüzu quanshu 吕祖全书) and the Complete Works of Zhang Sanfeng (Sanfeng quanji 三丰全集). The present editor (Qiu Ling) has added brief notes to this text.

What this part is about (in one sentence)

There are nine levels of entry, and none may be skipped: first gather the mind inward and keep to the center and to harmony; find where the breath stirs at the yinqiao and join it with stillness; when spirit and breath meet, the true seed is born of itself; when it surges up to the heart (unfolding the opening), lead it through the three passes up to the Muddy Pellet (opening the passes); then lay the foundation, obtain the medicine and form the elixir; and finally refine the self in daily life, “forgetting feelings in the face of circumstances” — moving from effort to naturalness.

Reading tips

  1. The most useful thing in this part is the passage from Xixinzi quoted in Qiu Ling’s notes: each time you sit, first quiet the mind, then close the mouth, then regulate the breath; “the mind must not be too tight — too tight and it becomes heated … the mind must not be too loose — too loose and it becomes cold”; and within the dim and obscure state there must be a thread of awareness, “and then it is not empty”. Anyone can use these few sentences.
  2. Phrases in the original such as “man and woman join their essences” or “gather that one’s breath and blood to replenish my spirit” are alchemical metaphors. In his preface Qiu Ling states plainly that even where the texts in this book originally speak of “grafting” (dual cultivation between a man and a woman), he explains them all according to the “pure and quiet” method — a single person’s body has its own yin and yang. Our translation follows the same principle and explains it in the Translator’s Notes.
  3. “Turn the spirit into intention, lead it out through the Caudal Funnel and break through the three passes in one rush” describes a forceful technique. We have added a safety note at the head of the chapter: follow such a movement only when the breath really surges up of its own accord; never push the breath with the mind when nothing is there.
