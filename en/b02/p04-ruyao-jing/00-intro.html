Introduction to Part Four

What this book is

The Mirror for Compounding the Medicine (Ruyao jing 入药镜) is one of the most famous short works in the history of inner alchemy; it is traditionally attributed to Cui Xifan. The whole text is written in lines of three characters, with the rhyme changing every four lines, which is why it has been called the “Three-Character Classic”; its language is plain and ancient, and its meaning deep and full. Lines such as “Upper Magpie Bridge, lower Magpie Bridge; Heaven answers with the stars, Earth answers with the tides” and “Water fears drying up, fire fears turning cold; miss by a hair, and no elixir forms” are quoted by almost every later alchemical book.

Qiu Ling says that the general meaning of the whole scripture is as follows: an opening of eight lines that sums everything up, an ending of two lines of exhortation, and three sections in between —

  1. Obtaining the medicine and forming the elixir;
  2. The jade-liquid River Chariot, refining the self and reverting the elixir;
  3. Taking the Great Medicine, forming the embryo and shedding the embryo.

The commentary chosen is the Simple Explanation (Qianjie 浅解) by “the Daoist Fool” (Chi daoren 痴道人) in the Collection on Understanding the Dao through the Union of Schools (Hezong mingdao ji 合宗明道集), compiled by Ran Daoyuan and published during the War of Resistance against Japan. This Daoist Fool had obviously read Li Hanxu closely; his explanation often compares the text with the Secret Principles of the Three Carts and the Nine Levels of Refining the Mind, so reading it after Li Hanxu’s three works makes for a good echo.

Qiu Ling's editorial note

汉代崔希范作《入药镜》，言大丹事理，以三字四句转韵为文，世称三字经，文笔苍古，语意浑含。全经大旨，前头八句，结尾两句，中分三段：首段得药结丹；中段玉液河车，炼己还丹；末段大药服食，结胎、脱胎。

自汉以来，注释此经者颇不乏人。为便于学者入门，特自抗日战争期间出版的冉道源集录《合宗明道集》中，选出痴道人对此经之浅解一种，以飨读者。

Cui Xifan wrote the Mirror for Compounding the Medicine on the matters and principles of the great elixir. It is written in lines of three characters, with the rhyme changing every four lines, and has been called the “Three-Character Classic”; its style is vigorous and ancient, and its meaning deep and implicit. The general meaning of the whole scripture is this: eight lines at the beginning and two lines at the end, with three sections in between. The first section concerns obtaining the medicine and forming the elixir; the middle section, the jade-liquid River Chariot, refining the self and reverting the elixir; the last section, taking the Great Medicine, forming the embryo and shedding the embryo.

Since ancient times there has been no lack of people who annotated this scripture. To make it easier for students to enter, I have specially chosen, from the Collection on Understanding the Dao through the Union of Schools compiled by Ran Daoyuan and published during the War of Resistance against Japan, the Daoist Fool’s simple explanation of this scripture, and offer it to readers.

What this part is about (in one sentence)

When the Prior Heaven and Posterior Heaven breaths unite and one is as if drunk or dazed, that is the Mysterious Pass; with wu and ji (the true intention) as the director and geng and jia (fire phasing) determining advance and withdrawal, one uses the wind of the breath to move the spirit-fire across the upper and lower Magpie Bridges into the Yellow Chamber; the fire must be such that “water fears drying up, fire fears turning cold”, and missing by a hair means failure — and after that, refining the self with the jade liquid, taking the golden liquid, forming and shedding the embryo, everything lies in “only utmost sincerity, following what is natural”.

Reading tips

  1. The original text is short in itself; it is best to read the whole thing aloud a few times first. The three-character lines roll off the tongue; they were meant to be learned by heart.
  2. The commentary in this part is not by Qiu Ling but is the Daoist Fool’s Simple Explanation; Qiu Ling wrote only the editorial note. We give the original and translation of the explanation in full.
  3. The most valuable lines: “Water fears drying up, fire fears turning cold; miss by a hair, and no elixir forms” (the right measure of fire); “only utmost sincerity, following what is natural” (“not the slightest forcing is allowed”); and the explanation’s comment on the “upper Magpie Bridge” — “it is not necessary to take the curled tongue as the fixed bridge”.
