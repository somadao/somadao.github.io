Author’s Preface

Front Matter · Qiu Ling’s Preface

Translation

This book was originally titled Clear Directions on the Methods and Formulas of Nature and Life (Xingming fajue mingzhi 性命法诀明指). It was written by the Old Man of Qianfeng, Zhao Bichen, when he was over seventy, and published around 1933. Zhao Bichen, whose Daoist name was Shunyizi, came from Changping County near Beijing. Of the true and false teachers he met — no fewer than thirty — only five or six knew the true formulas of the dual cultivation of nature and life. In the twenty-first year of the Guangxu reign (1895), at the Jinshan Temple, Zhao took as his teachers the Chan masters Liaoran and Liaokong, tenth-generation patriarchs of the Longmen (Dragon Gate) school founded by the Perfected Qiu Changchun. The ninth-generation patriarch of the Longmen school was Liu Huayang, the eighth Wu Chongxu; Zhao Bichen was the eleventh. Zhao also studied under the Daoist Qiaoqiao, Liu Mingrui, of the Nanwu school, and under Peng Maochang, Liu Yunpu, Tan Zhiming and others. The Daoist Qiaoqiao, Liu Mingrui, wrote the Chapters of the Qiaoqiao Grotto (Qiaoqiao dongzhang), the Song of the Subtleties of the Source of the Way (Daoyuan jingwei ge) and other books. In 1920, at their teachers’ command, Zhao Bichen and his elder brother Zhao Kuiyi founded the Qianfeng branch, which gathered a great many followers.

Zhao received the true transmission of the Wu-Liu school. The Wu-Liu school is the orthodox line of Longmen; it centers on cultivating the breath channels and the work of the Celestial Circuit, draws on Buddhist principles and the practice of Chan absorption, explains things in plain terms and points the way clearly, and has flourished everywhere since the Ming dynasty. This book springs from the same source as The Immortal Lineage of Wu and Liu (Wu-Liu xianzong), written by Wu Chongxu and Liu Huayang; it develops and explains the contents of that book in detail, and adds the formulas for the various methods it did not include. Being a modern work, its wording and sentences are also fairly plain and easy to understand. Zhao says that, at his teachers’ command, he did not hesitate to reveal the secrets of his school’s methods. This book is therefore one of the rare works among the alchemical scriptures and Daoist books of all ages that speak frankly and hold nothing back — a book hard to come by in the world. A student who obtains this volume and checks it against The Immortal Lineage of Wu and Liu will find that each brings out the best in the other.

This book lays out the secret formulas of Daoist alchemical practice in full, and is especially thorough on the formulas of the higher vehicle, sweeping away the old custom that these could only be passed on by word of mouth, face to face. Although its writing is somewhat unpolished and it repeats itself a great deal, alchemical books were never meant to excel in style. I have now cut away the dross, corrected omissions and errors, added punctuation, put the chapters in order and kept the essentials — some fifty thousand characters in all — added brief notes, and changed the title to The Alchemical Formulas of the Old Man of Qianfeng, Annotated, publishing it for the benefit of fellow enthusiasts.

The original book had sixteen steps of method formulas, with two further pieces appended: the Old Man of Qianfeng’s answers to his disciples’ questions, and an explanation of terms. In the book the Old Man of Qianfeng tried to explain ancient Daoist qigong in terms of modern physiology and anatomy. His aim was praiseworthy, but what he said was not always accurate, and the scientific terms he used are mostly outdated and hard to understand; so the editor has also removed these parts.

The value of this book lies in pointing directly and speaking in detail, revealing the secrets of heaven completely. I have read qigong works from Hong Kong, Taiwan and Japan that rate this book very highly, quote it often and also introduce Zhao. Although some qigong masters in Taiwan hold that Zhao’s method lays too much stress on “having form and image”, which they consider unsuited to Daoist alchemy, this too is a matter of differing views and does not dim the book’s light. In reprinting it now, I hope that readers will find their minds and eyes suddenly opened and catch a glimpse beyond the fence — like meeting an enlightened teacher. What joy could be greater! This is my preface.

Qiu Ling, March 10, 1988, while traveling in Hong Kong
